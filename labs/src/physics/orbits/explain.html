An orbit is an ellipse with the Earth's centre at one focus. The satellite moves along it by Kepler's laws while the Earth turns underneath; the plot shows the ground track.

$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{\mu}}, \qquad v = \sqrt{\mu\left(\frac{2}{r} - \frac{1}{a}\right)}$$

Circular orbits

Gravity provides exactly the centripetal force: \(v = \sqrt{\mu/r}\). Lower is faster: the ISS does a lap in 93 minutes at 7.7 km/s.

Higher

Further out, gravity is weaker: slower speed, longer path, a period of several hours (\(T \propto a^{3/2}\)).

Geostationary

At 35 786 km the period equals one sidereal day: above the equator the satellite stays over one point, and its ground track is a dot.

Inclination

The orbit plane is tilted by \(i\) to the equator; the ground track swings between latitudes \(\pm i\). During each orbit the Earth turns east, so each pass crosses the equator further west.

$$\Delta\lambda = -\omega_E T \approx -22.9° \text{ for the ISS}$$

Polar orbit

\(i = 90°\): over both poles. As the Earth turns beneath, the satellite sees the whole planet: mapping and weather.

Retrograde

\(i > 90°\): the satellite moves westward, against the Earth's spin. Launching there costs more fuel.

Elliptical orbits

Fast at perigee, slow at apogee (Kepler's second law: equal areas in equal times). The argument of perigee \(\omega\) turns the ellipse in its plane.

Molniya

\(e = 0.74\), \(i = 63.4°\), apogee over the north: the satellite hangs over Russia for most of its 12-hour orbit. At 63.4° the bulge does not rotate the ellipse.

The Earth's bulge (J2)

The equatorial bulge pulls on the orbit and makes its plane precess. At 98.6° and 800 km it turns about 1° a day, once a year: the orbit keeps the same angle to the Sun (sun-synchronous).

$$\dot\Omega = -\frac32 n J_2 \left(\frac{R_E}{p}\right)^2 \cos i$$

Without the bulge

For a perfect sphere the orbit plane stays fixed in space.

The ISS

\(\cos i > 0\): the plane turns west, about 5° a day.
