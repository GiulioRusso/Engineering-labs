Orbits and ground tracks

An orbit is an ellipse with the Earth's centre at one focus. Six numbers place it in space; the satellite then moves along it by Kepler's laws, while the Earth turns underneath.

The orbital elements

Size and shape: semi-major axis \(a\) and eccentricity \(e\). Orientation: inclination \(i\) (tilt to the equator), right ascension of the ascending node \(\Omega\) (where it crosses the equator going north), argument of perigee \(\omega\) (where in the plane the closest point is).

$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{\mu}}, \qquad v = \sqrt{\mu\left(\frac{2}{r} - \frac{1}{a}\right)}$$

The ground track drifts west

During one orbit the Earth turns eastward by \(\omega_E T\), so each pass crosses the equator further west. A geostationary orbit has \(T\) equal to one sidereal day: its ground track is a single point.

$$\Delta\lambda = -\omega_E T \approx -22.9° \text{ for the ISS}$$

The Earth's bulge turns the orbit (J2)

The equatorial bulge pulls on the orbit and makes its plane precess slowly. A retrograde orbit (\(i > 90°\)) at the right height turns exactly once a year, keeping the same angle to the Sun: sun-synchronous.

$$\dot\Omega = -\frac32 n J_2 \left(\frac{R_E}{p}\right)^2 \cos i, \qquad p = a(1 - e^2)$$
