Simple harmonic motion

The spring pulls back in proportion to the stretch (Hooke's law). The result is a sine wave in time, with a period set only by the mass and the stiffness, not by how far you pull. Energy swaps between the spring and the motion; the total stays fixed.

$$m\ddot x = -k x \;\Rightarrow\; x = x_0\cos\omega_0 t, \qquad \omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}, \quad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}$$

Four times the mass

More inertia, same pull: \(T \propto \sqrt m\), so the period doubles.

Four times stiffer

\(T \propto 1/\sqrt k\): half the period.

A bigger swing

Twice as far means twice the force, so it moves twice as fast: the period is the same (isochronism). The energy goes up four times, \(\tfrac12 k x_0^2\).

The pendulum

Gravity pulls the bob back along its arc with \(mg\sin\theta\). For small angles \(\sin\theta \approx \theta\) and the pendulum is a harmonic oscillator whose period depends only on the length.

$$\ddot\theta = -\frac{g}{L}\sin\theta, \qquad T \approx 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}} \quad (\theta_0 \ll 1)$$

Four times longer

\(T \propto \sqrt L\): twice the period. A 1 m pendulum ticks about once a second each way.

Heavier bob

Weight and inertia both grow with \(m\) and cancel: the period does not depend on the mass (Galileo).

A big swing

At large angles \(\sin\theta < \theta\): the pull is weaker than the linear law, so the pendulum is slower. At 115° the period is about 30 % longer. The exact period uses the arithmetic–geometric mean:

$$T = \frac{2\pi\sqrt{L/g}}{\operatorname{AGM}\!\left(1, \cos\tfrac{\theta_0}{2}\right)}$$

Almost over the top

Released near the top, it lingers there before falling: the period grows without limit as \(\theta_0 \to 180°\). The motion is far from a sine wave.

Damping

Friction proportional to the speed drains energy: the oscillation keeps its rhythm but its amplitude shrinks as \(e^{-\gamma t}\) (dashed envelope).

$$\ddot x + 2\gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2 x = 0, \qquad x \approx x_0 e^{-\gamma t}\cos\omega_1 t, \quad \omega_1 = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2}$$

Light damping

\(\gamma \ll \omega_0\): many swings, each slightly smaller. A guitar string or a swing.

Critical damping

\(\gamma = \omega_0\): the fastest return to rest without overshooting. Car suspensions and door closers are tuned close to this.

Overdamped

\(\gamma > \omega_0\): no oscillation at all, it creeps back slowly, as if moving through honey.

Driven oscillations and resonance

Push periodically at \(\omega_d\). After the start-up dies away, the mass follows the drive, with an amplitude that peaks when the drive matches the natural frequency (right plot).

$$\ddot x + 2\gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2 x = \frac{F_0}{m}\cos\omega_d t, \qquad A = \frac{F_0/m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2)^2 + (2\gamma\omega_d)^2}}$$

At resonance

Each push arrives just when it helps most: the amplitude grows until the damping removes energy as fast as the drive adds it, \(A = F_0/(2m\gamma\omega_0)\).

Far above resonance

The mass cannot keep up: tiny amplitude, moving opposite to the push (180° out of phase).

Less damping

The peak grows taller and narrower: its width is about \(2\gamma\). This is how a radio tunes to one station, and how soldiers marching in step can shake a bridge.

About the model

RK4 at \(\Delta t = 2\) ms over 20 s. Tests: spring period vs \(2\pi\sqrt{m/k}\) (10⁻⁵) and energy (10⁻⁹); pendulum at 115° vs the AGM period (10⁻⁵); damped envelope vs \(e^{-\gamma t}\) (2 %); steady driven amplitude vs the resonance formula at three frequencies (0.2 %).
