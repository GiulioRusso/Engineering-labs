The Hohmann transfer

To climb from one circular orbit to another, burn forward once to stretch the orbit into an ellipse that touches the target, coast half of it, then burn again at the far end to round it off. It is the cheapest two-burn transfer.

$$a_t = \frac{r_1 + r_2}{2}, \quad \Delta v_1 = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r_1}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2r_2}{r_1 + r_2}} - 1\right), \quad t = \pi\sqrt{\frac{a_t^3}{\mu}}$$

A small hop

A modest change in radius costs a few hundred m/s.

To the Moon's distance

Almost all the Δv is the first burn (escaping low orbit); arriving is cheap because the spacecraft is slow up there. The trip takes about 5 days.

A far target

Note the total, then compare with the bi-elliptic case for the same target.

Better engine

Same Δv, but a higher \(I_{sp}\) needs much less propellant: the rocket equation is exponential.

Going further to arrive cheaper

Overshoot to a distant apoapsis \(r_b\), where the spacecraft is slow and changing the orbit is cheap, then fall back to the target. It saves fuel only when the target is more than about 12 times further out, and it takes much longer.

Not worth it for GEO

\(r_2/r_1 \approx 6\): three burns cost more than Hohmann's two.

The rocket equation

Each burn costs propellant exponentially: the rocket must also accelerate the fuel it has not burned yet.

$$\Delta v = I_{sp}\, g_0 \ln\frac{m_0}{m_f}, \qquad m_\text{prop} = m_\text{dry}\left(e^{\Delta v / I_{sp} g_0} - 1\right)$$

Bi-elliptic

Overshoot to \(r_b\), far beyond the target, where the spacecraft is slow and changing the orbit is cheap; then fall back. For \(r_2/r_1 = 15\) the three burns cost less than Hohmann's two, at the price of a much longer trip.
