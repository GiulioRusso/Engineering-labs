Adding forces

What you are looking at

Three forces on a point (drag their tips), drawn tip to tail to build the resultant, which sets the acceleration.

Forces are vectors: direction matters as much as size. The total force is found by placing them tip to tail; the arrow from the first tail to the last tip is the resultant. If they close into a loop, the resultant is zero: equilibrium.

$$\mathbf F = \sum_i \mathbf F_i = \Big(\sum F_{ix},\ \sum F_{iy}\Big), \qquad \mathbf a = \frac{\mathbf F}{m}$$

Worked example

12 N east and 9 N north: resultant √(144 + 81) = 15 N at 37° north of east. On 2 kg: a = 7.5 m/s².

Common mistake

Adding magnitudes: 12 N + 9 N is not 21 N unless they point the same way.

Along one line

10 N to the right and 4 N to the left: forces on one line just add with signs, net 6 N to the right.

At right angles

12 N and 9 N at 90° give \(\sqrt{12^2 + 9^2} = 15\) N, not 21 N: perpendicular forces add like the sides of a right triangle.

Equilibrium

Three 12 N forces 120° apart close into a triangle: the net force is zero, so the puck does not accelerate (it can still move at constant velocity).

Torque

What you are looking at

A bar on a pivot, a force applied at distance r and angle α; the lever arm (perpendicular distance) is drawn.

What makes things turn is not the force alone but where and how you push. A door opens easily pushed at the handle, perpendicular; pushed near the hinge or along the door, it barely moves.

$$\tau = rF\sin\alpha = F \cdot d_\perp, \qquad \tau = I\alpha_{\text{ang}}$$

Worked example

10 N at 0.6 m, perpendicular: 6 N·m. At 30°: 3 N·m. Along the bar: 0.

Common mistake

Using the distance to the point where the force is applied instead of the perpendicular lever arm.

At the end

Largest \(r\), force at 90°: the most torque. That is why door handles are far from the hinges.

Near the pivot

Same force, a tenth of the lever arm: a tenth of the torque.

Along the bar

\(\alpha = 0\): the force pulls straight through the pivot and has no lever arm. Zero torque however strong it is.

At 30°

\(\sin 30° = \tfrac12\): only half the force is at right angles to the bar.

Buoyancy

What you are looking at

A block floating (or sinking) in a fluid; the submerged part is shaded and the forces drawn.

The fluid pushes harder on the bottom of the block than on the top, because pressure grows with depth. The net upward push equals the weight of fluid displaced (Archimedes). A floating body sinks until it displaces its own weight.

$$F_b = \rho_f g V_\text{sub}, \qquad \text{floating: } \frac{V_\text{sub}}{V} = \frac{\rho_b}{\rho_f}$$

Worked example

Ice (917 kg/m³) in fresh water: 91.7 % under; in sea water (1025): 89.5 % — the tip of the iceberg is about 10 %.

Common mistake

Thinking heavy objects sink: density decides — a steel ship floats because its average density, with the air inside, is below water's.

Ice

\(917/1000\): 92 % of an iceberg is under water.

Sea water

Denser water (1025 kg/m³) lifts more: 89 % under.

Same density

Fully submerged, buoyancy exactly balances weight: it floats at any depth (neutral buoyancy, like a fish or a submarine).

Denser than water

Even fully under, the displaced water weighs less than the block: it sinks.
