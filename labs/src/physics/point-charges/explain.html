Electric fields of point charges

What you are looking at

Point charges (draggable), electric field lines, a colour slice of the potential with equipotential contours, and optionally a Gauss surface with the flux through it.

Each charge pushes a positive test charge away (if positive) or pulls it in (if negative), with a force that weakens as 1/r². Field lines start on positive charges and end on negative ones; where they crowd, the field is strong. Equipotentials cross the field lines at right angles — moving along them costs no work.

$$\mathbf E = \sum_i \frac{q_i}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r_i^2}\hat{\mathbf r}_i, \qquad V = \sum_i \frac{q_i}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r_i}, \qquad \oint\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf A = \frac{Q_\text{inside}}{\varepsilon_0}$$
  1. Fields of several charges add as vectors (superposition); potentials add as numbers — easier.
  2. Number of lines ∝ charge.
  3. Gauss's law: the total flux out of a closed surface depends only on the charge inside.

Worked example

2 nC at 10 cm: E = 9·10⁹·2·10⁻⁹/0.01 = 1800 V/m, V = 180 V. Two equal like charges: the field is zero exactly midway.

Common mistakes

Thinking a field line is the path a charge follows (only if it starts at rest and moves slowly). Adding field magnitudes instead of vectors.

Try it

Enable the Gauss surface and drag a charge in and out: the flux jumps only when the charge crosses the surface.

One charge

Radial lines, spherical equipotentials. The field at distance \(r\) is \(kq/r^2\), the potential \(kq/r\).

Negative

Same pattern, arrows reversed: the lines come in from far away and end on the charge.

More charge

2.5 times the charge, 2.5 times as many lines and a field 2.5 times stronger everywhere.

A pair of opposite charges

Every line from the + charge lands on the − charge. Between them the field is strong and points from + to −.

Like charges

The lines push away from each other. Midway the two fields cancel: a point of zero field, a saddle of the potential.

Unequal charges

The −1 nC charge can absorb only a third of the lines from the +3 nC one; the rest escape to infinity. From far away the pair looks like a single +2 nC charge.

A dipole

Seen from a distance much larger than the separation, the fields nearly cancel: a dipole field, fading as \(1/r^3\).

A quadrupole

Alternating charges on a square: total charge 0 and no net dipole either. Far away the field fades even faster, as \(1/r^4\).

All positive

+8 nC in total: from far away, the field of a single charge at the centre, where the four fields cancel.

Gauss's law

Count the field flowing out of a closed surface: it depends only on the charge inside. A charge outside sends in as much as it lets out.

$$\oint \cE{\mathbf E}\cdot d\mathbf A = \frac{Q_\text{inside}}{\varepsilon_0}$$

Both inside

The flux counts \(+2 - 1 = +1\) nC, however the charges are arranged inside the sphere.

One charge

Remove the outside charge: the field lines change, the flux does not.

Opposite charges

Every line from the + charge lands on the − charge. Between them the field is strong and points from + to −.

A quadrupole

Alternating charges on a square: no net charge and no net dipole. Far away the field fades as \(1/r^4\).

Gauss's law

The flux out of the sphere counts only the charge inside (+2 nC). The −1 nC charge outside bends the lines but sends in as much flux as it takes out. Drag it in and watch the flux jump.

$$\oint \cE{\mathbf E}\cdot d\mathbf A = \frac{Q_\text{inside}}{\varepsilon_0}$$
