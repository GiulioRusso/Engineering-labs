Projectile motion

What you are looking at

A ball thrown at speed v₀ and angle θ, its trail, and its velocity split into a horizontal (constant) and a vertical (changing) part. The readout gives range, peak height and flight time.

The trick is to split the motion in two independent problems. Horizontally nothing pushes the ball: it keeps its speed. Vertically gravity takes away 9.81 m/s every second: up, stop, down. Put the two together and you get a parabola.

$$x = v_0\cos\theta\,t, \qquad y = h_0 + v_0\sin\theta\,t - \tfrac12 g t^2, \qquad R = \frac{v_0^2\sin 2\theta}{g}\;(h_0 = 0)$$
  1. Time to the top: vertical speed reaches zero at t = v₀ sin θ / g.
  2. Flight time (level ground): twice that.
  3. Range: horizontal speed × flight time.

Worked example

v₀ = 15 m/s at 45°: vₓ = v_y = 10.6 m/s. Top after 1.08 s at 5.73 m; lands after 2.16 s, 22.9 m away (= 15²/9.81).

Common mistakes

Thinking the speed is zero at the top: only the vertical part is. Thinking the horizontal motion slows down: without air, nothing pushes it.

Try it

Compare 30° and 60°: same range, different heights — sin 60° = sin 120°.

45°

On flat ground the range \(v_0^2\sin 2\theta/g\) is largest when \(\sin 2\theta = 1\): \(\theta = 45°\).

30°

A flatter, faster flight. Note the range, then try 60°.

60°: the same range

\(\sin(2\cdot 60°) = \sin(2\cdot 30°)\): complementary angles land at the same spot. The high shot just takes longer.

Straight up

No horizontal motion. It rises to \(v_0^2/2g\), stops for an instant (\(v = 0\), but \(a = g\) still) and falls back.

From a cliff

Thrown level, it falls exactly like a dropped stone: the time to land, \(\sqrt{2h_0/g}\), does not depend on the horizontal speed.

Air drag

Drag pushes against the velocity and grows like \(v^2\). The path is no longer a parabola, it lands short and steep, and the total energy (dashed) goes down: it heats the air.

Circular motion

What you are looking at

A body going round a circle at constant speed, with its velocity (tangent) and acceleration (toward the centre).

Moving at constant speed on a circle is still accelerating: the direction keeps changing. The acceleration points to the centre (centripetal), and something must provide the force — a string, friction, gravity.

$$v = \omega r, \qquad a = \frac{v^2}{r} = \omega^2 r, \qquad F = m\omega^2 r, \qquad T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega}$$

Worked example

r = 2 m, ω = 1.5 rad/s: v = 3 m/s, a = 4.5 m/s². A car taking a 50 m bend at 20 m/s needs a = 8 m/s² — almost all the grip a tyre has.

Common mistake

"Centrifugal force pushes outward": in the ground frame there is only the inward force; outward is what you feel in the rotating frame.

Twice as fast

Doubling \(\omega\) doubles the speed and quadruples the acceleration: \(a = \omega^2 r\).

A bigger circle

Same turning rate on twice the radius: twice the speed, twice the acceleration. The period stays the same.

Collisions

What you are looking at

Two carts colliding on a track; bars show momentum and kinetic energy before and after.

During the brief collision the carts push each other with equal and opposite forces (Newton's third law), so total momentum is always the same before and after. Kinetic energy is conserved only if the collision is perfectly elastic; the coefficient of restitution e says how much bounce is left.

$$m_1u_1 + m_2u_2 = m_1v_1 + m_2v_2, \qquad v_2 - v_1 = -e\,(u_2 - u_1)$$
  1. e = 1: elastic — energy kept, they bounce apart.
  2. e = 0: perfectly inelastic — they stick and move together; maximum energy lost (to heat, sound, deformation).

Worked example

Equal masses, one at 2 m/s hits one at rest, e = 1: they swap speeds (Newton's cradle). If they stick: both move at 1 m/s, half the kinetic energy is lost.

Common mistake

Conserving kinetic energy in a crash: only momentum is guaranteed.

Heavy hits light

The heavy cart barely slows down; the light one shoots off at almost twice the incoming speed.

Light hits heavy

The light cart bounces back, the heavy one moves off slowly. Like a ball against a wall, when \(m_2 \gg m_1\).

Head-on

Total momentum is zero before, so it is zero after: equal masses simply swap velocities and fly apart.

Partly elastic

They separate at half the approach speed. Momentum is kept; 75 % of the kinetic energy in the centre-of-mass frame is lost (\(1 - e^2\)).

Perfectly inelastic

They move on together at \(\frac{m_1 u_1 + m_2 u_2}{m_1 + m_2}\). The largest possible energy loss, still with momentum kept.
