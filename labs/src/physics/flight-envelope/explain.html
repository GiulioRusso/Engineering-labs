Lift, drag and the flight envelope

What you are looking at

An aircraft with airflow around the wing, the four forces (lift, weight, thrust, drag), the angle of attack, and plots of the lift curve and the speed range where flight is possible.

A wing turns air downward; the air pushes the wing up. More speed or a steeper angle of attack α gives more lift — until about 15°, where the flow separates from the upper surface and lift collapses: the stall. In level flight lift must equal weight, so slow flight needs a big α. In a banked turn lift tilts, so it must grow by the load factor n = 1/cos(bank).

$$L = \tfrac12\rho V^2 S\,C_L(\alpha), \qquad D = \tfrac12\rho V^2 S\,C_D, \qquad C_D = C_{D0} + kC_L^2, \qquad n = \frac{1}{\cos\phi}, \qquad V_\text{stall} = \sqrt{\frac{2nW}{\rho S C_{L,\max}}}$$
  1. Half the speed needs four times the lift coefficient.
  2. Thin air (altitude) means a higher true speed for the same lift.
  3. Turning raises the stall speed by √n: at 60° bank, ×1.41.

Worked example

A light aircraft stalls at 25 m/s level; in a 60° turn (n = 2) it stalls at 35 m/s. At 4000 m (ρ = 0.82 vs 1.11 at 1000 m) every speed is about 16 % higher.

Common mistake

Thinking a stall means the engine stopped: it is the wing, and it happens at an angle, at any speed.

Level flight

Lift equals weight, thrust equals drag. At each speed there is exactly one angle of attack that holds the aircraft up.

$$\tfrac12\rho V^2 S\,C_L = W, \qquad C_D = C_{D0} + k\,C_L^2$$

Slow flight

Half the speed needs four times \(C_L\): the nose comes up, close to the stall. Induced drag (\(\propto C_L^2\)) dominates.

Fast flight

A small \(C_L\) is enough; now friction drag \(C_{D0}\) dominates. The best glide (largest \(L/D\)) is in between, where the two drags are equal.

Thinner air

At 4000 m the air is about 33 % less dense: the same lift needs more speed or more angle of attack, and the stall speed rises.

Turns load the wing

Banked at \(\varphi\), the lift must also pull the aircraft round: it grows by the load factor \(n\). The stall speed grows with \(\sqrt n\).

$$n = \frac{1}{\cos\varphi}, \qquad V_s = \sqrt{\frac{2 n W}{\rho S\, C_{L\max}}}$$

Gentle turn

\(1/\cos 30° = 1.15\): only 15 % more lift.

Too slow

At 60° bank \(n = 2\), so the stall speed is \(\sqrt2 \approx 1.41\) times the level value: this speed is below it. Turning steeply at low speed is how many stalls happen.

The stall

Past the critical angle (about 15°) the flow breaks away from the upper surface: \(C_L\) collapses and the drag jumps.

Just below

Near the top of the lift curve: the most lift the wing can give at this speed.

Negative angle

Below the zero-lift angle \(\alpha_0\) the wing pushes down.

A 60° bank

\(n = 1/\cos 60° = 2\): the wing must carry twice the weight, so the stall speed rises by \(\sqrt 2\).

The stall

Past the critical angle (about 15°) the flow breaks away from the upper surface: \(C_L\) collapses and the drag jumps. Lift is now far below the weight.
