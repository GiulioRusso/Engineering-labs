Lift grows with angle of attack, until it stalls

Tilt the wing into the air and it deflects the flow downward: the reaction is lift. It grows with the square of the speed and almost linearly with the angle of attack \(\alpha\), until the flow separates from the upper surface.

$$L = \tfrac12\rho V^2 S\,C_L, \qquad C_L \approx a(\alpha - \alpha_0)$$

Level flight

Lift equals weight, thrust equals drag. At each speed there is exactly one angle of attack that holds the aircraft up.

$$\tfrac12\rho V^2 S\,C_L = W, \qquad C_D = C_{D0} + k\,C_L^2$$

Slow flight

Half the speed needs four times \(C_L\): the nose comes up, close to the stall. Induced drag (\(\propto C_L^2\)) dominates.

Fast flight

A small \(C_L\) is enough; now friction drag \(C_{D0}\) dominates. The best glide (largest \(L/D\)) is in between, where the two drags are equal.

Thinner air

At 4000 m the air is about 33 % less dense: the same lift needs more speed or more angle of attack, and the stall speed rises.

Turns load the wing

Banked at \(\varphi\), the lift must also pull the aircraft round: it grows by the load factor \(n\). The stall speed grows with \(\sqrt n\).

$$n = \frac{1}{\cos\varphi}, \qquad V_s = \sqrt{\frac{2 n W}{\rho S\, C_{L\max}}}$$

Gentle turn

\(1/\cos 30° = 1.15\): only 15 % more lift.

Too slow

At 60° bank \(n = 2\), so the stall speed is \(\sqrt2 \approx 1.41\) times the level value: this speed is below it. Turning steeply at low speed is how many stalls happen.

The stall

Past the critical angle (about 15°) the flow breaks away from the upper surface: \(C_L\) collapses and the drag jumps.

Just below

Near the top of the lift curve: the most lift the wing can give at this speed.

Negative angle

Below the zero-lift angle \(\alpha_0\) the wing pushes down.
