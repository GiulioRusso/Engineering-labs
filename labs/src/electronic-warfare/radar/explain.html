Pulse radar

The radar sends a short pulse every PRI \(= 1/\text{PRF}\) and listens. An echo that comes back after \(\Delta t\) is at range \(c\Delta t/2\). Echoes from beyond \(R_u\) arrive after the next pulse has left and are read at the wrong range. The matched filter (bottom) turns each echo into a peak; two targets are separated if they are further apart than the resolution.

$$R = \frac{c\,\Delta t}{2}, \qquad R_u = \frac{c}{2\,\text{PRF}}, \qquad \Delta R = \frac{c\tau}{2} \;\text{ or }\; \frac{c}{2B} \text{ (chirp)}$$

A shorter pulse

0.5 µs gives 75 m resolution: targets 120 m apart become two peaks. But a shorter pulse carries less energy.

Pulse compression

A 10 µs pulse has plenty of energy but on its own would only resolve 1.5 km. Sweeping its frequency over 5 MHz (a chirp) lets the matched filter compress it to the resolution of a 0.2 µs pulse: 30 m.

Second-time-around echo

At 1 kHz, \(R_u = 150\) km. A target at 200 km answers the previous pulse and shows up at 50 km.

Lower PRF

At 500 Hz, \(R_u = 300\) km: the target is shown at its true range. The price: fewer pulses per second, so less energy and worse Doppler.

The range–Doppler map

Over a burst of \(N\) pulses a moving target's echo shifts in phase from pulse to pulse at the Doppler frequency \(f_d = 2v/\lambda\). An FFT across the pulses sorts the echoes by velocity, the delay sorts them by range. Ground clutter sits at zero velocity. Both axes are sampled, so both can fold.

$$f_d = \frac{2v}{\lambda}, \qquad v_u = \pm\frac{\lambda\,\text{PRF}}{4}, \qquad \Delta v = \frac{\lambda\,\text{PRF}}{2N}$$

Velocity folding

At 10 kHz and 3 cm the unambiguous velocity is ±75 m/s: a target at +100 m/s appears at −50 m/s.

Range folding

The same high PRF gives only 15 km of unambiguous range: a target at 20 km appears at 5 km. Radars switch between PRFs to resolve both.

Longer bursts

128 pulses instead of 32: the velocity bins are four times finer, the peaks narrower.

Moving-target indication

Rejecting the zero-velocity line removes the ground clutter and keeps only what moves.

Detection in noise

The echo competes with receiver noise. The radar equation gives the signal-to-noise ratio; a threshold decides "target". A high threshold rarely fires on noise (low \(P_{fa}\)) but needs more SNR to detect (\(P_d\)).

$$\text{SNR} = \frac{P_t G^2 \lambda^2 \sigma\, n}{(4\pi)^3 R^4\, k T_0 B F}, \qquad P_{fa} = e^{-T^2/2}$$

Twice as far

The echo has to go out and come back: power falls as \(1/R^4\), 12 dB per doubling.

Integration

Adding 16 pulses coherently multiplies the SNR by 16 (+12 dB): exactly what was lost by doubling the range.

A lower threshold

\(P_{fa} = 10^{-2}\): more detections, but one range cell in a hundred lights up on noise alone.

A small target

An RCS of 0.01 m² (20 dB below 1 m²) shrinks the detection range by \(10^{20/40} \approx 3.2\) times.

The PPI scope

A plan-position indicator draws echoes at their range and bearing as the antenna turns; the sweep line marks where it points now. Each echo glows and fades until the next pass. Clutter close to the radar comes from the ground.

A fast jet

Between two sweeps the jet moves several kilometres: the tracker must predict where to look for it next.

Rain

Raindrops reflect too: a large patch of weak echoes that can hide targets. Doppler processing and circular polarization help remove it.

Slow scan

At 4 rpm each target is seen every 15 s: long range and dwell time, but slow updates.

Jamming and burn-through

A jammer floods the radar's receiver with noise. Its power falls only as \(1/R^2\) (one way), the echo as \(1/R^4\) (two ways): at long range the jammer wins, but as the target gets closer the echo grows faster. The burn-through range is where the signal-to-jamming ratio reaches what the radar needs.

$$\frac{S}{J} = \frac{P_t G \sigma}{4\pi P_j G_j R^2}\,\frac{B_j}{B_r}, \qquad R_{bt} = \sqrt{\frac{P_t G \sigma B_j}{4\pi P_j G_j B_r\,(S/J)_\text{req}}}$$

A stronger jammer

Ten times the jammer power (10 W) pulls the burn-through range in by \(\sqrt{10} \approx 3.2\) times.

Spot jamming

Concentrating the noise in the radar's own 1 MHz instead of 10 MHz puts ten times more of it where it matters. Radars answer by hopping frequency.

Stand-off jamming

A jammer far away, outside the radar's beam, must come in through the sidelobes (here −30 dB). Its power at the radar is constant while the target's echo grows as it approaches.
