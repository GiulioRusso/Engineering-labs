Phased-array beamforming

What you are looking at

Left: the actual field in front of the array at this instant (blue negative, orange positive). Each element at the bottom radiates circular waves; where they arrive in step they add, elsewhere they cancel. Right: the same array seen from far away — the radiation pattern in dB.

The beam is not "emitted" in a direction: it is what survives the cancellation. Give each element a slightly later phase than its neighbour and the line of constant phase tilts, so the direction where everything adds up tilts with it — the beam steers with no moving parts.

$$\beta_n = -k\,d\,n\sin\theta_0, \qquad AF(\theta) = \Big|\sum_n a_n\, e^{\,jkdn(\sin\theta - \sin\theta_0)}\Big|, \qquad \theta_{3\text{dB}} \approx \frac{0.886\,\lambda}{N d \cos\theta_0}$$

Step by step

  1. Pick a direction θ₀. The path to a far point in that direction is \(d\sin\theta_0\) shorter for each next element.
  2. Delay each element's phase by exactly that path: \(kd\sin\theta_0\) per element.
  3. Now all contributions arrive in phase along θ₀: the main beam.
  4. In other directions they arrive with a spread of phases and largely cancel: the nulls and sidelobes.

Worked example

16 elements, \(d = \lambda/2\): aperture 8λ, beam width \(0.886/8 = 0.11\) rad = 6.3°. Uniform weights give −13.2 dB sidelobes; Hamming weights give about −40 dB but a beam about 1.45 times wider (9.2°).

Common mistake: spacing

Elements further apart than \(\lambda/(1 + |\sin\theta_0|)\) let a second direction also add in phase: a grating lobe as strong as the main beam, pointing somewhere you did not ask for. Keep \(d \le \lambda/2\) to be safe at all steering angles.

Try it

Steer to 30° and watch the wavefronts tilt in the near field and the beam move in the polar plot. Then set the spacing to 1 λ: a second beam appears.

Steering

A phase step of −90° per element (\(d = \lambda/2\), \(\sin 30° = 0.5\)): the beam leaves at 30°, slightly wider (by \(1/\cos 30°\)).

Taper

Weighting the edge elements less smooths the aperture's "edges": sidelobes drop by almost 30 dB, the beam gets wider. The same trade as windowing a spectrum.

Grating lobe

\(d = \lambda\), steered to 20°: another direction (−42°) has whole-wavelength path differences too, and gets a full-strength beam.

Four elements

Aperture 2λ: a 25° beam and broad sidelobes. Aperture is what buys a narrow beam.

Maxwell's equations on a grid (FDTD)

What you are looking at

Left: \(E_z\) everywhere on the grid, computed step by step from Maxwell's equations — no formula for the wave is used. Top right: \(|H|\). Bottom right: \(E_z\) and \(H_y\) along the dotted line.

The two curl equations say: a changing \(E\) creates a circulating \(H\), and a changing \(H\) creates a circulating \(E\). On a grid that becomes a leapfrog: update \(H\) from the differences of \(E\) around it, then \(E\) from the differences of \(H\), and repeat (the Yee scheme).

$$\mu\frac{\partial \mathbf H}{\partial t} = -\nabla\times\mathbf E, \qquad \varepsilon\frac{\partial \mathbf E}{\partial t} = \nabla\times\mathbf H, \qquad S = \frac{c\,\Delta t}{\Delta x} \le \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}$$

Why the stability limit

In one time step a wave may not travel further than the grid can "pass the message" between neighbours. In 2-D that is \(S \le 1/\sqrt2 \approx 0.707\). Above it, errors grow exponentially and the field reaches astronomical values within a few hundred steps.

Common mistake

Making the time step bigger to "go faster". The grid cell sets the largest allowed step; to go faster you need fewer cells (lower resolution) or more computing power.

Try it

Put a wall in: the reflected wave and the incoming one form a standing wave — watch \(E\) and \(H\) on the cut: their peaks alternate in space.

A wall

A perfect conductor forces \(E_z = 0\) on its surface: total reflection, and a standing wave in front with \(E\) nodes where \(H\) has antinodes.

A slit

The opening acts as a new source: waves spread out behind it (diffraction), more widely the narrower the slit.

Two slits

Two new sources interfere: bright and dark directions behind the wall — the same physics as the double-slit experiment and as a two-element array.

Near the limit

S = 0.7: still stable, and the waves move 40 % faster per step than at 0.5. At 0.75 it would blow up.

Pointing a beam at a receiver

What you are looking at

Left: the beam (its gain against direction) and an aircraft crossing it; green when the link closes. Right top: the received power along the track against the sensitivity (red). Right bottom: how much gain is lost off boresight.

Gain is not free power, it is borrowed solid angle: concentrating the energy into a cone of \(\theta \times \theta\) multiplies it by about \(4\pi/\theta^2\).

$$P_r = P_t\, G_t(\psi)\, G_r \left(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi R}\right)^2, \qquad G_0 \approx \frac{4\pi}{\theta^2}, \qquad G(\psi) \approx G_0\, e^{-2.773(\psi/\theta)^2}$$

Worked example

A 4° beam: \(G_0 = 4\pi/(0.07)^2 \approx 2600\) = 34 dBi. Halving it to 2° gains 6 dB — and covers a quarter of the sky. Being 4° off boresight with a 4° beam costs \(2.773 \times 4.34 = 12\) dB: no extra transmit power is as cheap as pointing correctly.

Try it

Narrow the beam: the peak rises, the window where the link closes shrinks. A high-gain link must be kept aimed.

Narrow beam

2°: +6 dB at the peak, but the aircraft is inside the beam for half as long.

Aimed off

4° pointing error with a 4° beam: −12 dB at the moment the aircraft should have been on boresight.

Twice as far

One-way link: −6 dB per doubling of range (for a radar echo it would be −12 dB).

Lobing: two beams for one angle

What you are looking at

Left: two beams A and B squinted either side of the antenna axis (dashed), and the target (orange). Right top: A, B and the error signal Δ/Σ against target angle. Right bottom: the target weaving and where the antenna points as the loop follows it.

One beam cannot tell where the target is inside it: at the peak its slope is zero, so a small offset barely changes the power. Two squinted beams overlap in the middle; a target slightly to one side gives a stronger echo in that beam. The ratio does not depend on range, size or aspect — only on angle:

$$\frac{\Delta}{\Sigma} = \frac{B - A}{B + A} \quad\begin{cases} = 0 & \text{on axis} \\ > 0 & \text{towards B} \\ < 0 & \text{towards A} \end{cases}$$

A control loop turns the antenna by (gain × error) each step, driving the error to zero: the antenna follows the target.

Common mistake

Squinting too little: the error curve gets shallow and noise dominates. Too much: the overlap dip in the middle loses sensitivity.

Try it

Lower the loop gain and watch the antenna lag behind the weaving target; raise it and it follows closely.

Small squint

0.5° squint on a 4° beam: the Δ/Σ slope is gentle; small errors are hard to see.

Agile target

A 5° weave: the loop must turn the antenna quickly; with a modest gain it stays a little behind at the turns.

Low loop gain

The antenna lags far behind; with a weave faster than the loop, the target can leave the beam. Sequential lobing (switching A and B in time) is also what an inverse-gain jammer exploits; monopulse forms both at once from one pulse.

Searching a volume with a pencil beam

What you are looking at

A vertical cut through the sky: the radar at the origin, the elevation bars (wedges) it paints one after the other, the current one highlighted. Aircraft turn green when the beam is on them. Right: when each aircraft was hit.

A narrow beam sees only a small cone at a time. To search, the antenna scans in azimuth while stepping (nodding) in elevation, painting one bar per scan. The frame time is the number of beam positions times the time spent in each.

$$N_\text{beams} \approx \frac{\Omega}{\theta_{az}\,\theta_{el}}, \qquad T_\text{frame} = N_\text{beams}\,\frac{n_p}{\text{PRF}}, \qquad h_\text{bar}(R) \approx R\,\theta_{el}$$

Worked example

A 4° bar is 349 m tall at 5 km but 3.5 km tall at 50 km. Coverage is dense close in and full of holes far out, between and above the bars.

Common mistake

Assuming every aircraft in range is seen. An aircraft flying in a gap, or above the top bar, is simply not detected however large its echo.

Try it

Narrow the bars: gaps open far out. Add bars to fill them: the frame time grows and every aircraft is revisited less often.

Gaps

Four 2° bars cover only up to 8.5° elevation: the high aircraft close in is above the top bar.

More bars

Eight bars cover the sky but the frame takes twice as long: each aircraft is hit half as often.
