An airborne engagement

What you are looking at

Left: plan view. Our aircraft (green) flies north with its radar beam sweeping left and right. Three targets fly their own courses. Right top: every detection plotted as range against closing speed. Right bottom: what a radar-warning receiver (RWR) on target 1 hears.

A target is seen only when the beam is on it. What comes back carries its radial velocity — the part of the relative velocity along the line of sight:

$$v_r = -(\mathbf v_\text{tgt} - \mathbf v_\text{own})\cdot\hat{\mathbf r}, \qquad f_d = \frac{2 v_r}{\lambda} \quad (66.7\ \text{Hz per m/s at X-band})$$

Worked example

Target 1 flies straight at us at 280 m/s while we fly at 250 m/s towards it: closing speed ≈ 530 m/s → 35 kHz. Target 2 crosses at 250 m/s almost perpendicular to the line of sight: its closing speed is small, whatever its true speed. It is geometry that is measured, not speed.

The engagement runs both ways

Every time our beam sweeps across target 1, its RWR registers a pulse burst. The spacing of the blips gives our scan period, their width our beam width. Looking is itself a transmission: a scanning radar broadcasts its own parameters to anyone listening.

Common mistake

Reading the Doppler as the target's speed. A fast crossing target can sit at nearly zero Doppler — right where ground clutter lives — which is exactly how aircraft try to hide from pulse-Doppler radars ("beaming").

Try it

Stop our aircraft: the closing speeds change for every target. Speed up the scan: the RWR blips come more often but each is shorter.

Own ship at rest

Only the targets' own motion counts now: target 1's closing speed drops from about 530 to 280 m/s.

Fast scan

Each target is visited more often, but the beam dwells on it for less time: fewer pulses per look, worse Doppler resolution.

Narrow beam

RWR blips become short spikes: the beam width can be read from their duration (scan rate × time = beam width).

Ground clutter seen from the air

What you are looking at

Left: the ground below, coloured by the Doppler it returns (orange ahead, blue behind, dark abeam), with iso-range circles and iso-Doppler hyperbolas. The green outline is the patch lit by the beam. Right top: the clutter Doppler spectrum — the footprint sliced by those hyperbolas. Right bottom: how the footprint grows as the beam looks closer to the horizon.

A ground point ahead of the aircraft is approached at almost full speed; one abeam is not approached at all. From altitude h:

$$f_c = \frac{2v}{\lambda}\,\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + h^2}}$$

Setting it constant gives a cone around the flight line; its intersection with flat ground is a hyperbola. Constant range gives circles. Every ground cell has one range and one Doppler: the clutter spectrum is just the footprint counted along the hyperbolas.

Worked example

A 4° beam from 8 km: at 60° depression it covers 0.75 km of ground; at 10° it covers 19 km — 25 times more clutter in the same range cell. Directly below, the altitude line returns strongly at zero Doppler and minimum range.

Common mistake

Thinking clutter is noise. It is a structured signal, at known Doppler for each direction — which is exactly what lets pulse-Doppler radars filter it out, except for targets whose Doppler falls in the same band.

Try it

Lower the depression angle towards the horizon and watch the footprint and the spectrum widen. Turn the beam sideways: the clutter collapses towards zero Doppler.

Looking down

60°: a small patch, a narrow clutter line, little clutter power.

Near the horizon

8°: the beam's lower edge reaches tens of kilometres; the footprint floods a long strip of ground into the radar.

Looking sideways

80° off the nose: the footprint lies along the zero-Doppler hyperbolas. A target crossing at 90° lands right on top of it.
