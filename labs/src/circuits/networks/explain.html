Series and parallel: sharing a current or sharing a voltage

What you are looking at

Left: two resistors, one after the other (series) or side by side (parallel). The dots are charges; their speed is the current in each wire. Top right: the voltage across each resistor. Bottom right: the current in each.

Two parts can be connected so that the same current must pass through both (series), or so that they sit across the same two nodes and share the same voltage (parallel). Which one decides everything else.

$$R_\text{series}=R_1+R_2,\qquad R_\text{parallel}=\frac{R_1R_2}{R_1+R_2},\qquad V_2=V\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}\ (\text{divider})$$
  1. Series: one path, so one current I = V/(R1 + R2); the voltage splits in proportion to each R.
  2. Parallel: each branch sees the full V and takes V/R; the currents add.
  3. Parallel is always less than the smallest resistor: you added another path.

Worked example

10 V, 100 Ω and 200 Ω. Series: 300 Ω, 33 mA, 3.3 V + 6.7 V. Parallel: 66.7 Ω, 100 mA + 50 mA = 150 mA.

Common mistake

Calling two resistors "in series" because they are drawn in a line. Check: is there any junction between them where current could leave? If yes, they are not in series.

Parallel

Watch the dots split at the junction: more go through the smaller resistor, and they rejoin at the bottom.

Big R2

900 Ω out of 1000: R2 takes 90% of the voltage. The bigger resistor gets the bigger share.

Kirchhoff's current law

What you are looking at

Left: a source feeding three branches from one node; branch 3 has a switch. Top right: each branch current and the total. Bottom right: power in each branch.

Charge does not appear or disappear and cannot pile up in a wire. So at every junction, whatever flows in flows out. Watch the dots: the stream from the source splits, but never bunches up or thins out at the node.

$$\sum I_\text{in}=\sum I_\text{out}$$

Worked example

10 V across 100, 200, 300 Ω: 100 + 50 + 33 = 183 mA from the source. Open branch 3: 150 mA — the other branches don't change at all.

Common mistake

Thinking a new branch "steals" current from the others. With an ideal source each branch is independent; only the total changes.

Branch 3 open

No dots in branch 3; total current drops by exactly its share.

Thévenin: any network is one source and one resistor

What you are looking at

Top: a network (source, R1, R2) feeding a load R_L. Bottom: its Thévenin equivalent driving the same load. The load's dots move at exactly the same speed in both. Right: the load voltage and power for any load.

Seen from two terminals, any network of sources and resistors behaves exactly like a single source V_th behind a single resistor R_th. Not approximately: for every possible load.

  1. V_th: remove the load and measure the open-circuit voltage (here a divider: V·R2/(R1 + R2)).
  2. R_th: switch the sources off (voltage source → wire) and look back into the terminals (here R1‖R2).
  3. Now any load is a two-resistor problem.
$$V_\text{th}=V\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2},\qquad R_\text{th}=R_1\parallel R_2,\qquad P_L\ \text{max when}\ R_L=R_\text{th}$$

Worked example

10 V, 100 Ω, 200 Ω: V_th = 6.67 V, R_th = 66.7 Ω. A 470 Ω load gets 5.84 V. Maximum power with 66.7 Ω: 3.33 V, 167 mW.

Common mistake

Thinking maximum power means maximum efficiency: at the match only half the power reaches the load (aux plot).

Matched load

The dot sits at the top of the power curve.

Superposition: one source at a time

What you are looking at

Left: two sources feeding a middle node through R1 and R2, with R3 to ground. Switch either source off (it becomes a plain wire). Top right: the middle-node voltage from each source alone, their sum, and both together.

In a linear circuit, every voltage and current is a weighted sum of the sources. So you can solve with one source at a time and add the results. The third and fourth bars are always equal.

Worked example

V1 = 10 V alone: 5.45 V at the middle. V2 = 5 V alone: 1.36 V. Together: 6.82 V = 5.45 + 1.36.

Common mistakes

Removing a voltage source by leaving a gap: it must be replaced by a wire (0 V). Adding powers: power is not linear (it goes as V²).

Only V1

V2 is replaced by a wire: R2 now goes to ground, in parallel with R3.

Only V2

Now R1 is in parallel with R3.

The circuit as a landscape of potential

What you are looking at

A 3D picture: the circuit's floor plan in grey, and every wire lifted to the height of its voltage. Wires are flat (no voltage drop along a wire); resistors are orange ramps; the source is the red lift. Charges ride up the lift and roll down the ramps. Drag to rotate.

Voltage is height. A battery is a pump lifting charge from the floor (0 V) to the top (V). Each resistor lets it down by I·R. Two key rules become obvious: every wire is level (same node, same potential), and going round any loop you come back to where you started — every metre climbed is descended (Kirchhoff's voltage law).

$$\sum_\text{loop}\Delta V=0,\qquad \Delta V_R=IR$$

Worked example

10 V, R1 = 100, R2 = 200, R3 = 300, R4 = 150 Ω: R3 + R4 = 450 Ω in parallel with R2 gives 138 Ω; node 2 sits at 10·138/238 = 5.8 V; node 3 at 5.8·150/450 = 1.9 V.

Common mistake

Thinking current "uses up" voltage in wires: in an ideal wire there is no slope at all.

Big R1

R1 takes most of the drop: a long steep ramp, and everything after it sits low.

The RLC step response

What you are looking at

Left: a 1 V step applied to R, L and C in series. Top right: the capacitor voltage against ω₀t. Bottom right: the two poles of the circuit in the complex plane.

One storage element gives an exponential. Two give the possibility of oscillation, because energy can slosh between L and C. Whether it does depends on one number, the damping ratio ζ:

$$\omega_0=\frac1{\sqrt{LC}},\qquad \zeta=\frac R2\sqrt{\frac CL},\qquad s=-\zeta\omega_0\pm\omega_0\sqrt{\zeta^2-1}$$
  1. ζ < 1: complex poles, overshoot and ringing at about ω₀.
  2. ζ = 1: critically damped, the fastest response with no overshoot.
  3. ζ > 1: two real poles, a slow creep.

Worked example

L = 10 mH, C = 1 µF: ω₀ = 10 000 rad/s. Critical R = 2√(L/C) = 200 Ω. With 20 Ω, ζ = 0.1: overshoot ≈ 73%.

Common mistake

Reading "more R → more oscillation". It is the opposite: R damps.

ζ = 0.1

Poles close to the imaginary axis: long ringing.

ζ = 1

The two poles meet on the real axis.

ζ = 4

One pole slides toward 0: the slow one sets the creep.
