A layer is not a black box: it is a small amount of arithmetic applied in a pattern. Here every multiply and add is drawn. Cell colours: yellow = positive, blue = negative, grey = zero.

Activation functions: the bend in every layer

What you are looking at

Top: five common activation functions f(z), the selected one in bold, with your input z marked. Bottom: their slopes f′(z) — the factor by which gradients are multiplied on the way back during training.

Each layer computes a weighted sum z = w·x + b and passes it through an activation. Without it, any stack of layers would collapse into a single linear map: the activation is what lets a network bend the space.

  1. Sigmoid, tanh: smooth and bounded, but flat for large |z| — their slope tends to 0 (vanishing gradients).
  2. ReLU: max(0, z). Slope 1 or 0: simple, fast, the default; a unit stuck at z < 0 stops learning ("dead ReLU").
  3. Leaky ReLU, GELU: keep a little slope for z < 0.

Worked example

Ten sigmoid layers each pass back at most 0.25 of the gradient: 0.25¹⁰ ≈ 10⁻⁶. Ten ReLU layers in their active region pass back 1.

Sigmoid at z = 4

Output 0.98, slope 0.018: nearly no gradient.

ReLU at z = −2

Output 0 and slope 0: this unit contributes nothing and receives no update.

The dense layer: one dot product per output

What you are looking at

The input vector x, the weight matrix W, the bias b and the output y. Output j is built step by step from row j of W (orange) times the whole input; the current pair is outlined in white.
$$y_j=\sum_iW_{ji}x_i+b_j$$

Worked example

y₁ = 0.5·1 + (−1)·2 + 0.3·(−1) + 2·0.5 + 0.1 = −0.7.

Common mistake

Feeding an image to a dense layer flattens it: neighbouring pixels end up far apart in the vector, and the layer has to learn every position separately — 784 × 100 weights for a small MNIST layer.

Convolution: one small window, reused everywhere

What you are looking at

Left: a 7×7 image of a "7" (padding cells darker). Middle: the 3×3 kernel. Right: the output map filling in as the window (outlined) slides. The bottom line spells out the sum for the current output.
$$O_{mn}=\sum_{u,v}K_{uv}\,X_{m s+u d-p,\;n s+v d-p},\qquad N_\text{out}=\left\lfloor\frac{N+2p-d(k-1)-1}{s}\right\rfloor+1$$
  1. Place the kernel on a patch; multiply cell by cell; add: one output number.
  2. Slide by the stride s and repeat.
  3. Padding p adds a border of zeros; dilation d spaces the taps apart.

Worked example

N = 7, k = 3: no padding, stride 1 → 5×5; padding 1 → 7×7; stride 2 → 3×3; dilation 2 → 3×3.

Common mistake

Forgetting that the same 9 weights are used at every position: that is what makes convolution cheap and translation-equivariant.

Stride 2

The window jumps two cells at a time: output 3×3.

Padding 1

A ring of zeros: the kernel can centre on border pixels, the output stays 7×7.

Dilation 2

The taps are spread over a 5×5 area: a wider view without more weights.

Blur

All weights 1/9: each output is the average of its patch.

Pooling: summarise each window

What you are looking at

A 6×6 feature map and the 3×3 result of 2×2 pooling with stride 2; the current window is outlined.

Pooling has no parameters. It keeps the strongest (max) or the average response in each window, halving the size. It discards exact position: a feature that moves by one pixel often gives the same output.

Average pooling

Smoother, keeps more of the weak responses; max keeps only the peaks.

Channels: a filter is a block

What you are looking at

A 3D view: three input channels stacked as slabs, the 3×3×3 filter block (orange posts, white outlines on each slab), and the single output map it produces. Drag to rotate.

The step most often misunderstood. With C input channels, one filter is a k × k × C block. It multiplies every cell of all channels at that position and adds everything into one number. F filters give F output maps — the next layer's channels.

$$O_{mn}=\sum_{c=1}^{C}\sum_{u,v}K^{(c)}_{uv}X^{(c)}_{m+u,n+v}+b,\qquad \text{parameters}=(k^2C+1)F$$

A whole CNN: shapes and parameters

What you are looking at

Each block is a layer's output: height ∝ spatial size, width ∝ channels; underneath, its shape and parameter count.

Two patterns appear at once: spatial size shrinks while channels grow — the network trades resolution for description. And most parameters usually sit in the final dense layer, not in the convolutions.

Worked example

28×28 input, 8 then 16 filters: conv1 has (9·1 + 1)·8 = 80 parameters, conv2 (9·8 + 1)·16 = 1168, and the dense layer (7·7·16 + 1)·10 = 7850.

More filters
