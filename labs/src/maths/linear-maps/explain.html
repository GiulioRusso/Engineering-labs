A \(3\times3\) matrix moves every point of space. Its columns are where the three unit vectors land (red, green, blue arrows), and that is enough to know where everything goes. Drag the arrow tips. The cube's volume is \(\det A\); yellow lines are eigenvectors.

$$A\mathbf x = x_1\mathbf c_1 + x_2\mathbf c_2 + x_3\mathbf c_3, \qquad \det A = \mathbf c_1\cdot(\mathbf c_2\times\mathbf c_3), \qquad A\mathbf v = \lambda\mathbf v$$

A matrix moves every point

What you are looking at

The unit cube (ghost) and its image under the matrix A. The three coloured edges are the images of the unit vectors: they are the columns of A. Eigen-directions, if any, are drawn as lines.

A 3×3 matrix is a machine that moves every point of space: x ↦ Ax. It is linear, so it is completely decided by where it sends the three unit vectors — those images are the columns. Everything else follows: a point (x, y, z) goes to x·col₁ + y·col₂ + z·col₃.

$$A\mathbf x = x_1\mathbf a_1 + x_2\mathbf a_2 + x_3\mathbf a_3, \qquad \text{volume scale} = |\det A|$$
  1. Diagonal matrix: each axis is stretched by its own factor; the axes are eigenvectors.
  2. det A = product of the stretches = how much volumes grow.

Worked example

diag(1.8, 0.6, 1.2): the cube becomes a box 1.8 × 0.6 × 1.2, volume det = 1.296.

Common mistake

Reading the rows as the images of the axes: it is the columns.

Identity

\(I\) leaves every point where it is: \(\det I = 1\), every vector is an eigenvector with \(\lambda = 1\).

Uniform scale

Every direction is stretched by 1.5, so every vector is an eigenvector. The volume grows by \(1.5^3 = 3.375\).

Squash

Only \(z\) shrinks, to 0.3: the volume is 0.3.

Rotation

What you are looking at

The cube turned about the vertical axis.

A rotation keeps lengths and angles: its columns are unit vectors at right angles to each other (orthonormal), det = 1, and its inverse is its transpose. The axis of rotation is the only real eigenvector (eigenvalue 1).

$$R_z(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & -\sin\theta & 0\\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta & 0 \\ 0&0&1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad R^{-1} = R^{T}$$

Worked example

90°: (1, 0, 0) → (0, 1, 0), (0, 1, 0) → (−1, 0, 0). Two 90° turns: 180°, the matrix diag(−1, −1, 1).

Common mistake

Composing rotations in the wrong order: in 3D, AB ≠ BA.

A quarter turn

\(x\) goes to \(y\), \(y\) to \(-x\). Two of the eigenvalues are complex, \(\pm i\).

A half turn

Now every vector in the \(xy\) plane is flipped: eigenvalue \(-1\), twice. Still \(\det = 1\): turning is not mirroring.

Shear

What you are looking at

The cube slanted like a deck of cards pushed sideways.

A shear slides layers parallel to each other, by an amount proportional to their height. Volumes stay the same (det = 1) but angles change. Only one direction is left unchanged.

$$\begin{pmatrix}1 & s & 0\\0&1&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}: \; (x, y, z) \mapsto (x + sy,\ y,\ z)$$

Worked example

s = 1: the top-front edge moves one unit sideways; the box becomes a parallelepiped with the same volume.

Gentle shear

The top of the cube moves 0.4 along \(x\). The \(x\) axis is the only eigenvector.

Along z

Higher layers slide diagonally; the floor stays fixed.

Reflection

What you are looking at

The cube mirrored across a plane.

A reflection flips one direction and keeps the others: det = −1 — the cube's orientation is reversed (a right hand becomes a left hand). Reflecting twice gives back the identity.

$$\text{diag}(-1, 1, 1): x \mapsto -x, \qquad \det = -1, \qquad A^2 = I$$

Common mistake

Confusing −I (through the origin) with a mirror: in 3D, −I has det −1 but is a point inversion.

Mirror in the floor

\(z \to -z\): the cube hangs below the floor.

Through the origin

\(-I\) flips all three axes: \(\det = (-1)^3 = -1\).

Projection

What you are looking at

The cube flattened onto a plane (or a line).

A projection squashes space onto a lower-dimensional subspace: a shadow. Information is lost — many points land on the same image — so it cannot be undone: det = 0, no inverse. The number of dimensions that survive is the rank.

$$P^2 = P, \qquad \det P = 0$$

Worked example

Straight onto the floor: (x, y, z) → (x, y, 0), rank 2. Projecting twice is the same as once.

Common mistake

Trying to solve Ax = b when det A = 0: there are either no solutions or infinitely many.

Straight down

Every point drops to \(z = 0\): the shadow at noon.

Onto a line

Only one column is left: the whole space lands on the \(x\) axis. The rank (dimension of the image) is 1.

Any matrix

What you are looking at

The cube under a general matrix, with its eigen-directions.

Any matrix combines stretching, rotating and shearing. Eigenvectors are the special directions that are only stretched, not turned: Av = λv. For a symmetric matrix they are at right angles and the map is a pure stretch along them.

$$A\mathbf v = \lambda\mathbf v, \qquad \det(A - \lambda I) = 0, \qquad \det A = \lambda_1\lambda_2\lambda_3$$

Worked example

A symmetric 3×3 matrix with eigenvalues 2.2, 0.8 and 0.2 stretches a sphere into an ellipsoid with those semi-axes.

Try it

Pick "Undo it": the inverse matrix moves the cube back to the unit cube.

The inverse

\(A^{-1}\) undoes \(A\): its determinant is \(1/\det A\) and it has the same eigenvectors with eigenvalues \(1/\lambda\).
