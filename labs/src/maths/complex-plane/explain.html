A complex number \(z = a + bi\) is a point in the plane: real part across, imaginary part up. In polar form it is a length and an angle. Drag \(z\) and \(w\).

$$z = a + bi = r\,e^{i\theta}, \qquad r = |z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}, \quad i^2 = -1$$

Adding is joining arrows

What you are looking at

The complex plane: real part across, imaginary part up. z and w are arrows from the origin (drag their tips); their sum is drawn as the diagonal of the parallelogram.

A complex number a + jb is just a point — or an arrow — in a plane. Adding works exactly like adding displacements: real parts add, imaginary parts add. Put w's tail on z's tip and the sum is where you end up.

$$z + w = (a + c) + j(b + d), \qquad |z + w| \le |z| + |w|$$

Worked example

(2 + j) + (1 + 2j) = 3 + 3j. Lengths: √5 + √5 = 4.47, but |3 + 3j| = 4.24 — the arrows do not point the same way.

Common mistake

Adding magnitudes: you add arrows, not lengths.

Try it

Drag w round a circle: the sum traces a circle around z's tip.

Two real numbers

On the real axis, complex addition is ordinary addition: \(2 + 1 = 3\).

Number plus conjugate

\(\bar z = a - bi\) is the mirror image across the real axis. The imaginary parts cancel: \(z + \bar z = 2a\).

Opposite numbers

\(-z\) points the other way; the parallelogram collapses and the sum is 0.

Multiplying: rotate and stretch

What you are looking at

z, w and their product, with arcs showing each angle. The product's angle is the sum of the two angles; its length is the product of the two lengths.

Algebra says (a + jb)(c + jd) = (ac − bd) + j(ad + bc). Geometry says something much simpler: write each number as length and angle, and multiplying multiplies the lengths and adds the angles. Multiplying by j is a quarter turn — that is why j² = −1: two quarter turns point backwards.

$$z = r e^{j\theta}, \quad w = s e^{j\varphi} \;\Rightarrow\; zw = rs\,e^{j(\theta + \varphi)}$$

Worked example

(1 + j)(1 + j): both have length √2 and angle 45°. Product: length 2, angle 90° → 2j. Algebra: 1 + 2j + j² = 2j ✓.

Common mistake

Forgetting that j² = −1 when expanding brackets.

Try it

Put w on the unit circle: the product is just z rotated.

Times a real number

\(w = 2\) has angle 0: the product is \(z\) stretched twice as long, same direction.

Times i

\(i\) has length 1 and angle 90°: multiplying by it is a quarter turn, no stretch. \((2 + i)\,i = -1 + 2i\).

i times i

Two quarter turns make a half turn: \(i^2 = -1\). This is why \(i\) is needed: no real number squares to \(-1\).

Number times conjugate

The angles \(\theta\) and \(-\theta\) cancel: the product is real and equals \(|z|^2\). Here \((1 + i)(1 - i) = 2\).

Dividing: rotate back and shrink

What you are looking at

z, w and z/w with their angles.

Division undoes multiplication: divide the lengths, subtract the angles. Algebraically, multiply top and bottom by the conjugate w̄ to make the denominator real.

$$\frac{z}{w} = \frac{z\,\bar w}{|w|^2} = \frac{r}{s}e^{j(\theta - \varphi)}$$

Worked example

2j/(1 + j) = 2j(1 − j)/2 = j(1 − j) = 1 + j. Lengths: 2/√2 = √2; angles: 90° − 45° = 45° ✓.

Common mistake

Dividing real and imaginary parts separately: (a + jb)/(c + jd) ≠ a/c + j b/d.

A number over itself

Same length, same angle: the quotient is 1.

Divide by i

Undo a quarter turn: \(z / i = -i\,z\), a turn of −90°.

Powers spiral

What you are looking at

z, z², z³, … zⁿ joined in order.

Each multiplication by z turns by the same angle and stretches by the same factor: the powers lie on a spiral. Outward if |z| > 1, inward if |z| < 1, on a circle if |z| = 1. This is the picture behind growing and decaying oscillations — and behind e^{jωt}.

$$z^n = r^n e^{jn\theta} \qquad (\text{De Moivre})$$

Worked example

z = 1.0 at 34°: z⁸ is at 272°. jⁿ cycles j, −1, −j, 1.

Try it

Drag z across the unit circle and watch the spiral reverse.

Spiralling out

\(|z| > 1\): every power is longer than the last.

Spiralling in

\(|z| < 1\): the powers shrink towards 0.

Powers of i

\(i, -1, -i, 1\): four quarter turns bring you back. The powers repeat every 4.

Roots sit on a regular polygon

What you are looking at

The n numbers whose nth power is z, as dots: they form a regular n-gon.

To undo "turn by θ, n times", turn by θ/n. But turning a full extra circle lands in the same place, so θ/n + k·360°/n works for every k: n different roots, evenly spaced.

$$w_k = r^{1/n}\,e^{j(\theta + 2\pi k)/n}, \quad k = 0, \dots, n - 1$$

Worked example

Cube roots of 1: 1, e^{j120°}, e^{j240°} = 1, −0.5 ± 0.866j. Their sum is 0 — balanced like three-phase power.

Common mistake

Thinking √ gives one answer: in the complex plane every nonzero number has exactly n nth roots.

Square roots of 1

\(1\) and \(-1\): both square to 1, half a turn apart.

Sixth roots of unity

Six points 60° apart: a regular hexagon.

Square roots of i

\(i\) is at 90°, so its roots are at 45° and 225°: \(\pm\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}(1 + i)\).

Cube roots of −1

\(-1\) itself is one of them; the other two are \(\tfrac12 \pm \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} i\).
