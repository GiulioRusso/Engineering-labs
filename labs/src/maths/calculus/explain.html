The derivative: the slope at a point

What you are looking at

The curve, a point x₀ on it, and a second point h further on. The line through both (the secant) has slope Δy/Δx. Shrink h and the secant turns into the tangent. The bottom plot is the derivative f′(x) drawn for every x, with the current slope marked.

For a line the slope is the same everywhere. For a curve it changes from point to point, so we ask: how steep is it right here? Take a second point close by, measure the slope of the chord, and let the second point slide in. The number the slope settles on is the derivative.

$$f'(x_0) = \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0)}{h}$$
  1. Pick h and compute the chord slope (rise over run between the two points).
  2. Halve h, again and again: the slope converges.
  3. f′ > 0: the function is rising; f′ < 0: falling; f′ = 0: flat — a top, a bottom or a pause.

Worked example

f(x) = x² at x₀ = 1: h = 1 gives (4 − 1)/1 = 3; h = 0.1 gives (1.21 − 1)/0.1 = 2.1; h = 0.01 gives 2.01. The limit is 2 = 2x₀. In general (x²)′ = 2x, (sin x)′ = cos x, (eˣ)′ = eˣ.

Common mistakes

Putting h = 0 directly: 0/0 is meaningless — the limit is about what happens as h gets small. Thinking f′ = 0 always means a maximum: x³ is flat at 0 and keeps rising.

Try it

Pull h toward zero and watch the secant swing into the tangent. Then move x₀ and follow the dot on the bottom plot: it traces f′.

h → 0

For \(f = x^2/2\) the secant slope is \(x_0 + h/2\): with \(h = 0.01\) it is 1.005, almost exactly \(f'(1) = 1\).

Slope zero

At the bottom of the curve the tangent is horizontal: \(f'(0) = 0\). Minima and maxima are where the derivative vanishes.

The derivative of sine

At \(x = 0\) the sine rises with slope 1 \(= \cos 0\). Everywhere, \((\sin x)' = \cos x\).

Top of the sine

At \(x = \pi/2\) the sine peaks: slope \(\cos(\pi/2) = 0\).

Exponential

For \(e^{x/2}\) the slope is half the height at every point: \((e^{kx})' = k\,e^{kx}\).

The integral: adding up strips

What you are looking at

The area under the curve between a and b, approximated by n strips (left, midpoint or trapezoid). Strips above the axis count positive, below negative. The readout compares the sum with the exact value.

The integral is a sum of many tiny products: height × width. Cut the interval into n strips, add their areas, and make the strips thinner and thinner. The sum converges to the area.

$$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i)\,\Delta x, \qquad \Delta x = \frac{b-a}{n}, \qquad \int_a^b f\,dx = F(b) - F(a) \text{ with } F' = f$$
  1. Left rule: height at each strip's left edge — always wrong in the same direction on a rising curve.
  2. Midpoint and trapezoid: errors fall much faster as n grows (as 1/n² instead of 1/n).
  3. The fundamental theorem: instead of summing, find a function whose derivative is f and subtract its values at the ends.

Worked example

∫₀² x² dx: four left strips give 0.5·(0 + 0.25 + 1 + 2.25) = 1.75; the exact value is 2³/3 = 2.667. Midpoint with four strips: 2.625 — already within 1.6 %.

Common mistake

Treating "area" as always positive: the integral of sin over a full turn is 0, because the part below the axis subtracts.

Try it

Raise n with the left rule and watch the error shrink slowly; switch to midpoint and see it collapse.

More strips

With the left rule the error shrinks like \(1/n\): 50 strips are 12 times better than 4. The exact area of \(x^2/2\) from 0 to 2 is \(4/3\).

Midpoint rule

Taking each strip's height in its middle cancels most of the error: it falls like \(1/n^2\).

Trapezoids

Join the curve's values at the strip edges with straight lines. Also \(1/n^2\), about twice the midpoint error with the opposite sign.

Signed area

Below the axis, area counts negative. Over a full period the sine's positive and negative halves cancel: \(\int_0^{2\pi}\sin x\,dx = 0\).

Taylor polynomials: copying a function

What you are looking at

The function (grey) and its Taylor polynomial of order N around x₀ (blue). The bottom plot shows the error.

Near a point, any smooth function looks like a line (its tangent). Add a curvature term and it looks like a parabola; keep adding terms and the copy matches over a wider and wider range. Each term matches one more derivative at x₀.

$$f(x) \approx \sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{f^{(k)}(x_0)}{k!}(x - x_0)^k, \qquad \sin x = x - \frac{x^3}{6} + \frac{x^5}{120} - \cdots$$
  1. Order 0: the value. Order 1: the tangent line. Order 2: matches the bending too.
  2. Near x₀ the error shrinks like |x − x₀|^{N+1}: tiny close in, large far away.

Worked example

sin 0.5 with x − x³/6: 0.5 − 0.0208 = 0.4792; the true value is 0.4794. This is how calculators and physics approximations (sin θ ≈ θ for small angles) work.

Common mistake

Using a Taylor polynomial far from x₀: for some functions it never works there, however many terms you add.

Try it

Increase the order one step at a time and watch the region of good fit widen.

Order 3

\(\sin x \approx x - x^3/6\): good up to about \(|x| = 1.5\).

Order 9

Five terms of \(x - \tfrac{x^3}{3!} + \tfrac{x^5}{5!} - \dots\) follow the sine for more than a full half-wave. The series converges for every \(x\).

Exponential

\(e^{x/2} = \sum (x/2)^n / n!\): all terms positive, the fit improves fastest on the right.

Far from the centre

Near \(x_0\) the polynomial is excellent, but a polynomial must eventually shoot off to \(\pm\infty\) while the bell curve goes to 0. More terms widen the good region slowly.
