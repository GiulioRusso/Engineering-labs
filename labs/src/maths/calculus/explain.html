Derivative, integral, Taylor

Three ideas built on one picture: a smooth curve, looked at very close up (derivative), added up in thin strips (integral), or copied by a polynomial (Taylor).

The derivative is a limit of slopes

Draw a secant through \(x_0\) and \(x_0 + h\). As \(h\) shrinks, the secant turns into the tangent and its slope into the derivative.

$$f'(x_0) = \lim_{h\to 0} \frac{f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0)}{h}$$

The integral is a limit of sums

Cut the area into \(n\) strips and add their areas. More strips, smaller error. Taking the height at the strip's midpoint is much better than at its edge: the error falls like \(1/n^2\) instead of \(1/n\).

$$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \lim_{n\to\infty} \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i^*)\,\Delta x, \qquad \Delta x = \frac{b-a}{n}$$

Taylor: a polynomial that copies the curve

Match the value, the slope, the curvature… at one point. Each extra term hugs the curve over a wider range.

$$T_N(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{N} \frac{f^{(n)}(x_0)}{n!}(x - x_0)^n$$
