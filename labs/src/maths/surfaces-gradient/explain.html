A function of two variables is a landscape: height \(z = f(x, y)\) over the floor. Drag the point to walk on it. The gradient (purple) points uphill and is perpendicular to the level curves; the tangent plane is the flat approximation at the point.

$$\nabla f = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x},\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right), \qquad z \approx f(\mathbf x_0) + \nabla f(\mathbf x_0)\cdot(\mathbf x - \mathbf x_0)$$

A surface and its gradient

What you are looking at

A surface z = f(x, y) in 3D with a point on it, its tangent plane, contour lines (on the surface and on the floor), and the gradient arrow on the floor.

A function of two variables is a landscape: at every (x, y) a height. At a point, two slopes matter: east–west (∂f/∂x) and north–south (∂f/∂y). Put them together in a vector: the gradient. It points straight uphill, its length is how steep, and it is always at right angles to the contour lines.

$$\nabla f = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right), \qquad z \approx f(x_0, y_0) + \nabla f\cdot(\mathbf x - \mathbf x_0)$$
  1. ∂f/∂x: freeze y, slope in the x direction. Same for y.
  2. Walking along a contour, height does not change: the gradient is perpendicular to it.
  3. ∇f = 0: a flat spot — bottom, top or saddle.

Worked example

f = x² + y² at (1.5, 1): ∇f = (3, 2), length 3.6, pointing away from the bottom. Tangent plane: z ≈ 3.25 + 3(x − 1.5) + 2(y − 1).

Common mistake

Thinking the gradient lives on the surface: it is a 2D vector on the floor, telling you which way to walk.

Try it

Move the point: the arrow always crosses the floor contours at right angles, and gets longer where the contours are closer.

At the bottom

The tangent plane is horizontal: \(\nabla f = 0\). A point where the gradient vanishes is a critical point; here it is a minimum.

Far out

Twice as far, twice as steep: \(|\nabla f| = r/2\).

The saddle

What you are looking at

z = x² − y²: up along x, down along y.

At the centre the gradient is zero, yet it is neither a top nor a bottom: in one direction it is a minimum, in the other a maximum. A mountain pass. The second derivatives decide (Hessian).

$$f = x^2 - y^2, \qquad \nabla f = (2x, -2y), \qquad f_{xx}f_{yy} - f_{xy}^2 = -4 < 0 \Rightarrow \text{saddle}$$

Common mistake

Concluding "∇f = 0 so it is a minimum".

A saddle point

\(\nabla f = 0\) and the tangent plane is flat, yet it is neither a top nor a bottom: a critical point need not be an extremum.

Along x

On the \(x\) axis \(f = x^2/4\): the gradient points along \(+x\), away from the centre.

Along y

On the \(y\) axis \(f = -y^2/4\): uphill is back towards the centre.

Hills and valleys

What you are looking at

A landscape with several summits and hollows.

Gradient descent — walk against the gradient — finds the bottom of the nearest valley, not necessarily the deepest one. That is how machine learning trains its models, and why it can get stuck.

$$\mathbf x_{k+1} = \mathbf x_k - \eta\,\nabla f(\mathbf x_k)$$

Common mistake

Assuming a local minimum is the global one.

On a summit

At the top of a hill \(\nabla f = 0\): every direction goes down. A local maximum.

In the hollow

At the bottom of the hollow, again \(\nabla f \approx 0\): a local minimum.

A ripple

What you are looking at

Circular waves spreading from the centre: contours are circles and the gradient points radially.

For a function of the distance r only, the gradient is f′(r) along the radial direction. Where the ring crests or troughs, the gradient vanishes on a whole circle.

$$f = g(r), \quad r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} \;\Rightarrow\; \nabla f = g'(r)\,\hat{\mathbf r}$$

In the trough

At the bottom of the first ring the gradient vanishes, and along the ring the height is constant: a whole circle of minima, not a single point.
