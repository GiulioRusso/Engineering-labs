The geometric series

What you are looking at

The terms aₙ (bars) and the partial sums Sₙ (dots) as n grows; the band of width ±ε around the limit shows from which n on the sums stay inside.

A series is an infinite sum. It can still have a finite value if the terms shrink fast enough — like walking half the way to a wall, then half the remaining, and so on: you never pass the wall. The geometric series multiplies each term by the same ratio r.

$$S_N = a\frac{1 - r^{N}}{1 - r} \;\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}\; \frac{a}{1 - r} \quad (|r| < 1)$$
  1. |r| < 1: rⁿ → 0, so the sum converges to a/(1 − r).
  2. r < 0: the partial sums zig-zag above and below the limit.
  3. |r| ≥ 1: the terms do not shrink; the sum runs away.

Worked example

1 + ½ + ¼ + … = 1/(1 − ½) = 2. The repeating decimal 0.999… = 9/10 + 9/100 + … = 0.9/(1 − 0.1) = 1.

Common mistake

Thinking "infinitely many terms" must give infinity. It depends on how fast they shrink.

Try it

Move r toward 1: the limit grows and the sums take longer and longer to get inside the band.

Negative ratio

With \(r = -\tfrac12\) the steps alternate up and down, closing in on \(\frac{1}{1 + 1/2} = \tfrac23\) from both sides.

Close to 1

\(r = 0.9\): the limit is \(10\), but the error only shrinks by 10 % per term, so it takes about 30 terms per digit.

r = 1

Every term is \(a\): \(S_n = na\) grows without bound. A series can only converge if its terms go to zero.

r > 1

The terms grow, so the sums explode exponentially. No limit.

The p-series 1/nᵖ

What you are looking at

Terms 1/nᵖ and their partial sums.

Terms shrinking to zero are necessary but not enough. The harmonic series 1 + ½ + ⅓ + … has terms that go to zero, yet its sum grows without limit — just very slowly (like ln N). The dividing line is p = 1.

$$\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{n^p} \text{ converges} \iff p > 1, \qquad \sum \frac{1}{n^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{6}, \qquad \sum_{n\le N}\frac1n \approx \ln N + 0.577$$

Worked example

Harmonic series: 200 terms sum to 5.88; a million terms to only 14.4 — but it never stops growing.

Common mistake

"The terms go to zero, so it converges": false — the harmonic series is the counter-example.

The harmonic series

\(1 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac13 + \dots\) grows like \(\ln n\): after 200 terms it is only about 5.9, yet it passes every number eventually. Terms going to zero do not guarantee convergence.

p = 4

The terms fall so fast that a handful already gives \(\pi^4/90 \approx 1.0823\) to four digits.

p = ½

\(1/\sqrt n\) shrinks even more slowly than \(1/n\): the sum grows like \(2\sqrt n\).

Alternating series

What you are looking at

Terms with alternating signs and the partial sums zig-zagging around the limit, inside a narrowing funnel.

If the signs alternate and the sizes shrink steadily to zero, each new term overshoots the previous error by less: the sums trap the limit between them. The error after N terms is smaller than the next term.

$$1 - \tfrac12 + \tfrac13 - \cdots = \ln 2, \qquad 1 - \tfrac13 + \tfrac15 - \cdots = \frac{\pi}{4}, \qquad |S - S_N| \le |a_{N+1}|$$

Worked example

Leibniz: to get π to 2 decimals (error 0.005 in π/4 ≈ 0.00125) you need about 400 terms — correct but slow.

Common mistake

Rearranging the terms: for conditionally convergent series, reordering can change the sum.

π from odd numbers

\(4\left(1 - \tfrac13 + \tfrac15 - \dots\right) = \pi\). Beautiful but slow: the error after \(n\) terms is about \(1/n\).

A smaller ε

Since the error is below \(1/(n+1)\), staying within 0.01 needs about 100 terms. The ε band shows when the sums stay inside for good.

The limit of a sequence

What you are looking at

The sequence (1 + 1/n)ⁿ approaching e, with a band of width ±ε: from some N on, every term stays inside.

"aₙ tends to L" means: however thin a band you draw around L, eventually the sequence enters it and never leaves. A smaller ε needs a later N — but there is always one.

$$\lim_{n\to\infty} a_n = L \iff \forall\varepsilon > 0\ \exists N: n > N \Rightarrow |a_n - L| < \varepsilon, \qquad \left(1 + \frac1n\right)^n \to e$$

Worked example

(1 + 1/n)ⁿ: n = 1 gives 2, n = 10 gives 2.594, n = 100 gives 2.705, n = 1000 gives 2.717; e = 2.71828. The error is about e/(2n).

Common mistake

Thinking the sequence must reach L: it only has to get as close as you like.

A tighter band

A smaller \(\varepsilon\) needs a later \(N\). Here the error is about \(e/2n\), so \(\varepsilon = 0.005\) needs \(n \approx 270\).
