Feedback and steady-state error

The controller measures the error \(e = r - y\) and drives the plant with \(K e\). Once things settle, what error is left depends on the input and on the type of the loop: the number of integrators (poles at \(s = 0\)) in \(KG(s)\). The plane shows the closed-loop poles; the 3D view adds the surface \(|T(s)|\).

$$T = \frac{KG}{1 + KG}, \qquad e_\infty = \lim_{s\to0} \frac{s\,R(s)}{1 + KG(s)}$$

More gain

A type 0 loop always leaves an error: some error is needed to keep pushing. With \(K = 20\) it shrinks to \(1/21\), about 5 %.

An integrator

A type 1 loop integrates the error: as long as any error is left, the push keeps growing. It only stops when \(e = 0\).

Following a ramp

The type 1 loop follows a moving target with a constant lag \(1/K_v = 1/K\): to keep moving it needs a steady error.

Type 0 and a ramp

Without an integrator the output cannot keep up with a moving target: the error grows without limit.

Type 2, parabola

Two integrators follow a steadily accelerating target with a constant error \(1/K_a\), here \(2/K\).

The root locus

As the gain \(K\) grows from 0 to ∞, the closed-loop poles (dots) start at the open-loop poles (×) and travel to the zeros (○) or off to infinity along the yellow curves. Pick \(K\) by where you want the poles.

$$1 + K G(s) = 0 \iff \angle G(s) = 180°, \quad K = \frac{1}{|G(s)|}$$

The poles meet

At \(K \approx 0.63\) the two slow real poles meet at \(s \approx -0.45\) (breakaway) and split into a complex pair: from here on the response overshoots.

Oscillatory

The pair moves up and towards the axis: less damping, more overshoot, but a faster rise.

On the edge

At \(K = 12\) the pair reaches the \(j\omega\) axis at \(\pm j\sqrt3\): the loop oscillates forever. The Routh criterion gives the same limit: \(4 \cdot 3 > K\).

Too much gain

Past \(K = 12\) the pair is in the right half plane: more gain made the loop unstable.

A zero helps

A zero at \(-2\) attracts one branch and bends the others away from the right half plane: the loop stays stable for much larger gains. This is what a lead compensator does.

Two poles

With only two poles the branches go straight up and down from \(-1\): more gain means less damping, but never instability.
