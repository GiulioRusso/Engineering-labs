Feed the loop a sine of frequency \(\omega\): it comes out scaled by \(|L(j\omega)|\) and shifted by \(\angle L(j\omega)\). The yellow curve traces \(L(j\omega)\) in the complex plane as \(\omega\) grows: the Nyquist plot (to scale up to \(|L| = 2\), compressed beyond). Magnitude and phase against \(\log\omega\) are the Bode diagram below. Tick 3D view in View to lift the curve by \(\log\omega\): its shadows are both diagrams.

The Bode plot

What you are looking at

Magnitude (dB) and phase of the open-loop transfer function against log frequency, with the asymptotes and the current frequency marked.

On log–log axes every pole and zero becomes a corner between straight lines: a pole bends the magnitude down by 20 dB/decade and the phase by 90°, a zero bends them up. An integrator is a −20 dB/decade line everywhere. You can sketch any Bode plot by adding these pieces.

$$|G| = \prod|\text{factors}| \Rightarrow 20\log|G| = \sum 20\log|\text{factor}|, \qquad \angle G = \sum\angle\text{factors}$$

Worked example

K/(s/1 + 1): flat at 20 log K, −3 dB and −45° at 1 rad/s, then −20 dB/dec. Add a pole at 10: −40 dB/dec beyond 10, phase toward −180°.

Common mistake

Forgetting that gain moves the magnitude curve up or down but never changes the phase.

Two poles

Above both corners the slopes add: −40 dB per decade, and the phase heads to −180°.

An integrator

\(1/j\omega\) falls 20 dB per decade at every frequency and adds a constant −90°. Infinite gain at \(\omega = 0\): zero steady-state error to a step.

A zero

A zero rises +20 dB per decade above its corner and adds up to +90°: here it cancels the slope of the second pole above 5 rad/s.

Gain

\(K = 10\) lifts the whole magnitude by 20 dB. The phase does not change.

The Nyquist plot and stability margins

What you are looking at

G(jω) drawn in the complex plane as ω sweeps (and its mirror for negative ω); the critical point −1; the gain and phase margins.

A feedback loop goes unstable when the signal returns around the loop with the same size and inverted sign — that is, when G(jω) passes through −1. The Nyquist plot shows how close it comes. Encircling −1 means instability.

$$\text{GM} = \frac{1}{|G(j\omega_{180})|}, \qquad \text{PM} = 180^\circ + \angle G(j\omega_c)\;\text{ at } |G(j\omega_c)| = 1$$

Worked example

K/(s(s + 1)(s/2 + 1)): crossing at −1 when K = 3 (gain margin of the K = 1 loop: 3 = 9.5 dB). K = 5 encircles −1: unstable.

Common mistake

Checking only the gain margin: a loop with a small phase margin rings badly even if GM is large.

Small gain

The curve crosses the negative real axis far to the right of −1: large gain and phase margins, a calm, slow loop.

More gain

The whole curve grows: it crosses closer to −1, both margins shrink, the step response overshoots more.

Through −1

At \(K = 3\) the curve passes through −1 at \(\omega = \sqrt2\): GM = 0 dB, PM = 0°. The closed loop oscillates forever.

Encircling −1

Now −1 is inside the loop of the curve: two closed-loop poles in the right half plane. Unstable.

Compensation: lead and lag

What you are looking at

Bode plots with and without a compensator, the margins, and the closed-loop step response.

A lead compensator (zero before pole) adds phase near the crossover frequency: more phase margin, less ringing, faster response. A lag compensator (pole before zero) adds gain at low frequency only: smaller steady-state error without touching the margins.

$$C_\text{lead}(s) = \frac{s/\omega_z + 1}{s/\omega_p + 1},\; \omega_z < \omega_p, \qquad \varphi_{\max} = \arcsin\frac{\omega_p - \omega_z}{\omega_p + \omega_z}$$

Worked example

Zero at 0.8, pole at 8: up to 55° of extra phase at √(0.8·8) = 2.5 rad/s. The phase margin goes from 5° to about 45°.

Common mistake

Placing the lead's maximum far from the crossover frequency: it adds phase where it is not needed.

Without a compensator

\(K = 2.5\) gives a fast loop but only 5° of phase margin: the step response rings for a long time.

Lag

Ten times the gain (so ten times less ramp error) with a lag at 0.005–0.05 rad/s: at the crossover the lag is invisible, the margins stay near 30°. The price: a slow tail in the step response.

A wider lead

A larger \(p/z\) gives more phase but also more high-frequency gain, which amplifies sensor noise.
