The CMOS inverter

What you are looking at

Left: a PMOS on top (to V_DD) and an NMOS below (to ground), gates tied together as the input, drains tied together as the output; a highlighted path shows which transistor conducts. Top right: output against input (the transfer curve). Bottom right: the current flowing straight from V_DD to ground.

Digital circuits use the same MOSFETs as analog ones, but only in the two places where they make good switches: fully off and fully on. Input low: the NMOS is off and the PMOS connects the output to V_DD. Input high: the reverse. In both states one switch is open, so no current flows, and the output sits exactly on a rail.

  1. v_in < V_TN = 0.7 V: NMOS off → out = V_DD.
  2. v_in > V_DD − V_TP = 2.6 V: PMOS off → out = 0.
  3. In between both conduct; the output is where their currents balance, I_n = I_p, and it falls steeply around the switching threshold V_M.
$$I_n(v_\text{in},v_\text{out})=I_p(v_\text{in},v_\text{out}),\qquad V_M=\frac{V_{DD}}2 \text{ when } k_p=k_n$$

Worked example

Electrons move about twice as easily as holes (k_n = 200 µA/V², k_p = 100 µA/V²). Making the PMOS twice as wide equalises them: V_M = 1.65 V, exactly half of 3.3 V. With equal widths V_M drops to about 1.5 V.

Common mistakes

Thinking CMOS draws no current at all: during every transition both devices conduct briefly (the "crowbar" current, bottom chart). Slow input edges make this worse.

Try it

Let the input sweep and watch the highlighted path move from top to bottom; the current only flows in the middle.

Input at V_DD/2

Both transistors are on and fighting: the output is in the steep middle and the current is at its peak (about 90 µA here). A gate must never sit here.

Equal widths

The NMOS is now the stronger device: it starts winning earlier, so the curve and V_M shift left.

Noise margins: why a bit survives ten thousand gates

What you are looking at

Left: a chain of four inverters. Top right: the transfer curve with the two points where its slope is −1 (V_IL and V_IH). Bottom right: a noisy input, the signal after two gates, and after four.

An analog stage passes its input errors on and adds its own. A logic gate does the opposite: outside its steep middle region the gain is less than one, so small disturbances are squashed; inside it the gain is large, so a degraded level is pushed hard to the nearest rail. Noise stops accumulating — this is restoration.

$$NM_L=V_{IL}-V_{OL},\qquad NM_H=V_{OH}-V_{IH}$$
  1. V_IL: the highest input still read as "low" (slope −1).
  2. V_IH: the lowest input read as "high".
  3. V_OL, V_OH: what the gate outputs at those points.
  4. Any disturbance smaller than the margin is removed by the next gate.

Worked example

Symmetric 3.3 V inverter: V_IL ≈ 1.42 V, V_IH ≈ 1.88 V, V_OH ≈ 3.06 V, V_OL ≈ 0.24 V → both margins ≈ 1.18 V, about 36% of V_DD.

Common mistake

Taking V_DD/2 as the margin. The guaranteed band is set by the unity-gain points, and skewed sizing shrinks one side.

0.9 V of noise

The input is badly corrupted, yet after a few gates the blue trace is clean rails again.

Skewed gate

A weak PMOS moves V_M down: NM_L shrinks, NM_H grows. Low-level noise now gets through more easily.

Gate delay: an RC circuit in disguise

What you are looking at

Left: an inverter switching its output low, driving a load capacitance made of the gates it feeds plus the wire. Top right: the output discharging; the dot marks the 50% point. Bottom right: delay against fan-out.

Logic diagrams have no time in them, but every gate output must charge or discharge the input capacitance of everything it drives, through a transistor that, when on, behaves like a resistor. That is the RC circuit from the first circuits lesson.

$$R_\text{on}\approx\frac{1}{k\,W\,(V_{DD}-V_{th})},\qquad t_p\approx0.69\,R_\text{on}C_L,\qquad C_L=n\,C_g+C_\text{wire}$$

Worked example

k = 200 µA/V², W = 1, V_DD = 3.3 V: R_on ≈ 1.9 kΩ. Fan-out 2 with 2 fF gates and 1 fF of wire: C_L = 5 fF → t_p ≈ 6.6 ps. Fan-out 8: 17 fF → 22 ps.

Common mistake

Simply making every driver huge: a wide transistor has a large gate capacitance and slows down whatever drives it. Real designs use chains of gradually larger buffers.

Fan-out 8

More than three times the capacitance, more than three times the delay.

Wider driver

Four times wider: R_on ÷ 4, delay ÷ 4 — for this gate.

Power: the equation that stopped the megahertz race

What you are looking at

Top: total power against supply voltage, with your chip's operating point. Bottom: gate delay against supply voltage.

Each time a node rises, the supply delivers charge C·V_DD; half the energy is stored in C, half is burned in the PMOS. When it falls, the stored half is burned in the NMOS. So each full cycle costs C·V_DD², however good the transistors are. Multiply by how often nodes switch:

$$P=\alpha\,C\,V_{DD}^2\,f\;+\;I_\text{leak}V_{DD}$$

Worked example

α = 0.1, C = 100 pF, f = 100 MHz, 3.3 V: 10.9 mW. At 1.2 V: 1.4 mW — 7.6 times less. But the delay rises steeply as V_DD approaches the threshold: you trade speed for energy.

Common mistake

Raising the clock without counting: power grows linearly with f, and since faster needs a higher V_DD, roughly with f³. Around 2005 chips hit the heat limit and clocks stopped rising.

1.2 V

(1.2/3.3)² = 0.13: same work, an eighth of the power, much slower gates.

Leakage

When almost nothing switches (α = 0.001), the small but constant leakage current dominates: the "idle" power of modern chips.

Memory: two inverters holding each other

What you are looking at

Left: two inverters in a ring — each one's output is the other's input. Top right: the two node voltages over time, starting almost equal. Bottom right: the "butterfly": both inverters' transfer curves on the same axes; the crossings are the possible resting states.

A ring of two inverters agrees with itself in two ways: A high and B low, or the reverse. Those are the two stable states — one bit. There is a third crossing in the middle, an equilibrium where both sit at V_M, but it is unstable: the gain around the loop is larger than 1, so any tiny difference is amplified until the pair falls to one of the rails. This is the core of every SRAM cell and flip-flop.

$$\Delta V(t)\approx\Delta V_0\,e^{t/\tau}\;\Rightarrow\;t_\text{decide}\approx\tau\ln\frac{V_{DD}/2}{\Delta V_0}$$

Worked example

A 1 mV push decides in about 0.24 ns; 1 µV takes about 0.42 ns. A thousand times smaller push, only a fixed amount more time: the logarithm.

Common mistake

Assuming a flip-flop always decides in time. If its input changes right at the clock edge, the starting imbalance can be nearly zero and the decision arbitrarily late: metastability, the reason for synchronisers.

1 µV

The traces linger near the middle much longer before diverging.

Opposite push

Same circuit, opposite sign: it falls the other way and stores the other bit.
