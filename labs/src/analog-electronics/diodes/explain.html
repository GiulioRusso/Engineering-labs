The diode and its operating point

What you are looking at

Left: a supply, a resistor and a diode in series. Top right: the diode's current against its voltage (blue) and the resistor's load line (orange); where they cross is the operating point. Bottom right: the same curve with a logarithmic current axis — a straight line.

A diode is a one-way valve for current. Forward biased, its current grows exponentially with voltage: each extra 60 mV multiplies the current by ten. That is why, in practice, the voltage across a conducting silicon diode stays close to 0.6–0.7 V whatever the current. Reverse biased, almost nothing flows.

$$I = I_S\left(e^{V/V_T} - 1\right), \qquad V_T = \frac{kT}{q} \approx 25.9\ \text{mV at } 300\ \text{K}$$
  1. The resistor says: I = (V_S − V_D)/R — a straight line (the load line).
  2. The diode says: I = I_S e^{V_D/V_T} — the curve.
  3. Both must hold at once: the crossing. Solve by trying V_D ≈ 0.7 V, then refine.

Worked example

5 V and 1 kΩ: guess V_D = 0.7 V → I = 4.3 mA. The curve at 4.3 mA gives about 0.63 V → I = 4.37 mA. Done: the answer barely depends on the guess.

Common mistakes

Connecting a diode straight across a supply with no resistor: the exponential lets the current run away. Forgetting it is temperature sensitive: about −2 mV per kelvin at fixed current.

Try it

Change R from 10 kΩ to 100 Ω: the current changes 100 times, the diode voltage by a few tenths of a volt.

A small resistor

100 Ω: about 43 mA, and V_D only rises to about 0.75 V.

Hotter

At 350 K the saturation current has grown about 1000-fold: the same current needs roughly 0.1 V less.

Reverse bias

The valve is closed: only picoamperes leak through, and the whole supply appears across the diode.

The rectifier: from AC to DC

What you are looking at

Left: the circuit (one diode, or a bridge of four). Top right: the AC input and the output across the load. Bottom right: ripple against capacitor size.

Every phone charger starts here. A diode passes only the positive half of the mains sine: a bumpy but one-way voltage. A capacitor across the load fills up at each peak and supplies the load in between, slowly drooping: what is left is DC with a small ripple. A bridge of four diodes flips the negative halves up too, so the capacitor is refilled twice as often.

$$\Delta V \approx \frac{V_\text{peak}}{f\,R\,C} \;\;(\text{half wave}), \qquad \frac{V_\text{peak}}{2fRC}\;\;(\text{full wave})$$

Worked example

10 V peak, 1 kΩ, 100 µF, 50 Hz half-wave: ΔV ≈ 9.3/(50·1000·10⁻⁴) = 1.9 V. With 1000 µF: 0.19 V.

Common mistake

Forgetting the diode drops: the DC is about one (half-wave) or two (bridge) diode drops below the AC peak — and the AC peak is √2 times the RMS rating of the transformer.

No capacitor

Pure half-sines: one-way, but nowhere near steady.

A big capacitor

1000 µF: the output barely droops between peaks.

Full-wave bridge

Two diodes conduct on each half-cycle: twice the refills, half the ripple.

The Zener regulator

What you are looking at

Left: supply, series resistor, Zener diode and load. Right: the output against the supply voltage and against the load.

A Zener is a diode made to break down at a precise reverse voltage V_Z. Above it, it conducts as much current as needed to hold V_Z. Put it across the load with a series resistor: the resistor drops the excess supply, and the Zener soaks up whatever current the load does not take.

$$I_{R_s} = \frac{V_S - V_Z}{R_s} = I_\text{load} + I_Z \quad (I_Z \ge 0)$$

Worked example

12 V, 220 Ω, 5.1 V Zener: I_Rs = 31 mA. A 1 kΩ load takes 5.1 mA, the Zener 26 mA. Below 5.1/0.031 = 163 Ω the load wants more than 31 mA: the Zener turns off and regulation is lost.

Common mistake

Choosing R_s too large: at full load nothing is left for the Zener.

Too heavy a load

150 Ω would need 34 mA at 5.1 V: more than R_s can deliver. The output sags below V_Z.

Supply too low

6 V through 220 Ω cannot lift a 1 kΩ load above 5.1 V: the Zener stays off.
