The bipolar transistor: a tap controlled by a voltage

What you are looking at

Left: an NPN transistor with a voltage V_BE between base and emitter and V_CE between collector and emitter. Top right: collector current against V_BE. Bottom right: the "output characteristics", I_C against V_CE, one curve for each V_BE.

A diode has two terminals and one rule. A transistor adds a third terminal, and with it something no resistor can do: a small voltage in one place controls a large current somewhere else. The base–emitter junction is a diode, so the current follows the same exponential law. The trick is that almost all of that current is collected by the collector, not the base.

$$I_C = I_S\,e^{V_{BE}/V_T}, \qquad I_B = \frac{I_C}{\beta}\;(\beta \approx 100\text{–}300)$$
  1. Fix V_BE: the top chart gives I_C. Each extra 59.5 mV multiplies it by 10.
  2. Now change V_CE: on the bottom chart, the current barely moves. The collector takes whatever it is given, like a current source.
  3. Below about 0.2 V the curve collapses: saturation. The transistor is now a closed switch, not an amplifier.

Worked example

I_S = 10⁻¹⁵ A, V_BE = 0.66 V: I_C = 10⁻¹⁵·e^{0.66/0.02585} = 0.12 mA. At 0.72 V: 1.2 mA. At 0.78 V: 12 mA.

Common mistakes

Thinking the base current "is amplified": the controlling quantity is V_BE; β just tells you how much base current that costs. Forgetting saturation: β only applies in the active region.

Try it

Drag V_CE from 12 V down to 0: the dot slides along a flat line, then falls off the knee.

+60 mV, ×10

From 0.66 to 0.72 V the current goes from about 0.12 to 1.2 mA. This sensitivity is why you never bias a BJT by fixing V_BE directly — see "Bias point".

Saturation

With V_CE = 0.1 V both junctions are forward biased: the collector cannot collect. In digital circuits this is the "on" state.

The MOSFET: a channel opened by the gate

What you are looking at

Left: an n-channel MOSFET between V_GS and V_DS sources. Top right: I_D against V_GS (dashed line: threshold). Bottom right: I_D against V_DS for several gate voltages; the dashed orange parabola separates triode (left) from saturation (right).

The gate is insulated: it draws no current at all. A positive gate voltage attracts electrons under it and, above the threshold V_th, they form a conducting channel between drain and source. How much it conducts depends on the "overdrive" V_ov = V_GS − V_th.

$$I_D=\begin{cases}0 & V_{ov}\le 0 \;\;(\text{cutoff})\\ k\left(V_{ov}V_{DS}-\tfrac12 V_{DS}^2\right) & V_{DS}
  1. Is V_GS above V_th? If not: off.
  2. Compare V_DS with V_ov. Smaller: triode, the channel is a voltage-controlled resistor (used as a switch).
  3. Larger: the channel "pinches off" at the drain end and the current stops growing: saturation, a current source (used as an amplifier).

Worked example

k = 2 mA/V², V_th = 1 V, V_GS = 2.5 V: V_ov = 1.5 V. At V_DS = 3 V (≥ 1.5): I_D = ½·2·1.5² = 2.25 mA. At V_DS = 0.5 V: I_D = 2(1.5·0.5 − 0.125) = 1.25 mA.

Common mistake

"Saturation" means opposite things for the two devices: for a MOSFET it is the flat, amplifying region; for a BJT it is the switched-on, collapsed region.

Below threshold

0.8 V on the gate, threshold 1 V: no channel, I_D = 0 whatever V_DS does.

Triode

V_DS = 0.4 V is well below V_ov: the I–V is nearly a straight line through the origin, i.e. a resistor of about 1/(k V_ov).

Saturation

V_DS = 4 V: the curve is flat; the slight slope is channel-length modulation (λ).

What you are looking at

Transconductance: current out per volt in

What you are looking at

Top: g_m against bias current for both devices (log–log). Bottom: g_m divided by the current — how much control each milliamp buys.

Both devices turn an input voltage into an output current. The exchange rate is the slope g_m = ∂I/∂V, and it sets the gain of every amplifier you will build (A_v = −g_m R).

$$g_{m,\text{BJT}}=\frac{I_C}{V_T}, \qquad g_{m,\text{MOS}}=k\,V_{ov}=\sqrt{2kI_D}=\frac{2I_D}{V_{ov}}$$

Worked example

1 mA: BJT 1/0.02585 = 38.7 mS. MOSFET with k = 2 mA/V²: √(2·0.002·0.001) = 2 mS — nearly 20× less for the same current.

At very small currents the square law gives way to "weak inversion", where the MOSFET becomes exponential too, with g_m ≈ I/(1.5 V_T): it approaches, but never beats, the BJT.

Common mistake

Concluding MOSFETs are worse. They draw no gate current, scale down beautifully and can be made huge (larger k) to catch up; every modern chip is CMOS.

At 10 µA

BJT 0.39 mS, MOSFET 0.20 mS: only a factor 2. At low currents a MOSFET's overdrive is small, and g_m = 2I/V_ov gets close to the BJT's I/V_T.

At 20 mA

BJT 774 mS, MOSFET 8.9 mS: almost 90×. The BJT keeps g_m/I = 38.7 V⁻¹; the MOSFET falls to √(2k/I) ≈ 0.45 V⁻¹.

The bias point and why R_E is there

What you are looking at

Left: the classic biasing circuit — base voltage V_B, collector resistor R_C to V_CC, emitter resistor R_E to ground. Top right: the load line of R_C + R_E meets the transistor curve at the bias point. Bottom right: what warming does to the current, with R_E (blue) and without (red).

The circuit imposes a straight line: V_CE = V_CC − I_C(R_C + R_E). The transistor imposes its curve. Where they cross is the quiescent ("Q") point. For the most output swing, put V_CE roughly in the middle.

  1. The base is held at V_B. The emitter sits one V_BE (≈0.7 V) lower.
  2. So the emitter current is I_E ≈ (V_B − 0.7)/R_E — set by a resistor, not by the exponential.
  3. If the transistor warms and wants more current, V_E rises, V_BE shrinks, and the current is pulled back: negative feedback.

Worked example

V_B = 2 V, R_E = 1 kΩ: I_E ≈ 1.3 mA. V_CE = 12 − 1.3·(4.7 + 1) = 4.6 V. Warm by 50 K: V_BE drops 0.1 V, I_E rises only to 1.4 mA (+8%). Without R_E the same 0.1 V would multiply the current by e^{0.1/0.0259} ≈ 48.

Common mistake

Choosing R_C so large that V_CE falls below 0.2 V: the transistor saturates and the "amplifier" amplifies nothing.

+50 K with R_E

The current moves by a few percent: the blue curve stays near 1.

No emitter resistor

Only V_BE holds the current, and V_BE drops 2 mV/K: the red curve shoots up. In a power stage this is thermal runaway.

R_C too large

12 kΩ × 1.3 mA = 15.6 V > 12 V: the load line is too steep and the Q point lands in saturation.

The small-signal idea: zoom in until the curve looks straight

What you are looking at

Top: the device curve near the bias point, with its tangent (orange) and the input swing (yellow bar). Bottom: the signal current in time — the real one (blue) and the tangent prediction g_m·v (dashed).

Transistors are not linear. But if the signal is small, the curve over that tiny stretch is almost a straight line: its slope at the bias point is g_m. Then i = g_m·v and all of linear circuit theory applies. This is what "small-signal model" means.

$$i \approx g_m\, v, \qquad \text{valid while } v \ll V_T\ (\text{BJT})\ \text{or}\ v \ll 2V_{ov}\ (\text{MOS})$$

Worked example

BJT at 1 mA, 10 mV peak: up-swing e^{10/25.85}−1 = +47%, down-swing −32%. The output is lopsided by about 20%. At 1 mV it is under 2%.

Common mistake

Applying the small-signal model to large signals. It predicts symmetric outputs that the real circuit never gives.

1 mV

Blue and dashed lie on top of each other: linear.

40 mV

The upward half grows exponentially, the downward half is squashed: strong second harmonic.

MOSFET, 200 mV

A square law bends far more gently than an exponential: hundreds of millivolts are still nearly linear.

The current mirror

What you are looking at

Left: Q1 with its collector tied to its base (a "diode-connected" transistor) fed by R from V_CC, and Q2 sharing the same base voltage. Top right: the output current against the output voltage. Bottom right: the effect of a mismatch between the two transistors.

Two identical transistors with the same V_BE carry the same current. Q1 sets its own V_BE so that it carries I_ref = (V_CC − 0.7)/R. Q2 sees that V_BE and copies the current — whatever voltage its collector is at. A resistor and a transistor have become a current source, the most-used building block in analog chips.

$$I_\text{ref}=\frac{V_{CC}-V_{BE}}{R},\qquad I_\text{out}\approx I_\text{ref}\,\frac{1+V_\text{out}/V_A}{1+V_{BE}/V_A}\cdot\frac{1}{1+2/\beta}$$

Worked example

5 V, 4.3 kΩ: I_ref ≈ 1 mA. With β = 150 the two base currents steal 1.3%. At V_out = 2.5 V the Early effect adds about 3.6%.

Common mistake

Assuming any two transistors match. Discrete parts differ by several mV in V_BE: 18 mV doubles the current. Mirrors work on chips, where neighbours are near-identical.

5 mV mismatch

e^{5/25.85} = 1.21: a 21% error from a tiny offset.

β = 20

The two base currents (2I/β) come out of I_ref: the copy is 9% short.
