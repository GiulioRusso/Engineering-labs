What you are looking at

Four views of the same pulse. Top left: the pulse in time. Top right: how much of each frequency it contains, \(|X(f)|\) — computed numerically (solid) and from the formula (dashed): they agree. Bottom left: the same spectrum in decibels against log frequency, so you can see how fast it falls (the red dashed guide). Bottom right: the phase of \(X(f)\).

The Fourier transform asks one question for every frequency \(f\): how much does the signal look like a rotating phasor \(e^{j2\pi ft}\)?

$$X(f) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)\,e^{-j2\pi f t}\,dt$$

Three rules explain almost everything you will see here:

  1. Scaling. Make a pulse \(a\) times wider and its spectrum becomes \(a\) times narrower (and \(a\) times taller): \(x(t/a) \leftrightarrow |a|\,X(af)\). Short in time means wide in frequency, always.
  2. Shift. Moving the pulse by \(t_0\) multiplies \(X(f)\) by \(e^{-j2\pi f t_0}\): the magnitude is identical, only the phase tilts — a straight line of slope \(-2\pi t_0\).
  3. Smoothness. The sharper the features in time, the slower the spectrum falls. A jump gives \(1/f\) (−20 dB per decade), a kink \(1/f^2\) (−40), a perfectly smooth pulse falls faster than any power.

Common mistakes

Thinking a shift changes the spectrum: |X| does not move at all. Reading the negative frequencies as "extra": for a real signal they are the mirror image of the positive ones, \(X(-f) = X^*(f)\). Confusing amplitude and energy: |X|² is the energy per hertz.

The rectangular pulse

$$x(t) = A\,\text{rect}\!\left(\frac{t-t_0}{\tau}\right) \;\longleftrightarrow\; X(f) = A\tau\,\text{sinc}(f\tau)\,e^{-j2\pi f t_0}, \qquad \text{sinc}(u) = \frac{\sin \pi u}{\pi u}$$

Why a sinc? At \(f = 0\) the integral is just the area, \(A\tau\). At \(f = 1/\tau\) the phasor makes exactly one full turn during the pulse: positive and negative halves cancel, a zero. At \(1.5/\tau\) one and a half turns: one half-turn is left over, a sidelobe. So the zeros sit at every multiple of \(1/\tau\).

Worked example

τ = 1 s: main lobe from −1 to +1 Hz, first sidelobe at 1.43 Hz, 13.3 dB down (21 % of the peak). τ = 0.25 s: the lobe is four times wider (±4 Hz) and four times lower.

Try it

Change τ and watch the zeros move as 1/τ. Shift t₀: the top two panels stay still while the phase panel tilts.

A wider pulse

τ = 3 s: zeros every 1/3 Hz, a peak three times higher. Energy is concentrated near zero frequency.

A narrow pulse

τ = 0.25 s: the main lobe now reaches ±4 Hz. This is why radars use short pulses for fine range resolution — and why short pulses need wide receivers.

A shifted pulse

t₀ = 2 s: |X(f)| is exactly the same; the phase now rotates by 2π every 0.5 Hz. Delay is encoded only in phase — that is how an echo's delay can be measured from its spectrum.

Twice as tall

The transform is linear: \(2x \leftrightarrow 2X\). Every value doubles, the shape does not change, and the dB plot (normalised) does not move at all.

The triangular pulse

$$x(t) = A\,\max\!\left(0, 1-\frac{|t-t_0|}{\tau}\right) \;\longleftrightarrow\; X(f) = A\tau\,\text{sinc}^2(f\tau)\,e^{-j2\pi f t_0}$$

A triangle is a rectangle convolved with itself (slide one rectangle across another and measure the overlap: it grows, peaks, shrinks linearly). Convolution in time is multiplication in frequency, so the spectrum is the sinc squared: always positive, and its sidelobes fall twice as fast in dB.

Worked example

A triangle of half-width 0.5 s has the same total width as the 1 s rectangle. First sidelobe: −26.5 dB instead of −13.3 dB. In the dB panel the slope is −40 dB/decade: the triangle has no jumps, only kinks.

Try it

Look at the bottom-left panel and compare with the rectangle: same width, much less energy far from zero.

Same width as the rectangle

Half-width 0.5 s, total 1 s: zeros at multiples of 2 Hz, sidelobes squared. Smoother edges buy lower leakage — the principle behind every window function.

A wider triangle

Half-width 1.5 s: zeros every 0.67 Hz. The scaling rule works for every shape.

The Gaussian pulse

$$x(t) = A\,e^{-(t-t_0)^2/2\sigma^2} \;\longleftrightarrow\; X(f) = A\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}\,e^{-2\pi^2\sigma^2 f^2}\,e^{-j2\pi f t_0}$$

The Gaussian transforms into a Gaussian. Its time spread is \(\sigma_t = \sigma\) and its frequency spread \(\sigma_f = 1/(2\pi\sigma)\): their product is \(1/4\pi\), the smallest possible for any signal. It has no jumps, no kinks, no sidelobes: in dB it falls as a parabola, faster than any power of f.

Worked example

σ = 0.5 s → σ_f = 0.318 Hz. Halve σ to 0.25 s → σ_f = 0.637 Hz. The product stays 0.0796 whatever σ you choose.

Common mistake

Mixing up σ (of the amplitude) with the width at half height: FWHM = 2.355 σ.

Try it

Move σ and read the HUD: σt·σf stays exactly at the bound.

A narrow Gaussian

σ = 0.15 s: the spectrum widens to σ_f ≈ 1.06 Hz but stays a clean Gaussian with no ripples.

A wide Gaussian

σ = 1.5 s: the spectrum is a thin spike near zero, σ_f ≈ 0.1 Hz.

The sinc

$$x(t) = A\,\text{sinc}\big(2W(t-t_0)\big) \;\longleftrightarrow\; X(f) = \frac{A}{2W}\,\text{rect}\!\left(\frac{f}{2W}\right)$$

This is the rectangle turned around: a hard-edged band in frequency gives an oscillating pulse in time. It is the impulse response of the ideal low-pass filter, and the interpolating function of the sampling theorem.

Worked example

W = 1 Hz: zero crossings every 0.5 s (= 1/2W) and 100 % of the energy below 1 Hz. The price: the sinc never ends — its tails decay only as 1/t. Any real implementation must cut them, and the cliff in the spectrum then gets ripples.

Common mistake

Believing a perfectly band-limited signal can also be time-limited. It cannot: a signal cannot be finite in both domains.

Try it

Increase W: the zero crossings crowd together, the flat band widens.

A wider band

W = 3 Hz: zero crossings every 1/6 s. More bandwidth, sharper features in time.

A narrow band

W = 0.3 Hz: the sinc spreads over many seconds. A narrow filter rings for a long time.

The one-sided exponential

$$x(t) = A\,e^{-\lambda (t-t_0)}\,u(t-t_0) \;\longleftrightarrow\; X(f) = \frac{A\,e^{-j2\pi f t_0}}{\lambda + j2\pi f}, \qquad |X(f)| = \frac{A}{\sqrt{\lambda^2 + (2\pi f)^2}}$$

This is the impulse response of every first-order system (an RC circuit, a cooling cup of coffee). Its spectrum is flat up to \(f_{3\text{dB}} = \lambda/2\pi\), then falls at −20 dB per decade because of the jump at \(t_0\). Unlike the symmetric pulses, its phase is not linear: it bends from 0 to −90°.

Worked example

λ = 1/s: f₃dB = 0.16 Hz. A 1 kΩ, 1 µF RC circuit has λ = 1000/s → 159 Hz.

Try it

Increase λ: a faster decay pushes the corner to higher frequency.

A fast decay

λ = 5/s: corner at 0.8 Hz. Fast response = wide bandwidth.

A slow decay

λ = 0.4/s: corner at 0.064 Hz. A slow system only passes slow signals.

The chirp

$$x(t) = A\,\text{rect}\!\left(\frac{t}{T}\right)\cos\!\big(2\pi(\bar f\,t + \tfrac{\mu}{2}t^2)\big), \qquad f_i(t) = \bar f + \mu t, \quad \mu = \frac{f_1-f_0}{T}$$

The instantaneous frequency slides linearly from \(f_0\) to \(f_1\). Each frequency is visited for a moment, so the spectrum is roughly flat across the sweep: bandwidth \(B = |f_1 - f_0|\), independent of the duration. That breaks the rule "long pulse = narrow spectrum": a chirp can be both long and wide, with \(TB \gg 1\).

Worked example

T = 4 s, 1 → 5 Hz: B = 4 Hz, TB = 16. A plain pulse of 4 s would have only 0.25 Hz. After a matched filter the chirp compresses to about 1/B = 0.25 s — sixteen times shorter. This is pulse compression, used by every modern radar.

Common mistake

Expecting the peaks at ±f₀ and ±f₁ only. The cosine contains both \(e^{+j}\) and \(e^{-j}\): you see a flat band on each side, mirror images.

Try it

Shorten T: when TB drops near 1 the band stops being flat and the chirp behaves like an ordinary pulse.

A wide sweep

1 → 7 Hz: B = 6 Hz, TB = 24. Wider band, flatter top, finer compressed peak.

Too short

T = 0.6 s: TB = 2.4. There is not enough time to sweep: ripples (Fresnel) dominate and the spectrum looks like a sinc again.

Sweeping down

5 → 1 Hz: the magnitude spectrum is the same — only the phase (the order in which the frequencies arrive) differs. Up and down chirps are used in pairs to separate range from Doppler.

The time–bandwidth product

What you are looking at

Five pulses scaled to the same width τ (top left) and their spectra (top right). Bottom left: σt·σf of each, divided by the bound 1/4π. Bottom right: each pulse as a dot on the (σt, σf) plane; the red curve is the bound — nothing can sit below it.

Measure the "width" of a signal by the standard deviation of its energy, in each domain:

$$\sigma_t^2 = \frac{\int t^2 |x|^2\,dt}{\int |x|^2\,dt}, \qquad \sigma_f^2 = \frac{\int f^2 |X|^2\,df}{\int |X|^2\,df}, \qquad \sigma_t\,\sigma_f \;\ge\; \frac{1}{4\pi}$$

This is the Heisenberg–Gabor uncertainty principle. It is not about measuring errors: it is a property of waves. To be sure when something happened you need many frequencies; to be sure of the frequency you need to listen for a long time.

Worked example

Gaussian: exactly 1. Triangle: √1.2 ≈ 1.10. Rectangle: its σf diverges because |X|² falls only as 1/f² — the number you see grows with the frequency grid, the jumps never die out.

Common mistake

Thinking a cleverer pulse could beat the bound. A chirp has TB ≫ 1 by design: it is far above the bound, which is what lets compression trade time for bandwidth.

Try it

Move the common width: all dots slide along hyperbolas, the bars do not change at all. The ratio is a property of the shape, not of the size.

All narrower

τ = 0.4 s: every spectrum widens by the same factor. The dots slide up-left along the curve; the ratios are unchanged.

All wider

τ = 2.5 s: every spectrum shrinks. Only the shape decides how close to the bound a pulse can get.
