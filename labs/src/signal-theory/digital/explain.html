BPSK: one bit per symbol

What you are looking at

Top: the bits as a ±1 (NRZ) stream. Middle: the carrier multiplied by that stream, plus noise. Bottom: what the receiver computes for each bit — multiply by its own carrier and integrate; a red dot marks a wrong decision. Right: every received bit as a point in the I/Q plane; orange dots are the two ideal points with their bit labels.
$$s(t) = \pm\cos(2\pi f_c t), \qquad P_b = \tfrac12\,\text{erfc}\sqrt{E_b/N_0}$$
  1. Modulate: bit 0 → +cos, bit 1 → −cos. A change of bit is a jump of 180° in phase.
  2. Correlate: multiply the received signal by cos and integrate over one bit: ±1 plus a Gaussian noise term.
  3. Decide: positive → 0, negative → 1. An error happens when noise pushes the sample across zero.

\(E_b/N_0\) — energy per bit over noise density — is the fair way to compare schemes: it does not depend on bandwidth or bit rate.

Worked example

At 9.6 dB (E_b/N_0 = 9.1), BER = ½ erfc(3.02) ≈ 10⁻⁵: one wrong bit in 100 000. At 0 dB, BER ≈ 8 %.

Common mistakes

Confusing SNR and E_b/N_0 (they differ by the ratio of bit rate to bandwidth). Thinking the receiver looks at the waveform's shape: it only needs the correlator's sign.

Try it

Lower Eb/N0 until the cloud of points straddles the vertical axis — the red dots appear exactly then.

15 dB

Two tight clusters far from the decision line: practically no errors (BER ≈ 10⁻¹⁵).

0 dB

The clusters merge: about one bit in thirteen is wrong.

QPSK: two BPSK streams at once

What you are looking at

Top two rows: the I and Q streams (one bit each per symbol). Bottom: the resulting carrier phase per symbol. Right: the four-point constellation with Gray-coded labels and the received points.
$$s(t) = I\cos(2\pi f_c t) - Q\sin(2\pi f_c t), \qquad I, Q \in \{\pm 1/\sqrt 2\}$$

Cosine and sine are orthogonal: a receiver that correlates with each separately sees two independent BPSK channels in the same bandwidth. Twice the bits per hertz, and — per bit — the same error rate as BPSK.

Worked example

Bits 1, 0 → I = −, Q = + → phase 135°. Gray labels: 00 at 45°, 01 at 135°, 11 at 225°, 10 at 315° — neighbours differ in one bit.

Common mistake

Thinking four points must be "worse" than two at the same Eb/N0. Each axis carries one bit with half the energy, but the distance per bit is the same.

Try it

Drop to 3 dB: most errors move a point to an adjacent quadrant, flipping only one of its two bits.

3 dB

BER ≈ 2 % — the same as BPSK at 3 dB, while carrying twice the data.

M-QAM: amplitude and phase

What you are looking at

Left: the ideal constellation (Gray labels) with the decision boundaries. Right: the received symbols — blue decided correctly, red in the wrong box.

A square grid of M points: each axis takes √M levels. More points carry more bits per symbol (log₂M), but at the same average power they are closer together, so noise causes errors sooner.

$$d_\text{min} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\tfrac23(M-1)}}, \qquad P_b \approx \frac{2(1 - 1/\sqrt M)}{\log_2 M}\,\text{erfc}\sqrt{\frac{3\log_2 M}{2(M-1)}\frac{E_b}{N_0}}$$

Worked example

16-QAM: d = 0.63; 64-QAM: d = 0.31; 256-QAM: d = 0.15. Halving the distance costs about 6 dB; adding the extra bits per symbol gives some back — net about 4 dB per step.

Common mistake

Thinking the corner points are "stronger": they have more power but errors depend on distance to neighbours, which is the same everywhere; inner points have more neighbours and err more often.

Try it

Pick 64-QAM and raise Eb/N0 from 10 to 18 dB: the red points disappear first from the corners.

16-QAM at 10 dB

Clusters are separated; BER around 10⁻³. Used by 4G for good channels.

64-QAM at 14 dB

The clusters touch: BER about 10⁻³ — 6 bits per symbol need more power.

256-QAM

8 bits per symbol: even at 20 dB some errors remain. Wi-Fi and 5G use it only when the link is excellent.

BER curves

What you are looking at

Bit error rate (log scale) against Eb/N0 for six schemes: lines from theory, dots from actually sending random bits through noise in your browser.

Every curve has the same shape: almost flat at low SNR, then a "waterfall" where each extra dB cuts the error rate tenfold or more. Read them horizontally: how much Eb/N0 does each scheme need for a target BER?

Worked example

BER 10⁻⁵: BPSK/QPSK need 9.6 dB, 16-QAM 13.4, 64-QAM 17.8. Moving from QPSK (2 bits) to 64-QAM (6 bits) triples the throughput for about 8 dB — 6 times the power per bit.

Common mistake

Reading the curves vertically at one Eb/N0 and concluding "BPSK is always better". It is more robust; QAM is more efficient. Real systems switch between them as the channel changes (adaptive modulation).

Try it

Highlight each scheme and compare its dots with its line: the Monte Carlo agrees down to where errors become too rare to count.

The cost of bits

At 10 dB, BPSK makes about 4 errors per million bits, 64-QAM about 2 per hundred.

Pulse shaping and the eye diagram

What you are looking at

Top left: the raised-cosine pulse used for each symbol — note it is exactly zero at every other symbol instant (dots). Top right: its spectrum. Bottom: the eye diagram — many two-symbol stretches of the received signal overlaid — without noise (left) and with noise (right).

Square pulses waste bandwidth (their spectrum is a sinc). Smooth pulses use less, but they overlap their neighbours. The trick (Nyquist) is a pulse that overlaps — but passes through zero at every other sampling instant. Then, sampled at the right moment, each symbol sees no interference (no ISI).

$$p(t) = \text{sinc}(t/T)\,\frac{\cos(\pi\alpha t/T)}{1 - (2\alpha t/T)^2}, \qquad B = \frac{1+\alpha}{2T}$$
  1. The eye's vertical opening is the noise margin at the sampling instant.
  2. Its horizontal opening is the tolerance to timing errors.
  3. α trades bandwidth for a wider eye and faster-decaying tails.

Worked example

1 Msymbol/s with α = 0.35: bandwidth 675 kHz (one-sided). With α = 0 it would be 500 kHz, but any timing error would let the slowly decaying sinc tails pile up.

Common mistake

Sampling at the eye's edge: there all the neighbouring pulses contribute, and the margin is gone.

Try it

Move α from 0 to 1 and watch the eye open horizontally; then lower Eb/N0 until it closes.

α = 0

The sinc pulse: minimum bandwidth, but the eye is narrow — tiny timing errors cause large ISI.

α = 1

Double the minimum bandwidth, but a wide-open eye and short pulses.

4-PAM

Four levels stack three eyes; each is a third as tall, so noise closes them sooner — the price of two bits per symbol.

A closing eye

At 4 dB the noise fills the eye: decisions at its centre become unreliable.

The Shannon limit

What you are looking at

Spectral efficiency η (bits per second per hertz) against the Eb/N0 needed. The red curve is Shannon's limit: no system, however clever, works reliably to its left. Dots: the uncoded schemes at the chosen BER; dashed lines: their gap to the limit.
$$C = B\log_2(1 + S/N) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{E_b}{N_0}\bigg|_{\min} = \frac{2^\eta - 1}{\eta} \;\xrightarrow{\eta\to 0}\; \ln 2 = -1.59\ \text{dB}$$

Shannon proved that error-free communication is possible at any rate below C — not with a cleverer constellation, but with long error-correcting codes. Uncoded schemes sit 8–10 dB to the right of the limit; modern codes (turbo, LDPC, polar) get within a fraction of a dB.

Worked example

η = 2 (QPSK): the limit is 1.76 dB, uncoded QPSK needs 9.6 dB at BER 10⁻⁵ — a gap of 7.8 dB that coding recovers.

Common mistake

Thinking the limit forbids low-power links entirely: it allows any Eb/N0 above −1.59 dB, at a low enough rate. Deep-space probes live near that corner.

Try it

Tighten the target BER: the dots move right, the limit does not.

BER 10⁻⁹

Every uncoded scheme needs about 3 dB more than at 10⁻⁵; Shannon's curve, defined for arbitrarily small error, stays where it is.
