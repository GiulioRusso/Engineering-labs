Wavefronts from delayed elements

What you are looking at

Left: the field radiated by a row of elements (orange dots), each sending out circular ripples. Right: the delay applied to each element.

If every element fires at the same instant, the ripples line up parallel to the array and the wave goes straight ahead (broadside). Delay each element a little more than its neighbour and the line along which the ripples add up tilts: the beam turns, with no moving parts.

$$\Delta\tau = \frac{d\sin\theta_0}{c}, \qquad \Delta\varphi = -k\,d\sin\theta_0$$

Worked example

d = λ/2, θ₀ = 30°: each element lags its neighbour by a quarter period (90°). With 8 elements the last fires 1.75 periods after the first.

Common mistake

Thinking the elements point anywhere. Each one radiates broadly; the direction comes only from where their waves add in step.

Try it

Slide θ₀ and watch the front rotate.

No delay

All elements in phase: the fronts are parallel to the array and the beam is broadside.

50°

Large delays: the front tilts strongly and the beam (in the pattern below) points to 50°, wider than at broadside.

Spacing shapes the beam

What you are looking at

Left: the field of the array. Right: its radiation pattern in dB (polar, broadside up).
$$|AF(\theta)| = \left|\frac{\sin(N\psi/2)}{N\sin(\psi/2)}\right|, \qquad \psi = kd(\sin\theta - \sin\theta_0), \qquad \text{BW} \approx \frac{0.886\,\lambda}{Nd}$$

The beam width is set by the total aperture N·d: a bigger array makes a narrower beam. Spreading the elements gives a bigger aperture for free — until the spacing exceeds λ/2 or so, when the pattern repeats inside real space: grating lobes.

Worked example

8 elements at λ/2: aperture 4 λ, beamwidth ≈ 12.7°. At λ/4: 2 λ, 25°. At 1.2 λ: 9.6 λ, 5.3° — plus grating lobes at ±56°.

Common mistake

Adding elements without checking the spacing: the beam narrows only with aperture, and only if no grating lobes appear.

d = λ/4

Small aperture: a wide beam. No grating lobes possible.

d = 1.2 λ

The narrowest main beam — and two more full-strength beams. Energy is sent in three directions.

Phase shifters vs true time delay

What you are looking at

Top: patterns at three frequencies (f₀/r, f₀, r·f₀) steered with phase shifters (left) and with true delays (right). Bottom: beam direction against frequency.

A phase shifter applies a fixed phase, correct for one frequency. At another frequency the same phase corresponds to a different delay, and the beam points elsewhere — it squints. A true delay line delays every frequency by the same time, so it steers all frequencies to the same angle.

$$\sin\theta(f) = \frac{f_0}{f}\sin\theta_0 \quad\text{(phase shifters)}$$

Worked example

θ₀ = 40°, f = 1.2 f₀: the beam moves to 32.4°. 16 elements have a beam about 8.6° wide at 40°: a wideband radar pulse would be smeared across the beam.

Common mistake

Using phase steering for wideband signals (chirps, UWB). Large arrays need true-time-delay, at least at subarray level.

±30 %

At 1.3 f₀ the phase-steered beam is at 29.6°, at f₀/1.3 at 56.6°: a 27° spread. The time-delay beam stays at 40°.

The near-field beam

What you are looking at

Left: time-averaged intensity in front of the array (scaled by r so the far field looks uniform), with the Fraunhofer distance 2D²/λ. Right: cross-sections at three distances.

Near the array the beam is as wide as the aperture, with ripples (each element's wave arrives with a different path). Only beyond roughly 2D²/λ do the waves arrive almost parallel, the cross-section settles into the far-field pattern, and the beam spreads at a fixed angle.

Worked example

16 elements at λ/2: D = 8 λ, far field from 128 λ. A 10 GHz radar 1 m wide: 2·1/0.03 = 67 m.

Common mistake

Using the far-field beamwidth to predict the spot size a few metres from a large array.

32 elements

D = 16 λ, far field from 512 λ: much further than the view. All you see is near field: a collimated beam.

Grating lobes

What you are looking at

Left: the plane of spacing against steering angle; bright where a grating lobe is visible. The green curve is the limit. Right: the pattern, with predicted grating lobes as red lines.
$$\sin\theta_\text{GL} = \sin\theta_0 + m\frac{\lambda}{d}, \quad m = \pm 1, \pm 2, \ldots \qquad d < \frac{\lambda}{1 + |\sin\theta_{\max}|}$$

The array factor repeats every time ψ grows by 2π. With wide spacing, those repeats land at real angles. Steering moves all of them: an array clean at broadside can grow a grating lobe when steered.

Worked example

d = 0.6 λ steered to 50°: sin θ_GL = 0.766 − 1.667 = −0.90 → −64°. To scan ±60° you need d < 0.536 λ.

Common mistake

Checking grating lobes only at broadside.

d = λ/2

Safe for any steering up to 90°: the next repeat is always just outside.

A lobe enters

d = 0.7 λ at 40°: sin θ = 0.643 − 1.43 = −0.79 → a full beam at −52°.

Scanning and its cost

What you are looking at

Left: one pattern per steering angle (columns), including the element's own cos-like pattern. Right: beamwidth and gain against steering.

Seen from an angle θ₀, the array looks shorter: its projected aperture is N·d·cos θ₀. So the beam widens as 1/cos θ₀, and the gain falls — further reduced because each element radiates less sideways.

Worked example

At 60°, cos θ₀ = 0.5: twice the beamwidth, and about 6 dB less gain. Practical arrays scan ±60° at most; for full coverage radars use three or four faces.

60°

The beam is twice as wide as at broadside, and the gain is down by roughly 6 dB.

The planar array

What you are looking at

Left: the array factor of an Nx × Ny grid in direction-cosine space (u, v); the green circle is the visible region |u, v| ≤ 1. Right: cuts through the beam.
$$AF(u,v) = AF_x(u - u_0)\cdot AF_y(v - v_0), \qquad u = \sin\theta\cos\phi,\; v = \sin\theta\sin\phi$$

In (u, v) coordinates steering is just a shift of the whole pattern, and the pattern repeats every λ/d in each direction. Whatever repeat falls inside the circle is a grating lobe.

Worked example

d = 0.8 λ, steered to u₀ = 0.6: the next repeat is at u = 0.6 − 1.25 = −0.65 — inside the circle.

Common mistake

Reading (u, v) as angles: they are sines. The rim u² + v² = 1 is the horizon.

A lobe through the rim

The replica enters the visible circle from the opposite side as the beam is steered.

Amplitude tapering

What you are looking at

Top: the amplitude of each element. Bottom: the resulting pattern against the uniform one.

A uniform array has −13.3 dB sidelobes — the sinc of its rectangular aperture. Weighting the edge elements less smooths the aperture's edges and lowers the sidelobes, at the cost of a wider beam and slightly less gain (the edges are under-used).

  1. Hamming: about −42 dB, beam 1.5× wider.
  2. Dolph–Chebyshev: all sidelobes exactly at the design level (here −40 dB) — the narrowest beam for that level.

Common mistake

Expecting low sidelobes for free: every taper trades beamwidth and efficiency for sidelobe level.

Chebyshev

Equal-ripple sidelobes at −40 dB: the optimum compromise for a fixed level.

Hamming

Sidelobes near −42 dB, falling further away from the beam.

Null steering

What you are looking at

Top: the complex weights. Bottom: the pattern with nulls (blue) against plain steering (grey); red lines mark the interferers.

An array can also choose where not to listen. Take the steering vector and remove from it its component along the steering vector of each interferer: the result is orthogonal to the interferers (a perfect null there) and as close as possible to the original beam.

$$\mathbf w = \mathbf a_0 - \mathbf A(\mathbf A^H\mathbf A)^{-1}\mathbf A^H\mathbf a_0, \qquad \mathbf w^H\mathbf a(\theta_j) = 0$$

Worked example

12 elements, interferers at 40° and −25°: both nulled below −100 dB, main-beam gain almost unchanged. An interferer at 12°, inside the first sidelobe, costs more gain and shifts the beam.

Common mistake

Putting a null inside the main beam: you cannot both receive and reject the same direction.

Two nulls

Two interferers suppressed by more than 100 dB with almost no loss on the wanted direction.

A close interferer

A null at 12°, near the edge of the main beam, distorts it and costs gain.
