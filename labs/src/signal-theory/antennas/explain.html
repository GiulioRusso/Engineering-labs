The link budget

In free space the power spreads over a sphere, so it falls as \(1/d^2\): 6 dB per doubling of distance. In dB the budget is a simple sum: transmitted power plus both antenna gains, minus the free-space path loss. The link works if the result is above the receiver's sensitivity.

$$P_r = P_t + G_t + G_r - 20\log_{10}\frac{4\pi d}{\lambda} \quad \text{(dB, dBm, dBi)}$$

Twice as far

200 m instead of 100 m: 6 dB less, a quarter of the power.

Out of range

At 3 km the received power is below the sensitivity: more power, more antenna gain, or a lower frequency would be needed.

Higher frequency

With the same antenna gains, 5 GHz loses 6.4 dB more than 2.4 GHz (\(20\log\frac{5}{2.4}\)): the antennas' effective area shrinks as \(\lambda^2\).

A satellite link

36 000 km at 12 GHz is over 200 dB of loss. Dishes with 35–40 dBi of gain on both ends and a sensitive receiver close the link.

The dipole

A straight wire fed in the middle radiates most at right angles to itself and nothing along its axis. The polar plot shows the field strength in dB in a plane containing the wire (vertical, orange); the 3D view revolves it into a doughnut.

$$F(\theta) = \frac{\cos\!\left(\frac{kL}{2}\cos\theta\right) - \cos\frac{kL}{2}}{\sin\theta}, \qquad D = \frac{2}{\int_0^\pi F^2\sin\theta\,d\theta}$$

Short dipole

Much shorter than λ: the pattern is \(\sin\theta\), directivity 1.5 (1.76 dBi). Short antennas are also hard to feed efficiently.

Full-wave

The beam narrows and the directivity rises to about 2.4.

Side lobes

Longer than λ, the currents along the wire are no longer all in phase: the main beam splits and side lobes appear.

Phased arrays

Many small antennas in a row, each fed with a slightly delayed signal. The waves add up in one direction and cancel elsewhere; changing the delays steers the beam with no moving parts. The beam narrows as the array grows.

$$AF(\theta) = \frac{\sin(N\psi/2)}{N\sin(\psi/2)}, \quad \psi = 2\pi\frac{d}{\lambda}(\sin\theta - \sin\theta_0), \qquad \theta_{3\text{dB}} \approx \frac{0.886\,\lambda}{N d\cos\theta_0}$$

Two elements

Two sources half a wavelength apart: a broad beam forward and a null along the line of the array.

Sixteen elements

Eight times the aperture of two: a beam about 6° wide, with side lobes at −13 dB.

Steering

A phase step of \(-360° \cdot \frac{d}{\lambda}\sin\theta_0\) between neighbours points the beam to 30°. It widens a little as it is steered away from broadside.

Grating lobes

With elements a whole wavelength apart, other directions also see the waves in phase: copies of the main beam appear. Spacing must stay below \(\lambda/(1 + |\sin\theta_0|)\).

Polarization

The tip of the electric field traces a line, a circle or an ellipse in the plane across the wave, depending on the amplitudes and the phase between its two components. A polarizer (red) passes only the component along it.

$$\mathbf E = \big(\cos\omega t,\ a\cos(\omega t + \delta)\big), \qquad P_\text{out} = P_\text{in}\cos^2\alpha \text{ (linear: Malus)}$$

Linear

In phase (\(\delta = 0\)): the field swings along a fixed line at 45°.

Circular

Equal amplitudes, 90° apart: the field turns at constant length. Half the power passes any polarizer; GPS and satellite links use it so the receiver's orientation does not matter.

Elliptical

The general case: an ellipse, described by its axial ratio and tilt.

Crossed

Horizontal field, vertical polarizer: nothing passes. Two antennas with crossed linear polarizations barely talk to each other.

Waveguide modes

A hollow metal pipe carries microwaves only in certain field patterns (modes). Each has a cut-off frequency below which it cannot propagate. The arrows show the transverse electric field across the guide.

$$f_{c,mn} = \frac{c}{2}\sqrt{\left(\frac{m}{a}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{n}{b}\right)^2}, \qquad \lambda_g = \frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{1 - (f_c/f)^2}}$$

Below cut-off

At 6 GHz even the lowest mode (TE₁₀, 6.56 GHz in WR-90) cannot propagate: the field decays exponentially along the guide.

TE₂₀

Two half-waves across the wide side; cut-off 13.1 GHz. Above it, the guide is no longer single-mode.

TE₁₁

Field varying across both sides; cut-off 16.2 GHz.

TM₁₁

The lowest TM mode: same cut-off as TE₁₁, but with an electric field along the guide too (here only its transverse part is drawn).
