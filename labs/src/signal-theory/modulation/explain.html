Why a carrier?

What you are looking at

Top: the message (a slow wave — think of a voice). Middle: a much faster carrier. Bottom: the carrier with the message written into its amplitude — the dashed envelope is the message.

Three problems make sending the message directly impossible:

  1. Antennas. An antenna radiates well when it is about a quarter wavelength long. For 1 kHz, λ = 300 km: you would need a 75 km antenna. At 100 MHz, 75 cm.
  2. Sharing. Every voice occupies the same 0–4 kHz. Moving each one to its own carrier frequency puts them side by side: that is what tuning a radio selects.
  3. Propagation. Different frequency bands travel differently (ground wave, sky wave, line of sight); the carrier chooses the band.

The carrier must be much faster than the message (fc ≫ fm), so that it oscillates many times during each message cycle and can follow its shape.

Try it

Bring the carrier down to 4× the message: the envelope is barely recognisable.

A slow carrier

fc = 32 Hz, fm = 8 Hz: only four carrier cycles per message cycle. The envelope is sampled too coarsely to follow the message smoothly.

Amplitude modulation (AM)

What you are looking at

Top left: the AM signal and its envelope (dashed). Top right: its spectrum — the carrier line and two sidebands. Bottom left: what a simple envelope detector (diode + RC) recovers. Bottom right: how the transmitted power is split between carrier and sidebands.
$$s(t) = \big[1 + \mu\,m(t)\big]\cos(2\pi f_c t), \qquad \cos a\cos b = \tfrac12\big[\cos(a-b) + \cos(a+b)\big]$$

With a single tone \(m = \cos 2\pi f_m t\) the product splits into three lines: the carrier at fc, and two sidebands at fc − fm and fc + fm, each of amplitude μ/2. Every frequency of the message is copied twice, mirrored around the carrier: bandwidth 2fm.

Worked example

fc = 100 Hz, fm = 8 Hz, μ = 0.8: lines at 92 Hz (0.4), 100 Hz (1), 108 Hz (0.4). Power: carrier 0.5, sidebands 2 × 0.4²/2 = 0.16. Only 24 % of the power carries the message. Even at μ = 1 the maximum is 33 %.

Common mistakes

Pushing μ above 1 to get "more signal": the envelope then crosses zero and the detector folds it back — distortion. Thinking the carrier carries information: it is the same whatever the message; it is there only so the cheap envelope detector works. DSB-SC removes it, but then the receiver needs a synchronised oscillator.

Try it

Slide μ from 0 to 1.5 and watch the envelope, the sidebands and the detector output.

μ = 1

The envelope just touches zero at the message's minimum: the deepest modulation without distortion.

Overmodulation

μ = 1.4: 1 + μm goes negative, the carrier phase flips, and the envelope detector (which sees only |1 + μm|) outputs a folded, distorted message.

Double sideband, suppressed carrier

Only the two sidebands: all the power is useful. But the envelope is |m(t)|, not m(t), so an envelope detector fails — the receiver must multiply by an exact copy of the carrier (coherent detection).

A square message

The square wave has harmonics at fm, 3fm, 5fm…; each gets its own pair of sidebands. The modulated spectrum is the message spectrum, copied on both sides of the carrier.

Frequency modulation (FM)

What you are looking at

Top left: the FM signal — constant amplitude, its cycles squeezed and stretched by the message (dashed). Top right: its instantaneous frequency. Bottom left: the spectrum, with the predicted line heights |Jₙ(β)| (dots) and Carson's bandwidth (dashed). Bottom right: the Bessel functions that set those heights, against β.
$$f_i(t) = f_c + \Delta f\,m(t), \qquad s(t) = \cos\!\Big(2\pi f_c t + 2\pi\Delta f\!\int_0^t m\,d\tau\Big), \qquad \beta = \frac{\Delta f}{f_m}$$

The message sets the frequency; the phase is its integral. For a tone message the spectrum is a line at fc and an infinite set of sidebands at fc ± n·fm with amplitudes \(J_n(\beta)\). In practice only about β + 1 pairs matter:

$$B \approx 2(\Delta f + f_m) = 2f_m(\beta + 1) \quad\text{(Carson)}$$

Worked example

Broadcast FM: Δf = 75 kHz, fm = 15 kHz → β = 5, B ≈ 180 kHz (stations are 200 kHz apart). Here Δf = 20, fm = 8: β = 2.5, B ≈ 56 Hz.

Common mistakes

Thinking the bandwidth is 2Δf: the sidebands extend a bit beyond the swing (that is the + fm). Thinking the carrier line is always there: at β = 2.405 (the first zero of J₀) it vanishes entirely — all the power is in the sidebands.

Try it

Raise Δf slowly and follow the orange line on the Bessel plot: each time it crosses a zero of Jₙ, that spectral line disappears.

Narrowband FM

β = 0.25: J₀ ≈ 0.98, J₁ ≈ 0.12, the rest negligible. One pair of sidebands, like AM — but with the lower one inverted in phase, so the amplitude stays constant.

The carrier vanishes

β = 2.405: J₀ = 0. This "carrier null" is how engineers calibrate FM deviation with a spectrum analyser.

Wideband FM

β = 6: about seven significant sideband pairs; Carson predicts 2·8·7 = 112 Hz. More bandwidth, much better noise rejection after demodulation.

Phase modulation vs frequency modulation

What you are looking at

Top: the same message sent by PM (left) and FM (right). Bottom left: the two instantaneous frequencies. Bottom right: the two spectra.
$$s_{PM} = \cos\big(2\pi f_c t + k_p\,m(t)\big), \qquad f_i^{PM} = f_c + \frac{k_p}{2\pi}\frac{dm}{dt}$$

In PM the message sets the phase directly, so the frequency follows the message's rate of change. PM of m is the same as FM of dm/dt — they are two faces of angle modulation.

Worked example

kp = 2.5 rad, tone fm = 8 Hz: PM frequency swing = kp·fm = 20 Hz, the same as FM with Δf = 20. Double fm: PM swings 40 Hz, FM still 20.

Common mistake

Thinking you can tell PM and FM apart from a single tone: you cannot — a sine's derivative is a shifted sine. The difference shows with a richer message (try the square wave) or when fm changes.

Try it

Select the square message: PM jumps its phase at the edges (spikes in fᵢ), FM jumps its frequency (steps in fᵢ). BPSK is exactly PM with a square message.

A faster message

fm = 20 Hz: the PM swing grows to 50 Hz and its spectrum widens, while FM keeps the same 20 Hz swing.

A square message

PM holds two phases and jumps between them; FM holds two frequencies. The spectra differ completely.

AM, FM and PM side by side

What you are looking at

Each row is one modulation of the same message: the signal on the left, its spectrum on the right.
  1. AM changes the amplitude: narrow band (2fm), simple detector, but amplitude noise goes straight into the message.
  2. FM changes the frequency: wider band, a limiter discards amplitude noise, much better audio quality.
  3. PM changes the phase: the basis of digital modulation (PSK, QAM) and of GPS, Wi-Fi, 4G/5G.

Common mistake

Thinking FM is "better" in every way: it costs several times the bandwidth. Every choice is a trade between bandwidth, power and receiver complexity.

A triangle message

FM turns the triangle's straight ramps into steady frequency sweeps (chirps); PM turns them into two fixed frequencies, because the derivative of a triangle is a square.
