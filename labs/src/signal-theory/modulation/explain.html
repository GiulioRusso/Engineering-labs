AM and FM

A message (orange) rides on a fast carrier. AM changes the carrier's amplitude: the spectrum is the carrier plus two sidebands at \(f_c \pm f_m\). FM changes its frequency: the spectrum has many sidebands at \(f_c + n f_m\) with amplitudes \(J_n(\beta)\).

$$s_\text{AM} = \big(1 + m\cos 2\pi f_m t\big)\cos 2\pi f_c t, \qquad s_\text{FM} = \cos\!\big(2\pi f_c t + \beta\sin 2\pi f_m t\big)$$

m = 1

The envelope just touches zero. Even so, only a third of the power is in the sidebands that carry the message.

Over-modulation

With \(m > 1\) the envelope \(1 + m\cos\) goes negative and folds over: a simple envelope detector would distort the message.

FM, β = 1

The amplitude never changes. Power moves from the carrier (\(J_0^2\)) into a few sidebands; \(\sum J_n^2 = 1\).

Wide-band FM

Large \(\beta\) spreads the power over many sidebands. Carson's rule: about 98 % of it lies within \(2(\beta + 1)f_m\). Broadcast FM trades bandwidth for noise immunity.

No carrier

At \(\beta = 2.405\), the first zero of \(J_0\), the carrier line vanishes completely: all the power is in the sidebands. Engineers use this to calibrate FM deviation.

Digital constellations

Each symbol is a point in the I–Q plane carrying \(\log_2 M\) bits. Noise spreads the received points into clouds (blue); the receiver picks the nearest ideal point (orange). More points per symbol means more bits but closer neighbours, so more \(E_b/N_0\) is needed for the same bit error rate (right).

$$P_b^\text{BPSK} = P_b^\text{QPSK} = Q\!\left(\sqrt{2E_b/N_0}\right), \qquad Q(x) = \tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}(x/\sqrt2)$$

BPSK

Two points as far apart as possible: one bit per symbol, the lowest error rate for a given energy per bit.

QPSK at 4 dB

The clouds touch the decision boundaries (the axes): about 1 bit in 80 is wrong.

16-QAM

Four bits per symbol, twice QPSK, but the points are much closer: it needs about 4 dB more \(E_b/N_0\) for the same error rate. Wi-Fi and 4G switch between schemes as the signal changes.

8-PSK

Eight points on a circle: three bits per symbol, all with the same amplitude (friendly to saturating amplifiers).

IQ demodulation

The receiver multiplies the signal by its own \(\cos\) and \(-\sin\) of the carrier and averages over each symbol: out come the I and Q values. If its oscillator is off by a phase \(\varphi\), every point is rotated by \(-\varphi\); off by a frequency \(\Delta f\), the rotation grows with time and the constellation spins.

$$s = I\cos\omega t - Q\sin\omega t, \qquad \hat I + j\hat Q = (I + jQ)\,e^{-j(\varphi + 2\pi\Delta f\,t)}$$

Phase error

A 30° phase error rotates the whole constellation by −30°. Points drift towards the decision boundaries: a phase-locked loop must remove it.

90°

A quarter turn: what was I is read as −Q. A carrier phase ambiguity of 90° is why QPSK systems use differential coding or pilots.

Frequency offset

A 0.05 Hz offset adds 4.5° every symbol: the points walk around the circle. Real receivers track it continuously.

Noise

Thermal noise turns each point into a cloud; averaging over the symbol (a matched filter) keeps the cloud as small as possible.
