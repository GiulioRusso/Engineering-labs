Convolution

Flip \(h\), slide it to position \(t\), multiply by \(x\) point by point and take the area: that is \(y(t)\). The orange curve is the flipped, shifted \(h\); the violet one their product; the lower plot collects the areas.

$$y(t) = (x * h)(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(\tau)\,h(t - \tau)\,d\tau$$

Charging an RC circuit

A rectangular pulse into a system whose impulse response decays: the output rises while the pulse lasts, then decays. Every output is the input smeared by \(h\).

Triangle * rectangle

Convolution always smooths: the result is smoother than either input, and as long as their lengths added.

Gaussians

Two Gaussians give a Gaussian whose variances add: \(\sigma^2 = \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2\). In frequency, convolution is a product.

$$\mathcal F\{x * h\} = X(f)\,H(f)$$

Linear time-invariant systems

A system that is linear and does not change in time is completely described by its impulse response \(h(t)\): the output to any input is \(x * h\). Equivalently, each sine passes through scaled by \(|H(f)|\) and delayed by the phase of \(H(f)\).

$$y = x * h, \qquad x = \sin 2\pi f t \;\Rightarrow\; y = |H(f)|\,\sin(2\pi f t + \angle H(f))$$

Low-pass on a square wave

The RC circuit cannot follow the jumps: the high harmonics are cut, the edges become exponential.

High-pass on a square wave

Only the changes get through: a spike at every edge, decaying with \(\tau\). The constant part (DC) is blocked.

A slow sine

Far below the corner frequency \(1/2\pi\tau\) the low-pass passes the sine almost unchanged.

A fast sine

Above the corner the output is smaller and lags behind the input: \(|H| = 1/\sqrt{1 + (2\pi f\tau)^2}\), phase \(-\arctan 2\pi f\tau\).

An echo

\(h(t) = \delta(t) + a\,\delta(t - T)\): every input comes back once more, weaker. In frequency the echo makes a comb: \(|H|\) ripples with period \(1/T\).

Moving average

Averaging over a window \(T\) smooths the square wave; frequencies that fit whole periods into \(T\) are cancelled (zeros of the sinc).

Digital filtering

An FIR filter is a convolution with a short list of taps \(h[k]\). A windowed sinc gives a low-pass; subtracting it from an impulse gives a high-pass; the difference of two low-passes, a band-pass. The output (blue) is shifted back by the filter delay of \((M-1)/2\) samples.

$$y[n] = \sum_{k=0}^{M-1} h[k]\,x[n - k], \qquad h[k] = w[k]\,\frac{\sin(2\pi f_c (k - m)/f_s)}{\pi (k - m)}$$

High-pass

Now the slow 5 Hz wave is removed and the 120 Hz tone (plus the high-frequency noise) remains.

Band-pass

Only frequencies between 90 and 150 Hz pass: the 120 Hz tone, with a little noise from the same band.

Few taps

A short filter cannot have a sharp edge: the transition band is about \(f_s \cdot 3.3/M\) wide, and the 120 Hz tone is not fully removed.

More noise

White noise is spread over all frequencies; the low-pass keeps only the slice below 20 Hz, about 4 % of its power.
