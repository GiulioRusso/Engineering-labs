What you are looking at

Top: in the τ axis, the fixed signal h(τ) (blue) and the other signal g flipped and slid to position t (orange). Where they overlap, their product is shaded green. Bottom: the output; the orange dot is the green area at the current t, and the curve is what it traces as t moves.
$$(h * g)(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} h(\tau)\,g(t-\tau)\,d\tau$$

One output value takes four steps. Do them slowly, they are always the same:

  1. Flip g: \(g(\tau) \to g(-\tau)\). Its right end is now on the left.
  2. Shift it to t: \(g(t-\tau)\). Increasing t moves it to the right.
  3. Multiply by h(τ), point by point.
  4. Integrate: the area of the product is the output at that t.

Common mistakes

Forgetting the flip — invisible for symmetric shapes, wrong for everything else (try the ramp). Thinking the output has the length of the longer input: it lasts the sum of the two lengths. Mixing up t and τ: τ runs along the axis of the top plot; t is one number, the position of the slide.

Rectangle * rectangle

Two rectangles of height 1. Before they touch, the area is zero. As g enters, the overlap is a rectangle whose width grows linearly with t: a ramp. When one is fully inside the other, the area stops growing. Then it shrinks linearly. With equal widths there is no flat part: the result is a triangle.

$$\text{rect} * \text{rect} = \max(0,\, 1 - |t|)$$

Worked example

Widths 1 and 1: at t = −0.7 the overlap is from −0.5 to −0.2, width 0.3 → output 0.3. At t = 0: width 1 → output 1. Widths 1 and 2: rises over 1 s, flat for 1 s (height 1), falls over 1 s — a trapezoid 3 s long.

Try it

Stop at t = −0.7 and check the area by eye: the green strip is 0.3 wide and 1 tall.

Overlap growing

t = −0.7: the orange rectangle covers 0.3 s of the blue one. Output 0.3, and it grows by 1 for every second of slide.

Full overlap

t = 0: the two rectangles coincide, the area is 1 — the peak of the triangle.

Different widths

1 s and 2 s: the narrow one fits inside the wide one for a whole second, giving a flat top. Total length 3 s.

Any pair of signals

The same four steps work for any shapes. Convolution is the mathematical form of blurring, smoothing and memory: every output value is a weighted average of the input around that time, with weights given by the other signal.

Worked example

Gaussian * Gaussian (σ = 0.3 s each): another Gaussian with σ = √(0.3² + 0.3²) = 0.42 s. Variances add, as for the sum of two independent random variables — that is exactly what convolution of probability densities means.

Try it

Choose the ramp for g and slide slowly: once flipped, its low end leads and its tall end trails.

Rectangle * Gaussian

The rectangle keeps its width, but its vertical edges become smooth S-curves, each as wide as the Gaussian. This is what a camera's blur does to a sharp edge.

Gaussian * Gaussian

Still a Gaussian, √2 times wider. Blur twice with the same lens and the blurs add in quadrature.

Rectangle * exponential

A pulse into an RC circuit (whose impulse response is the exponential): the output charges as \(1-e^{-at}\) while the pulse lasts, then discharges. Every RC circuit computes this convolution.

Watch the flip

The ramp g rises from 0 to 1. Flipped and slid, its low end arrives first and the tall end last: the output grows slowly (quadratically) and then drops suddenly when the tall end leaves. Without the flip you would predict the opposite shape.

Discrete convolution, by hand

What you are looking at

Top left: the input sequence x[k]. Top right: the kernel h flipped and moved to n. Bottom left: the products of the samples that line up. Bottom right: their sum, y[n], built one index at a time. The HUD writes the sum out.
$$y[n] = \sum_k x[k]\,h[n-k]$$

This is what a computer does: no integrals, just multiply-and-add. A digital filter is nothing more than this sum.

Worked example

x = [1, 2, 3, 2, 1, …], h = [1, 1, 1]: y[2] = x[0]·1 + x[1]·1 + x[2]·1 = 1 + 2 + 3 = 6. With h = [1, −1]: y[2] = x[2] − x[1] = 1, the change between samples.

Common mistake

Lining up h without flipping. With [1, −1] that gives the previous minus the current sample — the opposite sign.

Try it

Step n by hand and check each product before looking at the sum.

Three-point sum

Each output is the sum of three neighbours: noise and sharp changes are smoothed (divide by 3 to get the average).

Difference

[1, −1] subtracts the previous sample: constant parts vanish, only changes remain — the discrete derivative, an edge detector.

Echo

[1, 0, 0, 0.5]: each output is the input plus half of the input three samples ago. Every reverb in audio is a (long) kernel like this.

The convolution theorem

What you are looking at

Top: h and g, and their convolution computed by sliding. Bottom left: their spectra. Bottom right: the spectrum of the convolution (solid) and the product of the two spectra (dashed) — they coincide.
$$h * g \;\longleftrightarrow\; H(f)\cdot G(f)$$

This is the most useful identity in signal processing. Sliding and integrating is hard; multiplying two curves is easy. So: to filter, multiply the spectrum by the filter's response; to understand what a convolution does, look at the product of spectra.

Worked example

rect * rect = triangle, and sinc · sinc = sinc²: the triangle's spectrum. Its sidelobes are the square of the rectangle's: −26.5 dB instead of −13.3 dB.

Common mistake

Thinking a filter can add frequencies. It multiplies: where the input spectrum is zero the output is zero too. Only non-linear systems create new frequencies.

Try it

Pick rect and Gaussian: the product inherits the Gaussian's lack of sidelobes.

rect * rect

Two sinc spectra multiply into sinc²: the triangle's spectrum, always positive, sidelobes squared.

Smoothing

Multiplying by the Gaussian's spectrum (no sidelobes, falling fast) kills the rectangle's high frequencies: that is why the blurred rectangle has smooth edges.
