Robot dynamics: the free arm

What you are looking at

Left: a two-link arm in a vertical plane (1 m and 0.8 m, 5 kg and 3 kg), released from rest with no motor torque; the orange line is the path of its tip. Top right: kinetic, potential and total energy. Bottom right: the entries of its inertia matrix B(q). Below: the elbow angle.

Kinematics says where the links are; dynamics says what torques it takes to move them. For a robot the equations of motion come from Lagrange’s method and have a fixed structure: an inertia matrix B(q) that multiplies the joint accelerations, Coriolis and centrifugal terms C(q, q̇)q̇, and gravity g(q). Even two links show what makes robots hard to control: B depends on the posture (a folded arm is easier to swing about the shoulder than a stretched one), the joints push on each other, and released from horizontal the arm swings chaotically. With no friction, energy just moves back and forth between kinetic and potential.

$$B(\mathbf q)\,\ddot{\mathbf q} + C(\mathbf q, \dot{\mathbf q})\,\dot{\mathbf q} + \mathbf g(\mathbf q) = \boldsymbol\tau, \qquad B_{11} = I_1 + m_1 l_{c1}^2 + I_2 + m_2\big(l_1^2 + l_{c2}^2 + 2l_1l_{c2}\cos q_2\big)$$
  1. Kinetic energy ½ q̇ᵀB(q)q̇, potential energy of the two centres of mass.
  2. Lagrange’s equations give B, C and g.
  3. B₁₁ changes with the elbow angle: 7.71 kg·m² stretched, 4.42 folded.

Worked example

Released horizontal: potential 0 (the reference). Hanging straight down is 65.7 J lower, so the kinetic energy can reach 65.6 J as the arm swings through the bottom.

Common mistake

Treating each joint as a separate motor with a fixed load. The load on the shoulder depends on the elbow’s angle and speed: the joints are coupled.

Try it

Watch B₁₂ and B₁₁ change as the elbow folds and unfolds, while the green total energy stays flat.

Hanging

Straight down, gravity has no lever arm: g(q) = 0, and the arm stays still. The stable equilibrium.

A small swing

Released 15° from hanging: a gentle, nearly periodic swing, a double pendulum at small amplitude.

Friction

Viscous friction in the joints (2 N·m·s): the total energy falls and the arm settles hanging down, near −65.7 J.

PD control with gravity compensation

What you are looking at

Left: the arm moving from hanging to the target posture (faint arm, red tip). Top right: the error of each joint. Bottom right: the motor torques. Below: the size of the error.

The simplest controller acts at each joint like a spring and a damper pulling towards the target: torque proportional to the angle error, minus some proportional to speed. On its own it cannot hold the arm up, because gravity keeps pulling: the arm settles where the spring’s pull balances the weight, below the target. Adding the gravity torque the model predicts, ĝ(q), removes that offset. With exact gravity compensation this law brings the arm to any set point, whatever its other dynamics: a classic result proven with a Lyapunov function. The course exercise main07 tunes exactly this law for the Jaco2.

$$\boldsymbol\tau = K_P(\mathbf q_d - \mathbf q) - K_D\,\dot{\mathbf q} + \hat{\mathbf g}(\mathbf q), \qquad \text{without } \hat{\mathbf g}: \quad K_P(\mathbf q_d - \mathbf q_\infty) = \mathbf g(\mathbf q_\infty)$$
  1. K_P sets the stiffness: a larger error gives a larger torque.
  2. K_D damps the motion: too little and it swings, too much and it creeps.
  3. ĝ(q) holds the weight, so the error can go to zero.

Worked example

Target (30°, 60°): holding it needs g = (46.7, 0) N·m (the second link is vertical, so the elbow needs nothing). With K_P = 100 and no ĝ the arm settles at (−5.1°, 55.7°): the shoulder sags 35°, because 46.7/100 rad is 27° and the sag also lowers the load.

Common mistake

Raising K_P to shrink the gravity sag. It shrinks only as 1/K_P and needs ever larger torques; compensating gravity removes it.

Try it

Untick gravity compensation and raise K_P: the sag shrinks, never vanishes.

No compensation

The arm stops short, hanging 35° low at the shoulder: the spring K_P(q_d − q) is holding the weight.

Little damping

K_D = 3: the arm overshoots and swings back and forth around the target, still 36° off after 4 s.

Stiffer

K_P = 400, K_D = 40: there in 1.5 s, but the first kick asks for over 900 N·m. Gains are limited by the motors.

Computed torque (inverse dynamics)

What you are looking at

The arm following a fast move (a quintic from hanging to the target in 1.5 s) with the chosen law; the faint arm is the reference. Right: joint errors and torques. Below: the size of the error.

PD control waits for an error before it acts. If we have a model of the arm, we can do better: compute, from the desired acceleration, the torque the dynamics need, B(q)q̈_d + Cq̇ + g, and apply it in advance. Adding a PD correction inside, the arm’s nonlinear, coupled dynamics cancel out exactly and each joint’s error obeys a simple linear equation, ë + K_D ė + K_P e = 0, which goes to zero as fast as we choose. The catch: it is only as good as the model. With wrong masses the cancellation is imperfect and an error remains.

$$\boldsymbol\tau = \hat B(\mathbf q)\big(\ddot{\mathbf q}_d + K_D\dot{\mathbf e} + K_P\mathbf e\big) + \hat C(\mathbf q, \dot{\mathbf q})\dot{\mathbf q} + \hat{\mathbf g}(\mathbf q) \quad\Rightarrow\quad \ddot{\mathbf e} + K_D\dot{\mathbf e} + K_P\mathbf e = \mathbf 0 \ \text{ if } \hat{\ } = \text{exact}$$
  1. Feedforward: the torque the reference motion needs.
  2. Feedback: a PD on the error, scaled by the inertia.
  3. The robot behaves like two independent double integrators.

Worked example

Exact model: the error stays below 10⁻⁶ rad all the way. PD + ĝ with the same gains on the same path: up to 30°. With the model’s masses 30 % too small, computed torque misses by 5.9°, mostly because it under-compensates gravity.

Common mistake

Trusting the model. Real payloads, friction and motor dynamics differ from it; practical controllers add robustness, integral action or adaptation.

Try it

Raise the model error from 0 to 60 % and watch the final error grow.

PD on the same path

Same gains, no model except gravity: the arm lags the fast reference by up to 30° and catches up only after the move ends.

A wrong model

Masses 30 % too light in the controller: the arm follows the shape of the move but ends 5.9° off, the gravity torque under-estimated. The PD part fights it, as in uncompensated PD.

PD with stiffer gains

K_P = 400, K_D = 40: the lag drops to 8°, at the cost of larger torques and more sensitivity to noise. Model-based control gets more accuracy for less gain.

About the model

Two uniform rods (1 m, 5 kg and 0.8 m, 3 kg), joint angles measured from the horizontal (shoulder) and from the first link (elbow), g = 9.81 m/s². Lagrangian model (Siciliano et al., ch. 7.3.2), RK4 at 1 ms. Controllers from ch. 8.5 (PD + gravity compensation) and 8.5.2 (inverse dynamics); the course exercise main07 applies the first to the Jaco2. Tests: energy conserved when free (10⁻⁶ J); PD + ĝ reaches the set point; without ĝ the arm settles at K_P⁻¹g; computed torque with the exact model tracks within 10⁻⁶ rad, over 1000 times better than PD + ĝ; a 30 % mass error leaves a visible but bounded error.
