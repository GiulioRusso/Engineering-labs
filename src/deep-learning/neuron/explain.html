A neuron is a tilted sheet

What you are looking at

A 3D view: the floor is the input plane (x₁, x₂). Blue crosses are class 0 (drawn on the floor), yellow crosses class 1 (drawn at height 1). The coloured sheet is the neuron's output p for every point of the plane; the orange line is where p = ½ — the decision boundary. Drag to rotate.

A neuron does two things. It projects each point onto the direction of its weights, collapsing the plane to one number z = w·x + b; then it squashes z into a probability with the sigmoid. Lines of equal z are straight, so the boundary is always a straight line.

$$p=\sigma(w_1x_1+w_2x_2+b),\qquad \sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}$$
  1. The direction of (w₁, w₂) is the direction the sheet climbs.
  2. Its length |w| is how steeply: large weights make a cliff, small weights a gentle ramp.
  3. The bias b slides the whole sheet sideways without turning it.

Worked example

w = (1, 1), b = 0: the boundary is x₁ + x₂ = 0. The point (1, 1) has z = 2, p = σ(2) = 0.88: "88% class 1".

Common mistake

Reading p as certainty. With huge weights every point gets p near 0 or 1, even points far from any data the neuron has seen.

Try it

Turn the weights until the sheet rises under the yellow crosses and stays low under the blue ones; watch the accuracy and the loss.

Large weights

Same line, much steeper sheet: a sharp yes/no.

Small weights

p stays close to ½ everywhere: the neuron is unsure about every point.

The bias

b = 2 moves the line by b/|w| towards the lower-left: more points are called class 1.

The wrong way

The sheet climbs away from the yellow points: low accuracy, high loss.

Learning is walking downhill

What you are looking at

Left: the data and the neuron's current boundary (background shading = p). Top right: the loss for every pair of weights — a landscape, dark = low — and the path the weights take. Bottom right: the loss step by step.

Turning the line by hand does not scale. Instead, give every possible line a score — the loss, low when the neuron is right and confident — and roll downhill. The gradient ∇L points uphill; step the other way.

$$L=-\frac1N\sum_i\big[y_i\log p_i+(1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\big],\qquad \nabla_wL=\frac1N\sum_i(p_i-y_i)\,\mathbf x_i,\qquad w\leftarrow w-\eta\nabla L$$

Worked example

A point with y = 1 and p = 0.3 contributes (0.3 − 1)·x = −0.7x to the gradient: the weights move by +0.7ηx, towards that point.

Common mistakes

Too small η: many tiny steps. Too large: the steps overshoot. And on the moons no setting helps: the problem is the shape of the boundary, not the training.

η = 0.05

The path is correct but slow: after 300 steps it is still on the way.

η = 3

Big steps reach the valley quickly; on this smooth loss they do not blow up.

Moons

The best straight line still misclassifies the tips of both moons: accuracy stalls around 85%.

A hidden layer bends the plane

What you are looking at

Left: the network's decision region on the moons, with each hidden unit's own line in grey. Top right: with 2 hidden units, the data as the output neuron sees it (the hidden space); otherwise the accuracy. Bottom right: the loss as training goes on.

One line cannot cut the moons apart. A hidden layer draws H lines at once and passes each through a non-linearity: the plane is bent (tanh) or folded (ReLU) along those lines. In the bent space a single straight line can separate the classes — and mapped back to the input it is a curve.

$$h_j=f(\mathbf w_j\cdot\mathbf x+b_j),\qquad p=\sigma\Big(\sum_j v_jh_j+c\Big)$$

Worked example

With 2 hidden units the hidden space is a plane again: watch the two moons get pulled apart in the top-right chart until the orange line separates them.

Common mistake

Thinking more units is always better: 16 units fit these 200 points easily, but with noisy data they also fit the noise (see Generalization).

Two units

The smallest network that can do it: the hidden plane shows the bending directly.

ReLU

ReLU is zero on one side of its line and straight on the other: the boundary is made of straight pieces with corners.

Sixteen units

Many lines: a smoother, more flexible boundary, learned faster.
