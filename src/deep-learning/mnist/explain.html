Recognising handwritten digits

What you are looking at

Left: 25 test digits the network never trains on, each with its current answer (✓ right, ✗ wrong). Top right: accuracy on all 1000 test digits after each epoch (orange) and on training digits (green). Bottom right: the confusion matrix, true digit against predicted. Below: the training loss. Press play: 3000 training digits, one epoch per second.

Each image is 14 × 14 = 196 numbers, one per pixel. The network multiplies them by weights, passes them through 32 hidden ReLU units, and produces 10 scores, one per digit; softmax turns the scores into probabilities, and the largest is the answer. At the start the weights are random and so are the answers, about one in ten right. Every mini-batch of 32 digits nudges all 6634 weights to make the correct digit a little more likely. Within one epoch most of the 25 digits flip to ✓.

$$\mathbf h = \mathrm{ReLU}(W_1\mathbf x + \mathbf b_1), \qquad \mathbf p = \mathrm{softmax}(W_2\mathbf h + \mathbf b_2), \qquad L = -\ln p_{\text{true digit}}$$
  1. Flatten the image into 196 inputs.
  2. Hidden layer: 32 weighted sums, ReLU.
  3. Output: 10 scores, softmax, pick the largest.
  4. Loss: −ln of the probability given to the right digit; backpropagation and Adam lower it.

Worked example

196 → 32 → 10: 196 · 32 + 32 + 32 · 10 + 10 = 6634 parameters. Test accuracy after 1, 3, 6 and 12 epochs: 81 %, 88 %, 89 %, 90 %; on the training digits it keeps climbing (96 % at 12): the gap is overfitting starting.

Common mistake

Reading the confusion matrix by columns. Rows are the true digit, columns the answer: off-diagonal brightness in row 4, column 9 means fours mistaken for nines.

Try it

Watch the confusion matrix sharpen into a bright diagonal, and see which off-diagonal cells stay lit.

No hidden layer

Softmax regression: 1970 parameters, each digit's score a weighted sum of the pixels. It still reaches 87.5 % after 12 epochs: most digits are told apart by where their ink is.

64 hidden units

13 258 parameters: faster and better, 91.5 % on test after 12 epochs, 98 % on training. Notice the widening gap: with only 3000 examples, more capacity also means more memorising.

One digit, up close

What you are looking at

Left: one test digit, enlarged. Top right: the probability the trained network (32 hidden units, 5 epochs) gives each digit. Bottom right: an occlusion map, how much the answer's probability drops when each 2 × 2 patch is blanked. Below: the hidden units' activations for this digit.

The probabilities say how sure the network is, not just what it thinks. The occlusion map asks the network which parts of the image it relies on: blank a patch, and if the answer weakens, that patch mattered. For a clear 0 the whole ring matters a little; for a confusable digit, a single stroke can decide.

$$p_k = \frac{e^{s_k}}{\sum_j e^{s_j}}, \qquad \text{importance of a patch} = p_\text{answer}(\mathbf x) - p_\text{answer}(\mathbf x\ \text{with the patch blanked})$$
  1. Read the tallest bar: the answer, and how sure.
  2. Read the runner-up: what it might be confused with.
  3. Bright patches: the evidence for the answer.

Worked example

Test digit #25 is a 0: the network gives it 99.9 %. Test digit #38, a 2, gets 49 % for 2 against 29 % for 3: right, but barely.

Common mistake

Taking the probability as a calibrated chance. A network can be 90 % sure and wrong; small networks trained briefly are often over- or under-confident.

Try it

Slide through the test digits and look for one where the runner-up is close.

An unsure 2

#38: 49 % "2", 29 % "3", 10 % "5". Its bottom stroke is short and curly, so it looks partly like a 3; the occlusion map lights up on that stroke.

A 7 read as a 9

What the weights look like

What you are looking at

Softmax regression (no hidden layer) after 5 epochs: for each digit, its 196 weights drawn as an image. Gold pixels raise that digit's score when inked, blue ones lower it. Top right: test accuracy per digit. Bottom right: the confusion matrix.

With no hidden layer, each digit's score is just its weights times the pixels: a template. The 0 template is a gold ring with a blue centre (ink in the middle argues against 0); the 1 is a gold vertical bar with blue on both sides. Recognition is overlap with the template. With a hidden layer, the first-layer weights are no longer whole digits but parts: strokes, edges, blobs that many digits share, which the next layer combines.

$$s_k = \mathbf w_k\cdot\mathbf x + b_k = \sum_{\text{pixels}} w_{k,i}\,x_i$$
  1. Gold: "ink here is evidence for this digit".
  2. Blue: "ink here is evidence against".
  3. Compare two templates to see why those digits get confused.

Worked example

Softmax regression after 5 epochs: 84.5 % overall; best on 1 (95 %), worst on 4 (76 %), which it confuses with 9 18 times: their templates overlap in the top half.

Common mistake

Expecting hidden units to be digit detectors. They are features; only the combination at the output is a digit.

Try it

Find the blue gap in the middle of the 0 template, and the gold loop at the top of the 9.

First layer of a 16-unit MLP

Sixteen hidden units, each with a 14 × 14 weight pattern: blurry strokes and opposing blobs, not digits. With 16 such features the network reaches 87 % (5 epochs), better than any set of 10 templates.

Its mistakes

What you are looking at

The first 25 test digits the network (32 hidden units, 5 epochs) gets wrong, labelled "true→answer confidence". Top right: mistakes per digit. Below: the confidence of every wrong answer, sorted.

Looking at errors is the fastest way to understand a classifier. Some are digits a person would hesitate on: a 4 closed at the top, a 7 with a crossbar, a squashed 5. Some are errors no person would make, often at high confidence: those show what the model's view of the image lacks, here mainly the low resolution and the fact that an MLP has no notion of shapes being shifted.

$$\text{error rate} = \frac{\text{wrong}}{1000}, \qquad \text{most common: } 4 \to 9,\; 8 \to 3,\; 2 \to 8$$
  1. Scan the gallery: would you have got it right?
  2. Look at the per-digit bars: 5 and 8 are hardest here.
  3. Check the sorted confidences: unsure mistakes are less worrying than sure ones.

Worked example

119 of 1000 wrong (88.1 %); 23 of them are fives (77 of 100 right) and 25 are eights.

Common mistake

Tuning a model by its test mistakes until they disappear. Then the test set is no longer unseen, and the accuracy you report is optimistic.

Try it

Switch to softmax regression: more mistakes, and different ones.

Softmax regression's mistakes

155 wrong (84.5 %); 4 → 9 alone happens 18 times. A single template per digit cannot cope with the many ways of writing a 4.

About the model

Data: a balanced MNIST subset (Y. LeCun, C. Cortes, C. J. C. Burges; CC BY-SA 3.0): the first 300 training and 100 test images of each digit, averaged to 14 × 14 and stored at 4 bits per pixel (tools/mnist.mjs). Network written from scratch: ReLU hidden layer, softmax output, cross-entropy, Adam (learning rate 0.003), mini-batches of 32. Tests: data sizes, balance and range; after 4 epochs softmax regression exceeds 78 % and a 32-unit MLP 85 % on the test digits, MLP better; confusion rows sum to 100; an untrained network is near chance; parameter counts.
