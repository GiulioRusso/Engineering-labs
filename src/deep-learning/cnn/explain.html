A convolution kernel on a digit

What you are looking at

Left: a test digit (a 7). Top right: a 3 × 3 kernel, its nine weights written in (gold positive, blue negative). Bottom right: the output, the kernel applied at every position (gold = strong positive response, blue = negative). Below: one row of the input and of the output.

A convolution slides a small window of weights over the image. At each position it multiplies the nine pixels under the window by the nine weights and adds them up: one number of the output. Because the same nine weights are used everywhere, the kernel asks the same question at every place: "is there a vertical edge here?", "how bright is it around here?". Edge kernels are positive on one side and negative on the other, so they respond where the image changes and give zero where it is flat.

$$y_{r,c} = \sum_{a=-1}^{1}\sum_{b=-1}^{1} k_{a,b}\; x_{r+a,\,c+b}$$
  1. Put the kernel on a pixel; multiply the overlapping pairs; add.
  2. Move one pixel; repeat (outside the image counts as 0).
  3. Gold output: the pattern of the kernel is there; blue: its opposite; dark: nothing.

Worked example

Vertical-edge kernel (Sobel) on the 7: the output ranges from −3.3 to +3.4: positive where ink begins going left to right (the left edge of the stroke), negative where it ends. A flat region gives exactly 0.

Common mistake

Thinking the kernel looks for a whole digit. It sees only 3 × 3 pixels: it finds local features; later layers combine them.

Try it

Switch between vertical and horizontal edges on a 7: the diagonal stroke shows in both, the top bar only in one.

Horizontal edges

The same kernel turned 90°: now the top bar of the 7 lights up, gold under it and blue over it.

Blur

All positive weights adding up to 1: each output pixel is a weighted average of its neighbourhood. Edges soften; the peak drops from 1 to 0.58.

Sharpen

5 in the centre, −1 around: a pixel is boosted when it is brighter than its neighbours. The stroke's edges get a bright rim and a dark halo.

A CNN learns its own filters

What you are looking at

A small convolutional network training live on the 3000 digits. Top row: its six 5 × 5 filters, starting random. Second row: what each filter produces on the chosen test digit, after ReLU. Bottom left: the digit and the current answer. Top right: test accuracy per epoch, against the 32-unit MLP (dashed). Bottom right: the confusion matrix.

Instead of choosing kernels by hand, let gradient descent choose them. The network: six 5 × 5 filters slide over the image (ReLU keeps the positive responses), a 2 × 2 max-pool keeps the strongest response in each little area, and one dense layer turns the 6 × 5 × 5 pooled values into 10 digit scores. Within an epoch or two the filters turn from noise into stroke and edge detectors. Because each filter is reused at every position, the network needs few weights and copes well with digits written a little to the left or right.

$$14\times14 \xrightarrow{6\ \text{filters } 5\times5} 6\times10\times10 \xrightarrow{\text{ReLU, max-pool } 2\times2} 6\times5\times5 \xrightarrow{\text{dense}} 10 \xrightarrow{\text{softmax}} \mathbf p$$
  1. Filters: 6 × (25 + 1) = 156 parameters.
  2. Dense: 150 × 10 + 10 = 1510.
  3. Total 1666, a quarter of the MLP's 6634.
  4. Backpropagation through max-pool sends the gradient only to the winning position of each 2 × 2 block.

Worked example

Test accuracy after 1, 3 and 6 epochs: 82 %, 89 %, 92 %; it reaches 92.7 % after 7. The MLP with four times the parameters: 81 %, 88 %, 89 %.

Common mistake

Expecting the learned filters to look like textbook edge detectors. Some do; others are blobs or mixtures that only make sense together with the dense layer.

Try it

Pick a different test digit while it trains: watch which feature maps light up for a 1 and which for a 0.

Only two filters

562 parameters: two filters must serve every digit. It still reaches about 88 % after 8 epochs, as good as softmax regression with 1970 parameters: shared local filters are an efficient way to see.

About the model

Valid 5 × 5 convolution, ReLU, 2 × 2 max-pool, dense softmax layer, cross-entropy, Adam (learning rate 0.005), mini-batches of 32, on the 14 × 14 MNIST subset. Tests: backpropagation vs finite differences on filters, biases and dense weights (10⁻⁴); edge kernels give 0 on a flat image and the identity kernel returns the input; after 3 epochs the 1666-parameter CNN beats softmax regression by more than 2 points and exceeds 88 %.
