Backpropagation

What you are looking at

Left: a network with 2 inputs, 2 tanh hidden units and a sigmoid output, trained on one example. Blue edges are positive weights, red negative. Each step plays in four phases: blue pulses carry values forward, the loss is computed, red pulses carry the error back (edges then show ∂L/∂w), and the weights glide to their new values. Top right: the output p step by step. Bottom right: the gradient of every parameter.

To train, we need ∂L/∂w for every weight. The chain rule gives it, and the trick is to apply it from the output backwards: each unit receives the error from the layer above, multiplies it by its own local slope, and passes it on.

$$\delta_\text{out}=p-y,\qquad \delta_j=\delta_\text{out}\,v_j\,(1-h_j^2),\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{jk}}=\delta_j\,x_k,\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial v_j}=\delta_\text{out}\,h_j$$
  1. Forward: compute and store every z and h.
  2. Output error: δ = p − y (sigmoid with cross-entropy).
  3. Backward: δ of each hidden unit = δ above × weight × local slope.
  4. Gradient of a weight = δ of the unit it enters × value it carries.
  5. Update: w ← w − η ∂L/∂w.

Worked example

p = 0.6, y = 1: δ_out = −0.4. With v₁ = 0.7 and h₁ = 0.5: δ₁ = −0.4 · 0.7 · 0.75 = −0.21. With x₁ = 1: ∂L/∂w₁₁ = −0.21, so w₁₁ grows by 0.21η.

Common mistake

Thinking backprop is a different learning rule. It is only an efficient way to compute the gradient; gradient descent does the learning.

η = 2.5

Same gradients, bigger steps: p jumps towards the target in one or two steps.

x₁ = 0

The gradient of a weight is δ × the value it carries: a zero input leaves its weights untouched.

Target 0

δ_out = p is now positive: every gradient changes sign and p is pushed down.

Mini-batches

What you are looking at

Left: the loss of a logistic neuron for every pair of weights (dark = low), with two paths from the same start: the full gradient (green) and mini-batches of B random points (orange). Top right: the loss on all data along both paths. Bottom right: how far a mini-batch gradient is from the full one, against B.

The full gradient averages over every training point: exact but expensive on millions of examples. A mini-batch averages over B random points instead. The answer is right on average, with noise that shrinks like 1/√B.

$$\mathbf g_B=\frac1B\sum_{i\in\text{batch}}\nabla L_i,\qquad \mathbb E[\mathbf g_B]=\nabla L,\qquad \operatorname{sd}(\mathbf g_B)\propto\frac1{\sqrt B}$$

Worked example

1 000 000 examples, B = 100: 10 000 cheap steps in the time of one full-gradient step, each about 10 times noisier than with B = 10 000.

Common mistake

Treating the noise as pure harm. A little noise helps escape flat regions and sharp minima; that is part of why SGD trains deep networks well.

B = 1

Every step follows one point: a zig-zag around the green path, but it gets there.

B = 80

All the points (drawn with replacement): almost the green path.
