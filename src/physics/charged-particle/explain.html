Cyclotron motion

What you are looking at

A charged particle in a uniform magnetic field B pointing up (z), seen from above, with its trail and the force and velocity arrows.

A magnetic field pushes on a moving charge at right angles to its velocity. A force that is always sideways never speeds the particle up or slows it down; it only turns it. Turning at a steady rate with a steady speed is exactly circular motion.

$$\mathbf F = q\,\mathbf v\times\mathbf B, \qquad r_L = \frac{m v_\perp}{|q|B}, \qquad \omega_c = \frac{|q|B}{m}, \qquad T = \frac{2\pi m}{|q|B}$$
  1. Magnetic force = centripetal force: qvB = mv²/r gives the radius.
  2. Period = circumference / speed = 2πr/v: the v cancels, so the period does not depend on speed.
  3. Sign of q sets the sense of rotation: right-hand rule for +, the other way for −.

Worked example

A proton at 10 km/s in 1 mT: r = 1.67·10⁻²⁷ · 10⁴ / (1.60·10⁻¹⁹ · 10⁻³) = 0.104 m, T = 65.6 µs. An electron at 3000 km/s in the same field: r = 1.7 cm, T = 35.7 ns.

Common mistakes

Thinking B does work on the charge: F ⊥ v, so no work, constant speed (the ΔKE readout stays at 0). Getting the turning direction wrong for negative charges: the force reverses.

Try it

Double the speed: the circle doubles, the period stays the same.

Stronger field

Three times \(B\): a circle three times smaller, turned three times faster.

Faster particle

The radius grows with \(v\), but the period does not change: this is what makes a cyclotron work.

Opposite charge

\(q \to -q\) flips the force: the antiparticle circles the other way, same size.

An electron

1836 times lighter than a proton: it circles 1836 times faster, on a much smaller circle at the same speed.

Helical motion

What you are looking at

The same particle launched at an angle to B (shown in 3D). The trail is a helix around a field line.

Split the velocity into a part across B and a part along B. The magnetic force v × B only involves the part across B, so that part goes round in a circle as before. The part along B feels nothing and stays constant. A circle carried steadily along an axis is a helix, like a stretched spring.

$$v_\perp = v\sin\alpha,\quad v_\parallel = v\cos\alpha, \qquad r_L = \frac{m v_\perp}{|q|B}, \qquad \text{pitch} = v_\parallel\,T = v_\parallel\frac{2\pi m}{|q|B}$$
  1. Find the angle α between v and B and split the velocity.
  2. v⊥ gives the radius, as for a circle.
  3. The period is still 2πm/|q|B; in one period the particle moves v∥T along B: the pitch.

Worked example

Proton at 10 km/s, 60° to a 1 mT field: v⊥ = 8.66 km/s gives r = 9.0 cm; v∥ = 5 km/s and T = 65.6 µs give a pitch of 32.8 cm.

Common mistake

Using the full speed in the radius formula: only the component across B counts.

Try it

Lower the angle towards 0°: the circles shrink and the pitch grows. The particle follows the field line, as electrons from the Sun do on their way to the aurora.

Almost along B

At 17°: circles of 3.1 cm, pitch 63 cm. The particle is guided by the field line.

The E × B drift

What you are looking at

A proton in B (up, z) and an electric field E along x. Instead of closing, the loops march sideways, along y. The readouts compare the measured drift with E/B.

Add an electric field across B. On the half of the circle where the particle moves with E it speeds up, so that part of the loop is wider; on the other half it slows down and the loop is tighter. The loops no longer close, and the centre of the circle moves steadily, at right angles to both E and B.

$$\mathbf v_d = \frac{\cE{\mathbf E}\times\cB{\mathbf B}}{B^2}, \qquad |v_d| = \frac{E}{B}$$
  1. Find the direction with the right-hand rule for E × B: here x × z = −y.
  2. The speed is E/B, with no q and no m in it.
  3. So every particle, positive or negative, light or heavy, drifts the same way at the same speed.

Worked example

E = 1 V/m, B = 1 mT: v_d = 1/10⁻³ = 1000 m/s. In the frame moving at 1000 m/s the electric field vanishes and the proton just circles.

Common mistake

Expecting the particle to accelerate along E. Across a magnetic field it cannot: E only makes it drift sideways at constant average speed.

Try it

Turn on the antiparticle: it circles the other way but drifts alongside.

Same drift for both

The antiparticle circles the opposite way but drifts in the same direction at the same speed: \(\mathbf v_d\) contains neither \(q\) nor \(m\).

Stronger E

The drift speed is \(E/B\): three times the field, three times faster.

E along B: acceleration

What you are looking at

Now E points along B (up). The particle circles as before, but the helix stretches upward as it goes; the ΔKE readout grows.

An electric field along B pushes on the motion along B, which the magnetic field ignores. So the particle accelerates along the field line, exactly as if B were not there, while still circling around it. Unlike B, E does work: the kinetic energy rises.

$$m\dot v_\parallel = qE_\parallel, \qquad \frac{dK}{dt} = q\,\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf v$$
  1. Across B: circle, unchanged.
  2. Along B: constant acceleration a = qE/m.
  3. The pitch grows turn by turn.

Worked example

Proton, E = 2 V/m: a = 1.6·10⁻¹⁹ · 2 / 1.67·10⁻²⁷ = 1.9·10⁸ m/s². In five turns (328 µs) it gains 63 km/s along B, six times its start speed.

Common mistake

Thinking the radius grows too: only v∥ changes; v⊥, and so the radius, stay the same.

Try it

Make E_z negative: the helix squeezes, stops and comes back.

E against the motion

The particle slows along \(\mathbf B\), stops after about a third of a turn, and is pushed back: the helix closes up and reverses.

About the model

Boris pusher with 200 steps per gyration: half electric kick, exact magnetic rotation, half kick. In a pure magnetic field it conserves the speed to round-off (test: \(10^{-12}\)), and the drift matches \(E/B\) within 0.2 %.
