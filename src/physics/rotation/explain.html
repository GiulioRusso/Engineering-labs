Rolling and the moment of inertia

What you are looking at

Four objects released together on identical 4 m slopes: a solid sphere, a solid disc, a hollow sphere and a hoop. Top right: the share of their energy that goes into spinning. Bottom right: distance against time; below, their speeds.

A rolling object has to do two things at once: move forward and spin. The energy it gains from falling is split between the two. How much the spin takes depends on where the mass sits: far from the axis (a hoop) it is hard to spin, so it takes a big share and little is left for moving forward. The measure of this is the moment of inertia I, the rotational version of mass.

$$I = cMR^2, \qquad Mgh = \tfrac12 Mv^2 + \tfrac12 I\omega^2, \;\; \omega = \frac{v}{R} \;\Rightarrow\; a = \frac{g\sin\theta}{1 + c}$$
  1. Look up c: solid sphere 2/5, disc 1/2, hollow sphere 2/3, hoop 1.
  2. Rolling without slipping ties the spin to the speed: ω = v/R.
  3. Energy (or forces and torques) give a = g sin θ / (1 + c): M and R cancel.
  4. Share of energy in spinning: c/(1 + c), from 29 % (sphere) to 50 % (hoop).

Worked example

θ = 20°, 4 m: sphere a = 9.81 · 0.342/1.4 = 2.40 m/s², arrives in 1.83 s at 4.38 m/s (a frictionless sliding block would reach 5.18 m/s). Disc 1.89 s, hollow sphere 1.99 s, hoop 2.18 s.

Common mistake

Thinking the heavier or bigger object wins. Only the shape factor c matters; a large heavy ball and a marble tie.

Try it

Change the mass and the slope: the order never changes.

Five times heavier

Every force and every inertia scale with \(M\): the race is identical.

Steeper slope

At 40° all are faster (sphere 1.33 s, hoop 1.59 s), but the ratios of their accelerations are the same: \(5/7 : 2/3 : 3/5 : 1/2\).

Torque and angular acceleration: the spool

What you are looking at

A mass hangs from a string wound round a spool (a disc on a fixed axle). As it falls it unwinds the string and spins the spool. Top right: where the energy goes. Bottom right: the drop against free fall.

The string pulls the spool at its rim: that is a torque, and a torque makes it spin faster and faster, as a force makes a mass speed up. The spool resists in proportion to its moment of inertia. Meanwhile the string holds the falling mass back, so it falls slower than in free fall. A heavy spool holds it back a lot, a light one hardly at all.

$$\tau = TR = I\alpha, \qquad mg - T = ma, \qquad a = \alpha R \;\Rightarrow\; a = \frac{mg}{m + I/R^2}, \quad I_\text{disc} = \tfrac12 M_s R^2$$
  1. Mass: mg − T = ma.
  2. Spool: TR = Iα, with α = a/R because the string does not slip.
  3. Eliminate T: the spool acts like an extra mass I/R² = Ms/2 that has to be accelerated too.

Worked example

m = 0.5 kg, a 2 kg spool: a = 0.5 · 9.81 / (0.5 + 1) = 3.27 m/s², tension T = 0.5 (9.81 − 3.27) = 3.27 N. After a 1.6 m drop two thirds of the energy is in the spinning spool.

Common mistake

Taking T = mg. The mass accelerates, so the string pulls less than its weight.

Try it

Increase the spool mass: the fall slows and more energy goes into the spin.

Heavy spool

An 8 kg spool behaves like 4 kg of extra inertia: \(a = 1.09\) m/s², a slow, steady fall.

Light spool

A 0.1 kg spool adds only 0.05 kg of inertia: \(a = 8.92\) m/s², close to free fall.

Conservation of angular momentum

What you are looking at

A skater seen from above, holding a 2 kg weight in each hand, spinning. Between t = 1 s and 2.5 s the arms pull in from 0.8 m to 0.25 m. Top right: the spin rate ω and the moment of inertia I. Bottom right: angular momentum (flat) and kinetic energy.

Without an outside twist (torque), the angular momentum L = Iω of a spinning body cannot change. Pull mass in towards the axis and I drops; the only way to keep L fixed is to spin faster. The kinetic energy is not conserved: it rises, and the extra energy comes from the work the skater's arms do pulling the weights inward against their tendency to fly out.

$$L = I\omega = \text{const}, \qquad I = I_\text{body} + 2m r^2, \qquad K = \tfrac12 I\omega^2 = \frac{L^2}{2I}$$
  1. Compute I at the start and at the end.
  2. ω₂ = ω₁ · I₁/I₂ (L is the same).
  3. K₂/K₁ = I₁/I₂ as well: the energy grows by the same factor.

Worked example

I_body = 1 kg m², two 2 kg weights: at 0.8 m, I = 1 + 2·2·0.64 = 3.56 kg m²; at 0.25 m, 1.25 kg m². Starting at 2 rad/s, the spin goes to 2 · 3.56/1.25 = 5.70 rad/s, and the energy from 7.1 J to 20.3 J.

Common mistake

Assuming kinetic energy is conserved too. Angular momentum is; the energy changes by the work of the arms.

Try it

Make the hand weights heavier: the speed-up is bigger, because more of I sits in the arms.

Arms out

The reverse: spinning at 5.7 rad/s with the arms in, letting them out to 0.8 m slows the spin to 2.0 rad/s. Skaters stop a spin this way.

No weights

With 0.2 kg in each hand, most of the inertia is in the body: I only drops from 1.26 to 1.03 kg m², and the spin rises by 22 %.

About the model

Closed-form results for ideal rigid bodies rolling without slipping, an ideal string and a skater modelled as a fixed body plus two point masses. Tests: the four rolling accelerations vs \(g\sin\theta/(1 + c)\); energy balance at the bottom (10⁻¹²); the race order; spool acceleration and energy; skater ω and KE ratios = I₁/I₂.
