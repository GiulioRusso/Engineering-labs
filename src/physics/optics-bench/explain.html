Refraction

What you are looking at

A ray hitting the boundary between two materials: the reflected ray, the refracted ray bending toward or away from the normal (dashed), and the angles.

Light travels slower in glass or water (speed c/n). A wavefront hitting the surface at an angle has one end slowed first, so the front swings round — like a car whose right wheels hit sand. Going into a denser medium it bends toward the normal; coming out, away from it. Beyond the critical angle no refracted ray exists: everything is reflected.

$$n_1\sin\theta_1 = n_2\sin\theta_2, \qquad \sin\theta_c = \frac{n_2}{n_1}\;(n_1 > n_2)$$

Worked example

Air to glass at 40°: sin θ₂ = sin 40°/1.5 = 0.43, θ₂ = 25°. Glass to air: θc = 41.8°. Diamond: 24.4° — light bounces around inside many times, hence the sparkle.

Common mistake

Measuring angles from the surface instead of from the normal.

Try it

Go from glass to air and raise the angle past 42°: the refracted ray vanishes.

Straight in

\(\theta_1 = 0\): \(\sin\theta_2 = 0\) too. A ray along the normal slows down but does not bend.

Into water

\(n = 1.33\) bends less than glass: a straw looks broken at the surface.

Glass to air

Going out into a faster medium, the ray bends away from the normal: \(\theta_2 > \theta_1\).

Total internal reflection

Beyond the critical angle (41.8° for glass to air) \(\sin\theta_2\) would exceed 1: no ray gets out, all light is reflected. This keeps light inside optical fibres.

Diamond

\(n = 2.42\): the critical angle is only 24°, so light entering a cut diamond bounces around inside before escaping: the sparkle.

The thin lens

What you are looking at

An object (arrow), a lens with its focal points F, three principal rays, and the image they form.

A converging lens bends every ray parallel to the axis through the far focus, and lets a ray through the centre go straight. Two such rays from the tip of the object meet at the tip of the image. Where they meet depends on how far the object is.

$$\frac{1}{d_o} + \frac{1}{d_i} = \frac{1}{f}, \qquad m = -\frac{d_i}{d_o}$$
  1. Beyond 2F: smaller, inverted, real image (camera).
  2. At 2F: same size. Between F and 2F: bigger (projector).
  3. Inside F: the rays diverge; extended back they meet behind the lens — a virtual, upright, enlarged image (magnifying glass).

Worked example

f = 10 cm, object at 25 cm: 1/dᵢ = 1/10 − 1/25 → dᵢ = 16.7 cm, m = −0.67.

Common mistake

Sign conventions: a negative dᵢ means a virtual image on the object's side.

At twice the focal length

\(d_o = 2f \Rightarrow d_i = 2f\), \(m = -1\): real, inverted, same size.

Just beyond F

The image moves far away and grows: a projector puts the slide just beyond F.

Magnifier

Inside F the rays leave diverging: the eye sees an upright, larger, virtual image on the same side as the object.

Diverging lens

\(f < 0\): the image is always virtual, upright and smaller, as in glasses for short sight.

The concave mirror

What you are looking at

A concave mirror, its focus, and the image of an arrow traced with principal rays.

A concave mirror behaves like a converging lens folded back on itself: parallel rays reflect through the focus at half the radius of curvature. Same equation, same three regimes.

$$\frac{1}{d_o} + \frac{1}{d_i} = \frac{1}{f}, \qquad f = \frac{R}{2}$$

Worked example

Shaving mirror: you stand inside F and see yourself upright and enlarged. Telescope mirror: objects at infinity image at F.

At the centre of curvature

\(d_o = 2f = R\): the image is real, inverted, same size, at the same place.

Inside F

Upright and magnified behind the mirror: a shaving or make-up mirror.

Young's double slit

What you are looking at

Waves from two slits spreading and overlapping; the intensity pattern on the screen shows bright and dark fringes.

Each slit is a new source. At each point of the screen the two waves arrive with a path difference d sin θ. If it is a whole number of wavelengths they add (bright); if half a wavelength, they cancel (dark). The fringe spacing measures the wavelength — this is how light was proved to be a wave.

$$d\sin\theta = m\lambda \;(\text{bright}), \qquad \Delta y \approx \frac{\lambda L}{d}$$

Worked example

Red laser 650 nm, slits 0.25 mm apart, screen at 2 m: fringes 5.2 mm apart.

Common mistake

Thinking closer slits give closer fringes: it is the opposite.

Slits closer

Halving \(d\) doubles the fringe spacing \(\lambda L/d\).

Slits apart

Twice the separation, half the spacing: the fringes crowd together.

Longer wavelength

Fringe spacing grows with \(\lambda\): red light makes wider fringes than blue.
