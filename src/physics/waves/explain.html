Travelling waves

What you are looking at

A wave moving along a string; a marked point bobs up and down while the shape travels.

A wave moves a shape, not the material: each point of the string only goes up and down. In one period T the shape advances one wavelength λ, so speed = λ/T = fλ. The speed is set by the medium; change the frequency and the wavelength adjusts.

$$y(x, t) = A\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(x - vt)\right), \qquad v = f\lambda, \qquad k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda},\; \omega = 2\pi f$$

Worked example

Sound in air, 343 m/s: 440 Hz has λ = 0.78 m. Light: 3·10⁸ m/s, Wi-Fi at 2.4 GHz has λ = 12.5 cm.

Common mistake

Thinking the string moves along with the wave.

Half the wavelength

The speed is set by the string, not by the wave: with \(v\) fixed, halving \(\lambda\) doubles \(f\).

A faster wave

A tighter or lighter string carries waves faster (\(v = \sqrt{T/\mu}\)). Same \(\lambda\), so each point oscillates faster.

A pulse

Any shape travels unchanged: \(y = g(x - vt)\) for any function \(g\). A sine is just the simplest repeating one.

Superposition

What you are looking at

Two waves (thin lines) moving through each other and their sum (thick).

Where two waves overlap, the displacements simply add, and afterwards each continues as if the other had never been there. Same sign: they reinforce; opposite: they cancel. This one rule explains interference, beats and standing waves.

$$y = y_1 + y_2, \qquad \text{beats: } f_\text{beat} = |f_1 - f_2|$$

Worked example

Two tuning forks at 440 and 442 Hz: loudness swells twice a second. Noise-cancelling headphones play the opposite wave.

Common mistake

Thinking cancelled waves are destroyed: they reappear as soon as they separate.

Destructive interference

An upward and a downward pulse cancel exactly when they overlap: for an instant the string is flat. The energy is still there, as motion of the string.

A standing wave

Two equal sine waves going opposite ways add up to a pattern that does not travel: points that never move (nodes) every \(\lambda/2\).

$$A\sin(kx - \omega t) + A\sin(kx + \omega t) = 2A\sin(kx)\cos(\omega t)$$

Beats

Two waves of slightly different frequency drift in and out of step: loud, quiet, loud. The loudness swells at the difference frequency \(|f_1 - f_2|\), which is how musicians tune by ear.

Standing waves on a string

What you are looking at

A string fixed at both ends vibrating in mode n: nodes (still points) and antinodes (largest motion).

A wave reflected at the fixed ends meets itself coming back. Only waves that fit a whole number of half-wavelengths between the ends survive: the harmonics. They are what gives an instrument its pitch and timbre.

$$L = n\frac{\lambda_n}{2}, \qquad f_n = n\frac{v}{2L}, \qquad v = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}$$

Worked example

A guitar string 65 cm long tuned to 110 Hz: v = 2·0.65·110 = 143 m/s; harmonics at 220, 330, 440 Hz… Touching it lightly at the middle kills the odd harmonics.

Common mistake

Thinking nothing moves at a node "because the wave stopped": two travelling waves cancel there at every instant.

Second harmonic

One node in the middle, twice the frequency: an octave above the fundamental.

Third harmonic

Two nodes, three times the frequency: an octave and a fifth.

Two modes together

A real plucked string vibrates in many modes at once. Their mix gives the shape its corners and the instrument its timbre.

The Doppler effect

What you are looking at

A moving source emitting circular wavefronts: bunched up in front, spread out behind.

Each crest is emitted from where the source is at that moment. Moving forward, it catches up with its own waves: in front they are closer (higher pitch), behind they are further apart (lower pitch). At the wave speed the crests pile up into a barrier; faster, they form a cone — a sonic boom.

$$f_\text{front} = \frac{f}{1 - v_s/v}, \qquad f_\text{behind} = \frac{f}{1 + v_s/v}, \qquad \sin\alpha = \frac{v}{v_s}$$

Worked example

An ambulance at 30 m/s with a 700 Hz siren: 767 Hz approaching, 644 Hz going away. A jet at Mach 2: cone half-angle 30°.

Common mistake

Thinking the pitch keeps rising as the source approaches: it is constant while it approaches and drops suddenly as it passes.

At rest

Concentric circles: both observers hear the source frequency.

At the wave speed

The source keeps up with its own wavefronts: they pile up into a wall in front of it, the sound barrier.

Supersonic

Faster than its waves, the source leaves them behind inside a cone. The observer ahead hears nothing until the cone sweeps past: a sonic boom. The cone half-angle is \(\sin\theta = c/v_s = 1/M\).

About the model

Closed-form waves. Doppler signals use the emission time found by bisection on \(|\mathbf p - \mathbf s(t_e)| = c(t - t_e)\). Tests: opposite waves = standing wave (10⁻¹²), string mode frequencies and fixed ends, wavefront counting vs \(fc/(c \mp v)\) (10⁻⁶).
