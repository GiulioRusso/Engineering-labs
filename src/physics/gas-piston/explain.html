A gas in a cylinder

What you are looking at

Molecules bouncing inside a cylinder with a piston; their speed sets the colour. The p–V diagram on the right tracks the process; readouts give pressure, volume, temperature, work and heat.

Pressure is molecules hitting the walls; temperature is how fast they move. Heat the gas at constant pressure and it expands. Squeeze it slowly while it can exchange heat (isothermal) and the pressure rises as 1/V. Squeeze it fast or insulated (adiabatic) and the work you do heats it up — the pressure rises even more steeply.

$$pV = nRT, \qquad \text{isothermal: } pV = \text{const}, \qquad \text{adiabatic: } pV^\gamma = \text{const},\; TV^{\gamma - 1} = \text{const}$$
  1. First law: ΔU = Q − W. Heat in raises internal energy or does work.
  2. Isothermal: ΔU = 0, all the work becomes heat that leaves.
  3. Adiabatic: Q = 0, all the work becomes internal energy (T rises).

Worked example

Air (γ = 1.4) at 300 K compressed adiabatically to half the volume: T = 300·2^{0.4} = 396 K. To a quarter: 522 K — why diesel engines need no spark plug.

Common mistake

Using Celsius in pV = nRT: always kelvin.

Try it

Compare isothermal and adiabatic squeezes to the same volume on the p–V diagram: the adiabatic curve is steeper.

Heating at fixed volume

The hot bottom wall speeds up the atoms that touch it. Faster atoms hit harder and more often: the pressure rises in proportion to the temperature.

$$\frac{p}{T} = \frac{N k_B}{V} = \text{const}$$

Twice the gas

Twice the atoms, same temperature: twice the hits, twice the pressure.

Starting cold

The atoms start slow (speed \(\propto \sqrt T\)); the pressure starts at half.

Isothermal: heat leaves as fast as work comes in

The walls are held at fixed temperature. Compressing does not warm the gas: the work done flows out as heat. Pressure rises only because the atoms are crowded.

$$pV = nRT = \text{const}, \qquad W = nRT\ln\frac{V_1}{V_2}$$

Half the volume

Same temperature, half the room: twice the pressure.

A quarter of the volume

Four times the pressure; on the p–V diagram the path is a hyperbola.

Adiabatic: squeezing heats the gas

An atom bouncing off a piston that moves inward comes back faster, like a ball off a swung bat. With insulated walls the gas warms as it is compressed and cools as it expands.

$$p V^\gamma = \text{const}, \quad T V^{\gamma - 1} = \text{const}, \qquad \gamma = \tfrac53$$

To half

\(T_2/T_1 = 2^{2/3} \approx 1.59\): 300 K becomes about 476 K. The p–V curve is steeper than the isothermal one.

To a quarter

\(4^{2/3} \approx 2.52\): this is how a diesel engine ignites its fuel without a spark.

By hand

Drag the piston. A fast push heats the gas more than a slow one, which lets the walls take the heat away.

Speeds follow Maxwell–Boltzmann

$$f(v) = 4\pi\left(\frac{m}{2\pi k_B T}\right)^{3/2} v^2 e^{-m v^2/2k_BT}$$
About the model

Point atoms, elastic walls; the lid is a heavy moving wall (\(v' = 2u - v\)). Atom–atom collisions are modelled as isotropic scattering at \(10^5\) per second, keeping each speed, so energy is shared between directions as in a real gas. Tests: measured pressure vs \(NkT/V\) within 3 % (statistical); slow adiabatic halving of the volume heats by ×1.61 vs the ideal ×1.587.
