Kinetic theory: pressure and temperature

What you are looking at

500 argon atoms bouncing in a box with a piston on top, coloured by speed. The bottom plate heats and cools the gas. Left plot: the p–V diagram. Right plot: a histogram of the atoms' speeds against the Maxwell–Boltzmann curve.

A gas is a crowd of atoms flying in straight lines and bouncing off the walls. Each bounce pushes the wall a little; billions of bounces per second add up to a steady push per area: the pressure. Temperature is a measure of how fast the atoms move. Heat the gas and the atoms speed up, so they hit harder and more often: the pressure rises.

$$pV = N k_B T = nRT, \qquad \tfrac12 m\overline{v^2} = \tfrac32 k_B T, \qquad \bar v = \sqrt{\frac{8k_BT}{\pi m}}$$
  1. Count the atoms (N, or n moles) and the volume.
  2. Temperature in kelvin gives the average kinetic energy per atom, 3/2 k_BT.
  3. pV = Nk_BT gives the pressure; the readout compares it with the pressure measured from the wall hits.
  4. Speeds are spread out, not all equal: few slow, few very fast, the peak at √(2k_BT/m).

Worked example

0.01 mol of argon at 300 K in 1 litre: p = 0.01 · 8.314 · 300 / 0.001 = 24.9 kPa. Mean speed √(8k_BT/πm) = 399 m/s, root-mean-square √(3k_BT/m) = 433 m/s, most probable 353 m/s.

Common mistakes

Using Celsius in pV = nRT: always kelvin. Thinking all atoms move at the same speed: temperature fixes the average, the histogram shows the spread.

Try it

Watch the histogram while the plate heats the gas: the peak moves right and flattens, following the curve.

Heating at fixed volume

The hot bottom wall speeds up the atoms that touch it. Faster atoms hit harder and more often: the pressure rises in proportion to the temperature.

$$\frac{p}{T} = \frac{N k_B}{V} = \text{const}$$

Twice the gas

Twice the atoms, same temperature: twice the hits, twice the pressure.

Starting cold

The atoms start slower (speed \(\propto \sqrt T\): mean 282 m/s at 150 K instead of 399 m/s) and the pressure starts at half.

Isothermal compression

What you are looking at

The piston squeezes the gas slowly while the walls hold its temperature. On the p–V diagram the measured path follows the dashed isotherm; the dotted adiabat is there for comparison.

Squeeze a gas and you do work on it. If the walls are kept at a fixed temperature and you go slowly, that energy leaks out as heat as fast as you put it in: the atoms keep their speed. The pressure still rises, only because the same atoms are crowded into less room and hit the walls more often.

$$pV = nRT = \text{const}, \qquad W_{\text{on gas}} = nRT\ln\frac{V_1}{V_2}, \qquad \Delta U = 0,\; Q = -W_{\text{on gas}}$$
  1. First law: ΔU = Q + W_on. At constant T an ideal gas keeps its internal energy, so ΔU = 0.
  2. All the work you do leaves as heat through the walls.
  3. Pressure goes as 1/V: a hyperbola on the p–V diagram.

Worked example

0.01 mol at 300 K, 1 litre, 24.9 kPa, squeezed to half: p = 49.9 kPa, work W = nRT ln 2 = 17.3 J, all of it given off as heat. To a quarter: 99.8 kPa, 34.6 J.

Common mistake

"Isothermal means no heat." The opposite: heat flows the whole time, that is what keeps T fixed. The process with no heat is the adiabatic one.

Try it

Read the pV/start readout during the squeeze: it stays at 1.

A quarter of the volume

Four times the pressure; on the p–V diagram the path runs further up the hyperbola.

Adiabatic compression

What you are looking at

The same squeeze with insulated walls: no heat in or out. Watch the colours warm up as the piston comes down; on the p–V diagram the path follows the steeper adiabat.

An atom bouncing off a piston that moves inward comes back faster, like a tennis ball off a racket swung into it. With insulated walls nothing takes that energy away, so the gas warms as it is compressed and cools as it expands. Hotter and crowded: the pressure rises faster than for the isothermal squeeze.

$$Q = 0,\qquad pV^\gamma = \text{const}, \qquad TV^{\gamma-1} = \text{const}, \qquad \gamma = \frac{c_p}{c_v} = \frac53 \text{ (argon)}$$
  1. First law with Q = 0: all the work goes into internal energy, ΔU = W_on.
  2. For a monatomic gas U = 3/2 nRT, so T rises.
  3. Use TV^(γ−1) = const for the new temperature, then pV^γ = const for the pressure.

Worked example

Argon at 300 K and 24.9 kPa, squeezed to half the volume: T = 300 · 2^(2/3) = 476 K, p = 24.9 · 2^(5/3) = 79.2 kPa (the isothermal squeeze gave 49.9). The work is all stored: ΔU = 3/2 nRΔT = 22.0 J.

Common mistake

Using γ = 1.4 for every gas. That is for air (two-atom molecules); single atoms like argon have γ = 5/3 and heat up more.

Try it

Compare with the Isothermal case at the same squeeze: same volume, higher temperature, higher pressure.

To a quarter

\(T_2/T_1 = 4^{2/3} \approx 2.52\): 300 K becomes 756 K. A diesel engine squeezes air (γ = 1.4) about 18 times, enough to light the fuel without a spark.

By hand

Drag the piston. With nothing holding the temperature, every push in heats the gas and every pull out cools it; push hard and fast for a big jump.

About the model

Point atoms, elastic walls; the lid is a heavy moving wall (\(v' = 2u - v\)). Atom–atom collisions are modelled as isotropic scattering at \(10^5\) per second, keeping each speed, so energy is shared between directions as in a real gas. Tests: measured pressure vs \(NkT/V\) within 3 % (statistical); slow adiabatic halving of the volume heats by ×1.61 vs the ideal ×1.587; mean speed vs \(\sqrt{8kT/\pi m}\) within 3 %; the Maxwell–Boltzmann curve integrates to 1.
