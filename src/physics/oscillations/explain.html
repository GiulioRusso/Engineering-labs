Simple harmonic motion

What you are looking at

A mass on a spring with its force and velocity arrows. The plots show x(t) and the energy sloshing between kinetic and elastic.

The spring pulls back in proportion to how far it is stretched. Let go, and the mass is pushed back toward the middle, overshoots, and is pushed the other way. The result is a sine wave that repeats forever, because nothing takes energy away: it just moves from the spring into the motion and back.

$$m\ddot x = -kx, \qquad x(t) = x_0\cos\omega_0 t, \qquad \omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}, \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}$$
  1. Force: −kx, always pointing back to the middle, largest at the ends.
  2. Speed: largest in the middle, zero at the ends where the mass turns round.
  3. Energy: ½kx₀² at the start, all elastic; in the middle it is all kinetic, ½mv²; the total stays the same.
  4. The period depends on m and k only, not on how far you pull it.

Worked example

m = 1 kg, k = 10 N/m, pulled out 30 cm: ω₀ = √10 = 3.16 rad/s, T = 1.99 s. Energy ½·10·0.3² = 0.45 J, so the top speed is √(2·0.45/1) = 0.95 m/s, which is also ω₀x₀. With 4 kg: T = 3.97 s.

Common mistakes

Thinking the force is largest in the middle: there it is zero, and the speed is largest. Thinking a bigger swing takes longer: it goes further but also faster.

Try it

Change the pull-out distance and read the measured period: it does not move.

Four times the mass

More inertia, same pull: \(T \propto \sqrt m\), so the period doubles.

Four times stiffer

\(T \propto 1/\sqrt k\): half the period.

A bigger swing

Twice as far means twice the force, so it moves twice as fast: the period is the same (isochronism). The energy goes up four times, \(\tfrac12 k x_0^2\).

The pendulum

What you are looking at

A pendulum of length L, its angle over time, and the period compared with the small-angle formula.

Gravity pulls the bob back toward the bottom with a force proportional to sin θ. For small angles sin θ ≈ θ and the pendulum behaves like a spring: the period depends only on the length. At big angles the restoring force grows less than θ and the swing slows down.

$$\ddot\theta = -\frac{g}{L}\sin\theta, \qquad T_0 = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}, \qquad T \approx T_0\left(1 + \frac{\theta_0^2}{16}\right)$$

Worked example

L = 1 m: T₀ = 2.01 s. At θ₀ = 2 rad (115°) the period is about 30 % longer. A 4 m pendulum: T = 4 s.

Common mistake

A heavier bob does not swing faster: mass cancels out.

Try it

Make the pendulum 4× longer: the period doubles. Change the mass: nothing. Release it from a large angle: the period grows beyond the small-angle formula.

Four times longer

\(T \propto \sqrt L\): twice the period. A 1 m pendulum ticks about once a second each way.

Heavier bob

Weight and inertia both grow with \(m\) and cancel: the period does not depend on the mass (Galileo).

A big swing

At large angles \(\sin\theta < \theta\): the pull is weaker than the linear law, so the pendulum is slower. At 115° the period is about 30 % longer. The exact period uses the arithmetic–geometric mean:

$$T = \frac{2\pi\sqrt{L/g}}{\operatorname{AGM}\!\left(1, \cos\tfrac{\theta_0}{2}\right)}$$

Almost over the top

Released near the top, it lingers there before falling: the period grows without limit as \(\theta_0 \to 180°\). The motion is far from a sine wave.

Air friction

The swing keeps its rhythm but shrinks as \(e^{-\gamma t}\) (dashed envelope): energy goes into the air.

Pushed in rhythm

Small pushes at the pendulum's own frequency \(\sqrt{g/L}\) add up swing after swing, as on a playground swing. The amplitude grows until friction removes energy as fast as the pushes add it.

Damped oscillations

What you are looking at

The same mass and spring with friction proportional to the speed. The dashed curves on x(t) are the envelope ±x₀e^(−γt); the energy plot runs downhill.

A real spring does not swing forever: air and internal friction take a little energy each cycle. When the friction force is proportional to the speed, the swing keeps almost the same rhythm but its size shrinks by the same fraction every second: an exponential decay. With strong enough friction it no longer swings at all.

$$\ddot x + 2\gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2 x = 0, \qquad x \approx x_0 e^{-\gamma t}\cos\omega_1 t, \quad \omega_1 = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2}$$
  1. Compare γ with ω₀. γ < ω₀: underdamped, it oscillates and shrinks.
  2. γ = ω₀: critically damped, the fastest return with no overshoot.
  3. γ > ω₀: overdamped, it creeps back without crossing zero.
  4. The energy falls twice as fast as the amplitude, as e^(−2γt), because it goes as x².

Worked example

ω₀ = 3.16 rad/s, γ = 0.08 /s: ω₁ = 3.161 rad/s, the period hardly changes. After 10 s the amplitude is e^(−0.8) = 0.45 of the start; it drops below 1 % after ln(100)/γ ≈ 58 s. Critical damping (γ = 3.16 /s) gets within 1 % in 2.1 s; heavy overdamping (γ = 8 /s) needs 7.1 s.

Common mistake

Thinking more damping always settles faster. Past critical damping it gets slower again: the friction holds the mass back too.

Try it

Slide γ up from 0 to 8 and watch the overshoot disappear at γ = ω₀.

Critical damping

\(\gamma = \omega_0\): the fastest return to rest without overshooting. Car suspensions and door closers are tuned close to this.

Overdamped

\(\gamma > \omega_0\): no oscillation at all, it creeps back slowly, as if moving through honey.

Driven oscillations and resonance

What you are looking at

The mass starts at rest and is pushed by a force F₀cos(ω_d t). Left plot: x(t), a start-up transient then a steady swing. Right plot: the steady amplitude for every drive frequency, with the cursor at the current one.

Push a damped oscillator periodically. At first its own motion and the forced motion mix; the own motion dies away (that is the damping) and what remains swings at the drive frequency, not its own. How big that swing is depends on how close the drive is to the natural frequency: pushes in step with the natural swing add up, and only the damping stops them growing.

$$\ddot x + 2\gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2 x = \frac{F_0}{m}\cos\omega_d t, \qquad A = \frac{F_0/m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2)^2 + (2\gamma\omega_d)^2}}, \qquad \tan\varphi = \frac{2\gamma\omega_d}{\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2}$$
  1. Wait for the transient to die out (a few times 1/γ).
  2. Below ω₀ the spring dominates: x ≈ F/k, in step with the push (φ ≈ 0).
  3. At ω₀ the spring and the inertia cancel: only damping limits A = F₀/(2mγω₀), and x lags by 90°, so the push is always along the velocity and always adds energy.
  4. Above ω₀ inertia dominates: small A, opposite to the push (φ → 180°).

Worked example

m = 1 kg, k = 10 N/m, γ = 0.2 /s, F₀ = 1 N. At ω_d = 1 rad/s: A = 0.111 m, lag 2.5° (the static stretch F₀/k is 0.1 m). At ω_d = ω₀ = 3.16 rad/s: A = 0.79 m, lag 90°. At ω_d = 7 rad/s: A = 0.026 m, lag 176°.

Common mistake

Thinking it oscillates at its own frequency once driven. In the steady state it follows the drive; ω₀ only sets how big the response is.

Try it

Sweep ω_d across ω₀ and read the lag in the readout: 0°, then 90° at the peak, then towards 180°.

Driven slowly

At ω_d = 1 rad/s, about three times below ω₀: the mass simply follows the force, stretched by about F₀/k, almost in phase.

Far above resonance

The mass cannot keep up: tiny amplitude, moving opposite to the push (180° out of phase).

Less damping

The peak grows taller and narrower: its width is about \(2\gamma\). This is how a radio tunes to one station, and how soldiers marching in step can shake a bridge.

About the model

RK4 at \(\Delta t = 2\) ms over 20 s. Tests: spring period vs \(2\pi\sqrt{m/k}\) (10⁻⁵) and energy (10⁻⁹); pendulum at 115° vs the AGM period (10⁻⁵); damped envelope vs \(e^{-\gamma t}\) (2 %); steady driven amplitude vs the resonance formula at three frequencies (0.2 %), and the measured phase lag vs the formula (within 0.6°).
