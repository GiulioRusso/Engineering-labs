The field of a point charge

What you are looking at

A point charge (draggable), its electric field lines, and a colour slice of |E| with equipotential contours.

A charge changes the space around it: put a small positive test charge anywhere and it feels a push away from a positive charge, or a pull towards a negative one. That push per unit charge is the electric field. It spreads out in all directions, so it weakens with the area of a sphere, as 1/r². The potential V is the energy per unit charge; it falls off only as 1/r.

$$\mathbf E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^2}\hat{\mathbf r}, \qquad V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r}, \qquad \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 8.99\cdot10^9\ \text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2$$
  1. Field lines point the way a positive test charge would be pushed: out of +, into −.
  2. Where lines crowd, the field is strong; their number is proportional to the charge.
  3. Equipotentials are spheres around the charge, crossing the lines at right angles: moving along them costs no work.

Worked example

2 nC at 10 cm: E = 8.99·10⁹ · 2·10⁻⁹ / 0.1² = 1798 V/m, V = 180 V. At 20 cm the field is four times weaker (449 V/m), the potential only twice (90 V).

Common mistake

Thinking a field line is the path a charge follows. It gives the direction of the force, not of the velocity.

Try it

Make the charge negative: same picture, arrows reversed.

Negative

Same pattern, arrows reversed: the lines come in from far away and end on the charge.

More charge

2.5 times the charge, 2.5 times as many lines and a field 2.5 times stronger everywhere.

Two charges: superposition

What you are looking at

A +2 nC and a −2 nC charge 30 cm apart, their combined field lines and the |E| slice with equipotentials.

Each charge makes its own field as if the other were not there; the total field is their vector sum at every point. That is superposition. For opposite charges the lines leave the + charge and land on the −; between them both fields point the same way and add up.

$$\mathbf E = \sum_i \frac{q_i}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r_i^2}\hat{\mathbf r}_i, \qquad V = \sum_i \frac{q_i}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r_i}$$
  1. Draw the field of each charge at the point.
  2. Add them as arrows (components), not as sizes.
  3. Potentials are plain numbers: just add them, signs included.

Worked example

Midway between +2 nC and −2 nC, 15 cm from each: each gives 8.99·10⁹ · 2·10⁻⁹/0.15² = 799 V/m towards the − charge, so E = 1598 V/m. The potential there is 0: +120 V − 120 V.

Common mistake

Concluding the field is zero where V = 0, or the other way round. Midway between opposite charges V = 0 but E is large; between like charges E = 0 but V is not.

Try it

Drag one charge closer: the lines bunch up between them and the field grows.

Like charges

The lines push away from each other. Midway the two fields cancel: a point of zero field, a saddle of the potential.

Unequal charges

The −1 nC charge can absorb only a third of the lines from the +3 nC one; the rest escape to infinity. From far away the pair looks like a single +2 nC charge.

A dipole

Seen from a distance much larger than the separation, the fields nearly cancel: a dipole field, fading as \(1/r^3\): from 20 to 40 cm it drops from 169 to 22 V/m, almost 8 times.

Four charges: a quadrupole

What you are looking at

Four charges on the corners of a square, alternating + and −.

Superposition works for any number of charges. Here the total charge is zero, so from far away there is no 1/r² field; and the + and − pairs are arranged so that there is no net dipole either. What is left falls off even faster, as 1/r⁴: from a distance the four charges almost hide each other.

$$Q = \sum q_i = 0, \qquad \mathbf p = \sum q_i\mathbf r_i = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; E \propto \frac{1}{r^4} \text{ far away}$$
  1. Total charge sets the far field (1/r²).
  2. If it is zero, the dipole moment sets it (1/r³).
  3. If that is zero too, the quadrupole (1/r⁴).

Worked example

At the centre the four fields cancel by symmetry: E = 0, and V = 0 too (equal distances, charges summing to 0).

Common mistake

Thinking zero total charge means zero field everywhere: close to the charges the field is strong.

Try it

Change one charge: the far field becomes a dipole or a point-charge field again.

All positive

+8 nC in total: from far away, the field of a single charge at the centre, where the four fields cancel.

Gauss's law

What you are looking at

A +2 nC charge inside a sphere (the Gauss surface) and a −1 nC charge outside. The readouts give the flux through the sphere, times ε₀, and the charge inside.

Count how much field flows out through a closed surface: that is the flux. For one charge at the centre of a sphere the field is the same all over the surface, and the flux is E × area = q/ε₀, whatever the radius. Move the charge, deform the surface, add charges outside: the flux still counts only the charge inside. A charge outside sends as much field in on one side as out on the other.

$$\Phi = \oint \cE{\mathbf E}\cdot d\mathbf A = \frac{Q_\text{inside}}{\varepsilon_0}$$
  1. Pick a closed surface.
  2. Add up the charges inside it, ignoring those outside.
  3. Flux = Q_inside/ε₀; with symmetry (sphere, cylinder, plane) this gives E directly.

Worked example

Inside: +2 nC, so Φ = 2·10⁻⁹ / 8.85·10⁻¹² = 226 V·m (flux·ε₀ = 2 nC). With both charges inside: 1 nC, 113 V·m.

Common mistake

Concluding E = 0 on the surface when the flux is 0: the field can go in and out at different places.

Try it

Drag the −1 nC charge into the sphere: the flux drops from 2 to 1 nC the moment it crosses.

Both inside

The flux counts \(+2 - 1 = +1\) nC, however the charges are arranged inside the sphere.

One charge

Remove the outside charge: the field lines change, the flux does not.
