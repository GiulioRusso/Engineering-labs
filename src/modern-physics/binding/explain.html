Binding energy, fission and fusion

What you are looking at

Left: the binding energy per nucleon B/A for the most stable nucleus of each mass number, from the semi-empirical mass formula (blue), and measured values for a few nuclei (orange). The bright dot is the chosen A; the green arrows point uphill from both ends. Top right: the formula's five terms for that nucleus, per nucleon. Bottom right: the energy released by splitting a nucleus into two halves. Below: the total binding energy.

A nucleus weighs less than its protons and neutrons separately. The missing mass, times c², is the binding energy, the work needed to pull it apart. Per nucleon it rises steeply through the light nuclei, peaks near iron and nickel at about 8.8 MeV, and falls slowly toward uranium. The semi-empirical mass formula explains the shape with a liquid-drop picture. Every nucleon binds to its neighbours (volume), those on the surface have fewer neighbours (surface), protons repel each other (Coulomb), unequal numbers of protons and neutrons cost energy (asymmetry), and pairs bind a little better (pairing). Moving toward the peak releases energy: splitting heavy nuclei is fission, joining light ones is fusion.

$$B = a_V A - a_S A^{2/3} - a_C\frac{Z(Z-1)}{A^{1/3}} - a_A\frac{(A-2Z)^2}{A} \pm \delta, \qquad \Delta m = \frac{B}{c^2},\quad 1\ \text{u}\,c^2 = 931.5\ \text{MeV}$$
  1. Volume grows as A, surface as A^(2/3): small nuclei are mostly surface and weakly bound.
  2. Coulomb repulsion grows as Z²/A^(1/3): it wins for heavy nuclei and pulls B/A down.
  3. Q of a reaction = binding energy after − binding energy before.

Worked example

Uranium-235 at 7.59 MeV per nucleon splits into fragments near A = 118 at about 8.5 MeV: (8.5 − 7.6) × 235 ≈ 200 MeV per fission, about 10⁷ times a chemical reaction. D + T → ⁴He + n: 28.30 − 2.22 − 8.48 = 17.6 MeV.

Common mistakes

Thinking a larger binding energy means more energy stored in the nucleus. It is the energy already given off when it formed; tightly bound nuclei are the low-energy end state.

Try it

Slide A from 2 to 250 and watch the five bars: surface dominates the losses for light nuclei, Coulomb for heavy ones, and the curve peaks where the two balance.

The top of the curve

⁶²Ni and ⁵⁶Fe are the most tightly bound nuclei, about 8.79 MeV per nucleon. Stars fuse light elements up to here; anything heavier needs energy put in.

Uranium splits

Splitting A = 236 into two halves releases about 200 MeV by the formula. Fission is not spontaneous, though: the nucleus must first be deformed over a barrier, which a captured neutron provides.

Light nuclei join

⁴He is exceptionally tightly bound for its size (7.07 MeV per nucleon, against 1.1 for deuterium), so joining deuterium and tritium into helium releases 17.6 MeV. The liquid-drop formula is poor for such light nuclei; the measured dots tell the truth.
