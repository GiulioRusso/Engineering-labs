Time dilation and the light clock

What you are looking at

Two identical light clocks: a flash of light bouncing between two mirrors, one tick per round trip. The left clock is at rest in the lab; the right one rides on a ship moving at β = v/c (the posts underneath are the lab sliding past). Top right: the slowing factor γ. Bottom right: the ticks each clock has counted.

Light travels at the same speed c for every observer: that is the starting point of relativity. Seen from the lab, the light in the moving clock does not go straight up and down: the mirrors move while it travels, so it runs along a longer diagonal path. Same speed, longer path: each tick takes longer. The moving clock runs slow, and not just this one: every process on the ship, from wristwatches to heartbeats, slows by the same factor.

$$\left(\frac{c\,\Delta t}{2}\right)^2 = L^2 + \left(\frac{v\,\Delta t}{2}\right)^2 \;\Rightarrow\; \Delta t = \frac{2L/c}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}} = \gamma\,\Delta\tau, \qquad \gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \beta^2}}$$
  1. At rest: the light goes up and down, 2L, one tick Δτ = 2L/c.
  2. Moving: the light runs the hypotenuse; Pythagoras with the same c gives Δt.
  3. Ratio Δt/Δτ = γ, always ≥ 1, growing without limit as β → 1.

Worked example

β = 0.6: γ = 1/√(1 − 0.36) = 1.25. While the lab clock ticks 5 times, the moving one ticks 4. At β = 0.99, γ = 7.09.

Common mistake

Thinking the ship's crew sees their own clock slow down. On board everything is normal; it is the lab observer who measures the slowing (and the crew says the same of the lab's clocks).

Try it

Push β towards 0.99 and watch the moving photon crawl along its long zigzag.

At 0.99 c

\(\gamma = 7.09\): one tick of the moving clock lasts seven lab ticks. Muons made high in the atmosphere live long enough to reach the ground only because of this.

At 0.1 c

\(\gamma = 1.005\): half a percent, and that is already 30 000 km/s. At everyday speeds the effect is billions of times smaller, which is why we never notice it (GPS satellites do have to correct for it).

The twin paradox

What you are looking at

A spacetime diagram: distance across, Earth time up. The Earth twin stays at x = 0 (blue); the traveller (orange) flies at 0.8 c to a star 8 light-years away and comes back. Dots mark each twin's birthdays on their own clock; the gold dashed line is a light ray.

On each leg the traveller's clock runs slow by γ, as seen from Earth. So on return the traveller is younger. The "paradox": the traveller could say Earth was moving, so Earth's twin should be younger. But the two are not equal: only the traveller turns round, jumping from the outgoing to the returning frame. In spacetime the stay-at-home path is the straight line, and between two events the straight path is the one that collects the most time.

$$T_\text{Earth} = \frac{2D}{v}, \qquad T_\text{traveller} = \frac{T_\text{Earth}}{\gamma}$$
  1. Earth time for the round trip: distance there and back over speed.
  2. The traveller's own (proper) time: divide by γ.
  3. Count the dots on each worldline: those are the birthdays each twin celebrates.

Worked example

D = 8 ly, β = 0.8, γ = 5/3: Earth waits 2 · 8/0.8 = 20 years; the traveller ages 20/(5/3) = 12 years. They part as twins and meet 8 years apart in age.

Common mistake

Putting the asymmetry in the acceleration's size. The turn can be as quick as you like; what matters is that the traveller's worldline is bent and Earth's is straight.

Try it

Raise β towards 0.99: the trip takes Earth just over 16 years, the traveller little more than 2.

At 0.5 c

32 years on Earth, 27.7 for the traveller (\(\gamma = 1.155\)): a gap of 4 years.

Spacetime diagrams and simultaneity

What you are looking at

Lab axes (grey), light rays at 45° (gold), and the axes and grid of a frame moving at β (orange). Events A and B happen at the same lab time, ct = 1, two light-years apart. The red and green dashed lines are the moving frame's lines of "same time" through A and through B.

In the lab, A and B are simultaneous. But "at the same time" means "on a line of constant time", and a moving observer's lines of constant time are tilted: their time axis leans towards the light ray, and their space axis leans up by the same angle, keeping the light ray halfway between them. Through A and B those tilted lines are different, so for the moving observer the two events are not simultaneous. Which one comes first depends on the direction of motion.

$$t' = \gamma\left(t - \frac{vx}{c^2}\right), \qquad x' = \gamma(x - vt), \qquad c^2t'^2 - x'^2 = c^2t^2 - x^2$$
  1. The ct' axis (x' = 0) is the line x = βct; the x' axis (t' = 0) is ct = βx.
  2. Apply the Lorentz transformation to A and B.
  3. The time difference is −γβΔx/c: zero only if the events are at the same place or β = 0.

Worked example

β = 0.6, γ = 1.25: A at (ct, x) = (1, −1) becomes ct' = 1.25 (1 + 0.6) = 2; B at (1, 1) becomes ct' = 1.25 (1 − 0.6) = 0.5. B happens 1.5 years before A for the moving observer.

Common mistake

Thinking this can reverse cause and effect. It cannot for events that light could connect (inside each other's light cone): their order is the same in every frame. Only events too far apart for any signal can swap.

Try it

Slide β through 0: the order of A and B swaps exactly there.

The other way

At \(\beta = -0.6\) the roles swap: now A is at ct' = 0.5 and B at 2.

At 0.9 c

Both orange axes fold towards the light line: \(t'_A = 4.36\), \(t'_B = 0.23\). Every frame sees light at 45°: that is the constancy of c, drawn.

About the model

Units with c = 1 (years and light-years). Tests: the interval \(t^2 - x^2\) is unchanged by 200 random boosts (10⁻¹²); two boosts equal one at the relativistic sum of the speeds, always below c; the light-clock tick from the geometry of the diagonal (bisection) equals \(\gamma\) times the rest tick (10⁻¹²); twins at 0.8 c and 8 ly: 20 and 12 years; A and B transform as in the worked example.
