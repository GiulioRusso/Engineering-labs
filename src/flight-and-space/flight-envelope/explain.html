Lift and drag

What you are looking at

A light aircraft (1000 kg, 16.2 m² of wing) in level flight with its four forces, the airflow and the angle of attack α. The plots show the lift curve and the drag polar, with the current point.

A wing turns the air downward; the air pushes the wing up. The lift grows with the air density, the wing area, the square of the speed, and the angle of attack. In level flight the lift must equal the weight, so at each speed there is exactly one angle that holds the aircraft up: slow means nose up, fast means nose down. Producing lift costs drag, and drag is what the engine pays for.

$$L = \tfrac12\rho V^2 S\,C_L(\alpha) = W, \qquad D = \tfrac12\rho V^2 S\,C_D, \qquad C_D = C_{D0} + kC_L^2$$
  1. From L = W find the needed C_L = 2W/(ρV²S).
  2. The lift curve gives the angle for that C_L.
  3. The drag polar gives C_D: a fixed friction part C_D0 plus the induced part kC_L², the price of lift.
  4. Friction drag grows with V², induced drag falls as 1/V²: the best lift-to-drag ratio is where they are equal.

Worked example

At 1000 m (ρ = 1.11 kg/m³) and 55 m/s: C_L = 0.36, α = 2.2°, drag 923 N, L/D = 10.6. At 30 m/s: C_L = 1.21, α = 12.0°, L/D = 11.6. The best L/D is 13.2 at C_L = 0.71.

Common mistake

Thinking flying faster always costs more drag. Below the best-L/D speed, slowing down raises the drag, because the induced part grows.

Try it

Sweep the airspeed and watch the angle of attack and the drag readout: the drag is smallest in the middle.

Slow flight

Half the speed needs four times \(C_L\): the nose comes up, close to the stall. Induced drag (\(\propto C_L^2\)) dominates.

Fast flight

A small \(C_L\) is enough; now friction drag \(C_{D0}\) dominates. The best glide (largest \(L/D\)) is in between, where the two drags are equal.

Thinner air

At 4000 m the air is about 33 % less dense: the same lift needs more speed or more angle of attack, and the stall speed rises from 26.9 to 31.4 m/s.

Turning flight and the load factor

What you are looking at

The aircraft banked by φ in a level turn. The lift tilts with the wings; the V–n plot shows the speeds and load factors where flight is possible.

To turn, the aircraft banks so that part of the lift pulls it sideways, round the turn. The other part must still carry the weight, so the total lift has to grow: by the load factor n = 1/cos φ. Everything on board feels n times heavier. More lift from the same wing needs a bigger angle of attack, so the stall comes sooner: the stall speed grows by √n.

$$L\cos\varphi = W, \quad L\sin\varphi = \frac{mV^2}{r} \;\Rightarrow\; n = \frac{L}{W} = \frac{1}{\cos\varphi}, \qquad r = \frac{V^2}{g\tan\varphi}, \qquad V_s = \sqrt{\frac{2nW}{\rho S C_{L\max}}}$$
  1. Bank angle → load factor n = 1/cos φ.
  2. Needed C_L is n times the level value: check it is below C_Lmax.
  3. Turn radius from the sideways part of the lift: r = V²/(g tan φ).

Worked example

30° bank at 55 m/s: n = 1.15, α = 2.8°, radius 534 m. 60° bank at 50 m/s: n = 2, α = 8.1°, radius 147 m. The level stall speed of 26.9 m/s becomes 38.1 m/s at 60° bank.

Common mistake

Thinking the stall speed is a fixed number. It depends on the load factor (and the altitude and weight): the same aircraft stalls at 38 m/s in a steep turn.

Try it

At 60° bank, lower the speed until the readout says it stalls.

A 60° bank

\(n = 1/\cos 60° = 2\): the wing must carry twice the weight, so the stall speed rises by \(\sqrt 2\).

Too slow

At 60° bank \(n = 2\), so the stall speed is \(\sqrt2 \approx 1.41\) times the level value, 38.1 m/s: 34 m/s is below it. Turning steeply at low speed is how many stalls happen.

The stall

What you are looking at

The angle of attack set by hand at 30 m/s, here 22°. The airflow leaves the upper surface; on the lift curve the point has fallen past the peak.

Up to a critical angle, tilting the wing further gives more lift. Beyond it (about 15° here) the air can no longer follow the curved upper surface: it breaks away in eddies. The lift drops instead of rising, and the drag jumps. That is the stall. It is set by the angle, not the speed: at any speed, past the critical angle, the wing stalls.

$$C_L \approx a(\alpha - \alpha_0) \text{ for } \alpha < \alpha_s, \qquad a = \frac{2\pi A}{A+2}, \qquad C_{L\max} = a(\alpha_s - \alpha_0)$$
  1. Below α_s: C_L grows linearly (slope a ≈ 4.9 per radian here).
  2. At α_s ≈ 15.3°: C_Lmax = 1.5.
  3. Above: C_L falls, the drag coefficient jumps; the aircraft sinks.
  4. Recovery: lower the nose to reduce α, then pull out gently.

Worked example

At 30 m/s: α = 15° gives C_L = 1.47, lift 1.22 × weight. At 22° (stalled) C_L = 1.00, lift only 0.83 × weight, and L/D drops from 10.4 to 6.2.

Common mistake

Thinking a stall means the engine stopped: it is the wing, and it happens at an angle, at any speed.

Try it

Slide α up through 15° and watch the lift readout turn round.

Just below

Near the top of the lift curve: the most lift the wing can give at this speed: at 15°, C_L = 1.47.

Negative angle

Below the zero-lift angle \(\alpha_0\) the wing pushes down.
