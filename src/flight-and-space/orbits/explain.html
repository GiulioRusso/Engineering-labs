Kepler orbits

What you are looking at

The Earth, a satellite's orbit in 3D, and the ground track (the point directly below the satellite) on the map.

An orbit is a fall that keeps missing the ground: the satellite moves sideways so fast that the Earth curves away as quickly as it falls. On a circle gravity supplies exactly the centripetal force. Further out gravity is weaker, so the satellite moves slower on a longer path: higher orbits take much longer.

$$\frac{GMm}{r^2} = \frac{mv^2}{r} \;\Rightarrow\; v = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r}}, \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{\mu}}, \qquad \mu_\oplus = 3.986\cdot10^{14}\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}^2$$
  1. Radius from the centre: r = R_E + altitude (R_E = 6378 km).
  2. Speed v = √(μ/r); period T = 2πr/v, which is Kepler's third law T² ∝ a³.
  3. For an ellipse use the semi-major axis a in T, and vis-viva v² = μ(2/r − 1/a) for the speed at any point.

Worked example

ISS altitude 420 km: r = 6798 km, v = 7.66 km/s, T = 93.0 min. Geostationary at 35 786 km: T = 23 h 56 min, one turn of the Earth, so it stays over one point.

Common mistake

"There is no gravity in orbit": at 400 km gravity is still 89 % of surface gravity. Astronauts float because they are falling with the station.

Try it

Raise the altitude and read the period: 8 times further out (in a) is 22.6 times longer.

Higher

Further out, gravity is weaker: slower speed, longer path, a period of several hours (\(T \propto a^{3/2}\)).

Geostationary

At 35 786 km the period equals one sidereal day: above the equator the satellite stays over one point, and its ground track is a dot.

An ellipse

Fast at perigee, slow at apogee: equal areas in equal times (Kepler's second law). The vis-viva equation gives the speed at any distance.

Molniya

\(e = 0.74\), \(i = 63.4°\), apogee over the north: the satellite hangs over Russia for most of its 12-hour orbit. Perigee 540 km, apogee 39 920 km, period 12.0 h. At 63.4° the bulge does not rotate the ellipse.

Inclination and ground tracks

What you are looking at

The ISS orbit, tilted 51.6° to the equator, and its ground track on the map: a wave that shifts west at every pass.

The orbit plane passes through the Earth's centre, tilted by the inclination i. The satellite goes north for half an orbit and south for the other half, reaching latitudes ±i. Meanwhile the Earth turns eastward under the plane, so each new pass crosses the equator further west.

$$|\text{latitude}| \le i \;(\text{or } 180° - i), \qquad \Delta\lambda = -\omega_E T, \quad \omega_E = 360°/23.93\,\text{h}$$
  1. Highest latitude = i (prograde, i < 90°) or 180° − i (retrograde).
  2. Westward shift per orbit = Earth's rotation during one period.
  3. Ascending node Ω: where it crosses the equator going north; it sets which longitudes the tracks start from.

Worked example

ISS: T = 93.0 min, so the Earth turns 360° · 93.0/1436 = 23.3° per orbit; about 15.5 orbits a day. Its tracks cover everything between 51.6° N and 51.6° S.

Common mistake

Thinking the orbit itself moves west. The orbit plane is fixed in space; the ground underneath turns.

Try it

Set i = 0°: the track collapses onto the equator. Set 90°: it covers the poles.

Polar orbit

\(i = 90°\): over both poles. At 700 km the period is 98.8 min and each pass lands 24.8° further west. As the Earth turns beneath, the satellite sees the whole planet: mapping and weather.

Retrograde

\(i > 90°\): the satellite moves westward, against the Earth's spin. Launching there costs more fuel.

The Earth's bulge (J2)

What you are looking at

An 800 km orbit tilted 98.6°, with the effect of the Earth's equatorial bulge switched on. Watch the nodes: the orbit plane slowly turns.

The Earth is not a perfect sphere: it is 21 km wider at the equator than pole to pole. The extra mass at the equator tugs on a tilted orbit, like a twist on a spinning top, and the orbit plane slowly precesses around the Earth's axis. Pick the inclination well and the plane turns exactly once a year, keeping step with the Sun.

$$\dot\Omega = -\frac32\, n\, J_2 \left(\frac{R_E}{p}\right)^2 \cos i, \qquad n = \sqrt{\mu/a^3},\; p = a(1-e^2),\; J_2 = 1.083\cdot10^{-3}$$
  1. cos i > 0 (prograde): the plane turns west. cos i < 0 (retrograde): east.
  2. Lower orbits feel it more: (R_E/p)² and n both grow.
  3. Sun-synchronous: choose i so that Ω̇ = 360°/365.24 days = +0.986° per day.

Worked example

800 km, i = 98.6°: Ω̇ = +0.985° per day, just what a sun-synchronous orbit needs. The ISS (420 km, 51.6°): −4.95° per day, a full turn in about 73 days.

Common mistake

Thinking J2 changes the period or the shape a lot. It mainly turns the orbit; the size and eccentricity stay put.

Try it

Move i towards 90°: the precession stops (cos i = 0).

Without the bulge

For a perfect sphere the orbit plane stays fixed in space.

The ISS

\(\cos i > 0\): the plane turns west, 4.95° a day.
