Hohmann and bi-elliptic transfers

What you are looking at

The starting and target circular orbits, the transfer ellipse(s), the burns (Δv arrows), and the fuel needed from the rocket equation.

To go to a higher orbit, speed up: that raises the opposite side of the orbit. A Hohmann transfer is an ellipse touching both circles — one burn to enter it, one to leave it at the top. For very distant targets (r₂/r₁ > 11.9) going even further out and coming back (bi-elliptic) can cost less Δv, but takes much longer.

$$\Delta v_1 = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r_1}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2r_2}{r_1 + r_2}} - 1\right), \quad \Delta v_2 = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r_2}}\left(1 - \sqrt{\frac{2r_1}{r_1 + r_2}}\right), \qquad \frac{m_0}{m_f} = e^{\Delta v / (I_{sp} g_0)}$$
  1. Each burn is a change of speed Δv; the total Δv is the mission's price.
  2. The rocket equation turns Δv into fuel: the fuel needed grows exponentially with Δv.

Worked example

LEO (300 km) to GEO: Δv₁ = 2.43 km/s, Δv₂ = 1.46 km/s, total 3.89 km/s, 5.3 h. With Isp = 320 s, m₀/m_f = e^{3890/3139} = 3.45: 1 t dry needs 2.45 t of propellant.

Common mistake

Thinking higher orbits need more speed: you burn to get there, but you end up slower.

A small hop

A modest change in radius costs a few hundred m/s.

To the Moon's distance

Almost all the Δv is the first burn (escaping low orbit); arriving is cheap because the spacecraft is slow up there. The trip takes about 5 days.

A far target

Note the total, then compare with the bi-elliptic case for the same target.

Better engine

Same Δv, but a higher \(I_{sp}\) needs much less propellant: the rocket equation is exponential.

Going further to arrive cheaper

Overshoot to a distant apoapsis \(r_b\), where the spacecraft is slow and changing the orbit is cheap, then fall back to the target. It saves fuel only when the target is more than about 12 times further out, and it takes much longer.

Not worth it for GEO

\(r_2/r_1 \approx 6\): three burns cost more than Hohmann's two.

The rocket equation

Each burn costs propellant exponentially: the rocket must also accelerate the fuel it has not burned yet.

$$\Delta v = I_{sp}\, g_0 \ln\frac{m_0}{m_f}, \qquad m_\text{prop} = m_\text{dry}\left(e^{\Delta v / I_{sp} g_0} - 1\right)$$

Bi-elliptic

Overshoot to \(r_b\), far beyond the target, where the spacecraft is slow and changing the orbit is cheap; then fall back. For \(r_2/r_1 = 15\) the three burns cost less than Hohmann's two, at the price of a much longer trip.
