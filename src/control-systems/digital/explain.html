Sampling and the z-plane

What you are looking at

Left: the z-plane, with the unit circle, the poles of the sampled system (red) and, dashed, the spiral each pole traces as the sample time T grows from 0. Top right: the same poles in the s-plane; the dashed lines at ±π/T mark how fast an oscillation the samples can still represent. Bottom right: the continuous step response (grey) and the samples taken every T (stems). Below: the size and angle of the z-pole against T.

A computer sees a system only at the sampling instants. Between two samples a mode e^{st} is multiplied by e^{sT}: in sampled time the pole s becomes the pole z = e^{sT}. The whole left half of the s-plane folds into the inside of the unit circle, the jω axis onto the circle itself, and the right half-plane outside it. The radius |z| = e^{σT} says how much a mode decays per sample, the angle ωdT how far it turns. Fast sampling crowds every pole near z = 1. Slow sampling can push the angle past π, and then the samples alias: they no longer show the real oscillation.

$$z = e^{sT}, \qquad |z| = e^{\sigma T},\ \ \angle z = \omega_d T, \qquad |z| < 1 \iff \sigma < 0$$
  1. Find the continuous poles s = σ ± jωd.
  2. Map each one: radius e^{σT}, angle ωdT.
  3. Stable if inside the circle; at least two samples per period (ωdT < π) to see the oscillation.

Worked example

ωn = 2 rad/s, ζ = 0.3: s = −0.6 ± 1.91j. With T = 0.3 s, z has radius e^(−0.18) = 0.84 and angle 0.57 rad: 11 samples per oscillation. With T = 1.65 s the angle reaches π and the pole sits on the negative real axis: the samples just alternate.

Common mistakes

Reading stability off the real part of z. In the z-plane the boundary is the circle |z| = 1, not the imaginary axis.

Try it

Raise T slowly: the red poles slide along their spirals toward the origin and the stems spread out. Then make ζ negative and see them leave the circle.

Fast sampling: poles near z = 1

T = 0.05 s: e^{sT} ≈ 1 + sT, so the z-poles hug the point 1. The samples trace the continuous curve closely.

T = π/ωd: the pole reaches −1

Exactly two samples per oscillation period: the z-poles meet on the negative real axis and the sampled response alternates up and down.

ζ < 0: outside the circle

A right-half-plane pole maps outside the unit circle at any sample time: sampling cannot stabilise an unstable system.

A controller in a computer

What you are looking at

Left: a motor's angle after a step command, with a PD controller in continuous time (grey) and the same controller computed every T seconds (blue). Top right: the control signal, continuous and held constant between samples (a staircase). Bottom right: the overshoot against the sample time. Below: the error in time.

A digital controller reads the sensor, computes, and holds its output until the next sample (a zero-order hold). Holding a staircase instead of a smooth signal delays the control by about half a sample on average, and a delay is pure phase lag: about ωT/2 radians at frequency ω. That lag eats the phase margin. A derivative is computed from two samples, which adds its own error. With fast sampling, the loop behaves like the continuous one. As T grows the overshoot rises, and at some point the loop goes unstable.

$$u(t) = u[n]\ \ \text{for}\ nT \le t < (n+1)T, \qquad u[n] = K_p e[n] + K_d\frac{e[n] - e[n-1]}{T}, \qquad \Delta\varphi \approx \frac{\omega T}{2}$$
  1. Design the controller in continuous time.
  2. Choose T 10–30 times faster than the closed-loop bandwidth.
  3. Check the extra phase lag ωcT/2 at the crossover against the phase margin.

Worked example

Kp = 4, Kd = 1 on 1/(s(s + 1)): 16 % overshoot in continuous time. Every 0.02 s: 17 %. Every 0.2 s: 24 %. Every 0.4 s: 43 %. At 0.8 s the loop is unstable.

Common mistakes

Treating the sample rate as a software detail. It is part of the loop dynamics: a controller that is fine at 1 kHz can oscillate at 10 Hz.

Try it

Raise the sample time from 0.005 to 1 s and follow the dot on the overshoot curve: it hugs the continuous value at first, then climbs steeply.

T = 0.02 s: like continuous

About 100 samples per second against a closed-loop bandwidth near 2 rad/s: the blue and grey curves are almost the same.

T = 0.4 s: more overshoot

The staircase is coarse, the hold adds phase lag, and the overshoot is nearly three times the continuous one.

T = 0.8 s: unstable

The controller reacts so late that it pushes in the wrong direction, and the oscillation grows.
