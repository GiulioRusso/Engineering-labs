Reading FFT bins

What you are looking at

Top left: the record. Top right: the FFT in its raw order — bins 0 … N−1 map to 0 … fs, and the upper half is really the negative frequencies. Bottom left: the same after fftshift, centred on DC. Bottom right: a zoom on the two tones, with zero padding (line) and without (dots).
$$\Delta f = \frac{f_s}{N} = \frac{1}{T}$$
  1. Bin k is the frequency k·fs/N. Bin N/2 is fs/2; bins above it are −fs/2 … 0 (they wrapped around).
  2. fftshift just swaps the two halves so that zero is in the middle.
  3. Two tones closer than 1/T fall into one main lobe: only observing for longer separates them.
  4. Zero padding (appending zeros) evaluates the same spectrum on a finer grid — a smoother curve, not a sharper one.

Worked example

fs = 1 kHz, T = 0.128 s: Δf = 7.8 Hz. Tones at 100 and 118 Hz (18 Hz apart) just separate. To resolve 1 Hz you need 1 s of data, whatever fs is.

Common mistakes

Reading bin 120 of a 128-point FFT as a high frequency (it is −8 bins). Believing zero padding improves resolution: it only interpolates.

Try it

Bring the tones closer than 1/T and lengthen T until they split again.

Observe longer

T = 0.512 s: Δf = 2 Hz; the two tones are now many bins apart and clearly separate.

Zero padding ×8

The curve is smooth and shows the true lobe shape between the original bins (dots), but the two lobes are just as wide as before.

A window to find a weak tone

What you are looking at

Top left: the record (and window). Top right: linear spectra, rectangular vs Hamming. Bottom: the same in dB, where the weak tone (dashed) is visible only if it rises above the strong tone's sidelobes.

A strong tone not centred on a bin spreads sidelobes across the spectrum: −13 dB, then falling only 6 dB per octave. A tone 40 dB weaker 18 Hz away is buried. The Hamming window pushes sidelobes below −43 dB, at the price of a main lobe twice as wide.

$$w[n] = 0.54 - 0.46\cos\frac{2\pi n}{N-1}$$

Worked example

100 Hz at amplitude 1, 118 Hz at 0.01 (−40 dB), 7.8 Hz bins: 2.3 bins apart. Rectangular sidelobes there ≈ −19 dB: the weak tone is invisible. Hamming's sidelobes are near −43 dB: the weak tone just clears them as a separate peak.

Common mistake

Windowing when you need to separate two equal tones very close together: the wider main lobe can merge them. Choose the window for the job.

Try it

Lower tone 2 to −60 dB: Hamming fails, Blackman (−58 dB) only just copes.

No window

The weak tone sits under the strong tone's sidelobes: you cannot see it.

Hamming

Sidelobes below −43 dB: the weak tone appears as its own peak.

Shifting a spectrum: RF to IF

What you are looking at

Top: the RF pulse and the I/Q result of multiplying it by a complex phasor. Bottom: the spectra before and after — everything moves down by fLO.
$$y(t) = x(t)\,e^{-j2\pi f_\text{LO}t} \;\Longleftrightarrow\; Y(f) = X(f + f_\text{LO})$$

Multiplying by a complex exponential is the frequency-domain version of a time delay: a pure shift. This is the mixer in every receiver.

Worked example

fc = 400 Hz, fLO = 300 Hz: the +400 lobe lands at 100 Hz (IF), the −400 lobe at −700 Hz (to be filtered out).

Try it

Set fLO = 400: the wanted lobe goes to DC and I becomes the envelope.

To DC

fLO = fc: zero-IF. The +fc lobe is now centred on 0 Hz.

Resampling

What you are looking at

Left: the original signal (tones at 50, 180, 320 Hz, sampled at 1 kHz) and its spectrum, with the new Nyquist frequency dashed. Right: the signal after keeping one sample in D, and its spectrum; red stems mark where tones above the new Nyquist fold to.

Throwing away samples lowers the rate to fs/D, and with it the Nyquist frequency to fs/2D. Anything above it folds back — unless it was removed first. Decimation is always two steps: low-pass at the new Nyquist, then keep every D-th sample.

Worked example

D = 4: new rate 250 Hz, Nyquist 125 Hz. 180 Hz folds to 70 Hz, 320 Hz to 70 Hz too (320 − 250). Without the filter, two fake tones appear at 70 Hz.

Common mistake

Decimating a clean-looking signal without filtering because "there is nothing up there" — noise is always up there, and it folds into your band.

Try it

Toggle the filter at D = 4 and watch the red stems vanish.

No filter

The 180 and 320 Hz tones reappear at 70 Hz: indistinguishable from a real 70 Hz signal.

With the filter

Only the 50 Hz tone survives, correctly placed.

From I/Q to amplitude, phase and frequency

What you are looking at

Top left: the I/Q point of a chirp pulse traced as time advances, with the current phasor. Top right: I, Q and the amplitude. Bottom left: the unwrapped phase. Bottom right: the instantaneous frequency, its derivative.
$$A = \sqrt{I^2 + Q^2}, \qquad \varphi = \text{atan2}(Q, I), \qquad f_i = \frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{d\varphi}{dt}$$

I and Q are the coordinates of a rotating arrow. Its length is the amplitude, its angle the phase, and how fast it turns is the frequency. A chirp turns faster and faster: its phase is a parabola, its frequency a straight line.

Worked example

Here fᵢ = −40 + 800t Hz: at 50 ms the arrow is momentarily still (fᵢ = 0), at 150 ms it turns at 80 Hz.

Common mistakes

Forgetting to unwrap: atan2 jumps from +π to −π every turn, and a naive derivative gives huge spikes. Trusting the phase where the amplitude is tiny: there noise rotates it at random.

With noise

The amplitude barely changes, but the instantaneous frequency becomes jumpy at the pulse's edges where the arrow is short.

Frequency estimation and the Cramér–Rao bound

What you are looking at

Top left: one noisy pulse. Top right: its spectrum near the peak, true frequency dashed, estimate green. Bottom left: histogram of 200 estimates. Bottom right: error against SNR — the bound (red), the interpolated estimator (green), the plain nearest bin (grey).

The strongest bin gives the frequency only to within a bin. Fitting a parabola through the peak and its neighbours (in log) locates the true peak between bins. How well can anyone do? The Cramér–Rao bound:

$$\sigma_f \ge \frac{f_s}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{6}{\text{SNR}\,N(N^2 - 1)}}$$

Worked example

N = 128 at 1 kHz, SNR 20 dB: bin width 7.8 Hz, bound 0.027 Hz. The interpolated estimator gets within a small factor; the nearest bin is stuck near 2 Hz (uniform within a bin).

Common mistake

Thinking resolution (1/T) limits accuracy. Resolution is about separating two tones; one tone's frequency can be measured far more precisely than a bin, given enough SNR.

Try it

Lower the SNR below 0 dB: the estimates break away from the bound (threshold effect).

0 dB

At low SNR the noise sometimes wins the peak search: occasional big errors push the measured spread far above the bound.

Four times the samples

N = 512: the bound improves by 4^{3/2} = 8 — longer observation is the best investment.

Finding plateaus

What you are looking at

Top left: a noisy signal with flat levels and ramps, smoothed. Top right: its smoothed derivative with the flatness threshold. Bottom left: the density of the flat samples' values, whose peaks are the levels. Bottom right: the plateaus found and their levels.
  1. Smooth first: a derivative amplifies noise, so fit a local quadratic (Savitzky–Golay) and differentiate the fit.
  2. Where |A′| is below a threshold, the signal is flat: that says when.
  3. Collect the values of the flat samples and estimate their density with a Gaussian kernel: each plateau makes a peak, which says at what level.
$$\hat p(x) = \frac{1}{Nh}\sum_i K\!\left(\frac{x - x_i}{h}\right)$$

Worked example

Levels 0.2, 1.0, 0.6 and 1.0 again: the derivative finds four flat stretches, the density only three peaks — the two 1.0 plateaus merge, as they should.

Common mistake

Choosing h too small: noise makes many little peaks. Too large: neighbouring levels merge. h should be comparable to the noise level.

More noise

A wider smoothing window keeps the derivative usable; edges of plateaus become less sharp.

A narrow kernel

h = 0.015: the density becomes spiky and false levels appear.
