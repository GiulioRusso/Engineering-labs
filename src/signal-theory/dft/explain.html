The transform is a correlation

What you are looking at

Top left: a signal with two tones (4 Hz and 9 Hz, blue) and a probe cosine at the test frequency f (orange). Bottom left: their product, green where positive, red where negative. Right: the spectrum |X(f)| drawn as the probe sweeps; the dot is the current value.
$$X(f) = \underbrace{\int x(t)\cos(2\pi ft)\,dt}_{\text{Re}} \;-\; j\underbrace{\int x(t)\sin(2\pi ft)\,dt}_{\text{Im}}$$
  1. Multiply the signal by a probe at the test frequency.
  2. If the signal contains that frequency, the two rise and fall together: the product is mostly positive, the area is large.
  3. If not, the product oscillates around zero: green and red cancel.
  4. Do it with a cosine and a sine (to catch any phase): that pair is \(e^{-j2\pi ft}\).

Worked example

At f = 4 Hz the 4 Hz tone (amplitude 1) times the cosine gives cos², whose average is 1/2: over the 2 s record the area is 1/2 × 2 = 1, while the 9 Hz part averages to zero. So |X(4)| = 1. The 9 Hz tone has amplitude 0.6, so |X(9)| = 0.6.

Common mistake

Using only the cosine. A tone shifted by a quarter period gives zero area with the cosine and full area with the sine: you need both, which is why X is complex.

Try it

Untick the automatic sweep and move f slowly through 4 Hz: the green takes over and the dot climbs.

On a tone

f = 4 Hz: the product is cos²-shaped, always above zero. Its area is the height of the spectral peak.

Between tones

f = 6.5 Hz: the product beats, positive and negative in turn, and the net area is nearly zero.

Winding the signal around a circle

What you are looking at

Top left: the samples x[n]. Right: each sample drawn as a point at distance x[n] from the origin, at an angle that turns f times around the circle during the record. The orange dot is the centre of mass of all the points. Bottom left: how far the centre of mass is from the origin, for every winding frequency.
$$X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\,e^{-j2\pi kn/N} = N\cdot\underbrace{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n} x[n]\,e^{-j2\pi kn/N}}_{\text{centre of mass}}$$

The factor \(e^{-j2\pi kn/N}\) is a unit arrow that turns clockwise; multiplying by it wraps the signal around the origin. When the winding speed matches a tone, every crest of the tone lands on the same side of the circle: the points bunch up and the centre of mass moves out. Otherwise the points spread evenly and it stays at the origin.

Worked example

48 samples, tones at bins 5 (amplitude 1) and 12 (0.6). Wound at 5: centre of mass 0.5 from the origin → |X[5]| = 48 × 0.5 = 24 = N/2 × amplitude. At 12: 0.3 → 14.4.

Common mistake

Expecting the centre of mass to equal the amplitude: a real tone is half a positive and half a negative frequency, so each side gets amplitude/2.

Try it

Let it sweep and watch the flower of points: it collapses into a lopsided loop at 5 and 12.

Wound at bin 5

The points pile up on the right: centre of mass at 0.5. That distance, times N, is the DFT magnitude.

Wound at 8.5

No tone near: the pattern is balanced and the centre of mass stays near the origin.

Wound at 0

No rotation: every point stays on the real axis and the centre of mass is the plain average of the signal — the DC value, X[0]/N.

Building the spectrum one bin at a time

What you are looking at

The DFT computed slowly: for k = 0, 1, 2, … the signal is wound exactly k turns (right) and the length of the centre of mass becomes the bar at bin k (bottom left).

The DFT only evaluates integer windings \(k = 0 \dots N-1\). These are special: a whole number of turns means that, for a tone at a different integer bin, the points are perfectly balanced. The bins are orthogonal; each one sees its own tone and nothing else.

  1. Bins 0 to N/2 are the frequencies 0 to fs/2, spaced fs/N.
  2. Bins above N/2 wind faster than half a turn per sample: on a sampled signal that is the same as winding backwards. They are the negative frequencies, mirrors of the first half.

Worked example

Tones at 6 and 17 with N = 48: bars at 6, 17, and at their mirrors 48 − 6 = 42 and 48 − 17 = 31. Everything else is exactly zero.

Common mistake

Reading bins above N/2 as high frequencies. For a real signal they repeat the first half (complex conjugates); spectrum plots usually show only 0 … N/2.

Try it

Watch bin 42 light up with the same height as bin 6.

Bin 6

Exactly six turns: the 6-cycle tone stacks up, the 17-cycle tone cancels perfectly. Bar height N/2 = 24.

Bin 42

42 turns per 48 samples is the same as −6: the mirror of bin 6. Same height, opposite phase.

The DFT samples the continuous transform

What you are looking at

Top: a rectangular pulse sampled N times over 4 s. Bottom: its continuous transform (a sinc, line) and the DFT values multiplied by Δt (dots). The dots sit on the line.
$$X(f_k) \approx \Delta t \cdot X[k], \qquad f_k = \frac{k}{N\Delta t} = \frac{k}{T}$$
  1. The record length T sets the spacing of the dots: Δf = 1/T.
  2. The sampling step Δt sets how far they reach: up to fs/2 = 1/(2Δt).
  3. The pulse sets the shape: zeros at multiples of 1/width.

Worked example

T = 4 s, N = 64: dots every 0.25 Hz up to 8 Hz. N = 256 in the same 4 s: still every 0.25 Hz, now up to 32 Hz. To get denser dots you need a longer record (or zero-padding, which interpolates).

Common mistake

Believing more samples always means finer frequency resolution. Resolution is 1/T; more samples in the same time only widen the range.

Try it

Narrow the pulse: the sinc spreads, the dots stay on it.

A narrow pulse

0.15 s: main lobe ±6.7 Hz. With only 64 samples the pulse is a couple of samples wide; the DFT still follows the sinc where it can.

More samples

256 samples over the same 4 s: the dots reach 32 Hz but are still 0.25 Hz apart.

Why the FFT is fast

What you are looking at

Left: the 8-point FFT as a network. Inputs (in bit-reversed order) enter on the left; each stage combines pairs with one multiplication (the "butterfly"); the highlighted lines are the chosen stage. Right top: number of multiplications for the direct DFT (N²) and the FFT ((N/2)log₂N). Right bottom: the two actually timed in your browser.

Split the sum into even and odd samples. Each half is itself a DFT of length N/2, and the two are joined with one "twiddle factor" per pair:

$$X[k] = E[k] + W_N^k\,O[k], \qquad X[k + N/2] = E[k] - W_N^k\,O[k], \qquad W_N = e^{-j2\pi/N}$$
  1. One multiplication \(W_N^k O[k]\) gives two outputs — the trick that halves the work at each level.
  2. Repeat the split until the DFTs have length 2: \(\log_2 N\) levels, N/2 butterflies each.
  3. Total: \((N/2)\log_2 N\) products instead of \(N^2\).

Worked example

N = 1024: DFT 1 048 576 products, FFT 5 120 — 205 times fewer. N = 1 million: 10¹² vs 10⁷. This is what made digital signal processing practical.

Common mistake

Thinking the FFT is an approximation. It computes exactly the same numbers as the DFT, only in a smarter order.

Try it

Step through the stages: 2-point DFTs, then 4-point, then 8-point.

Stage 1

Neighbouring inputs (x[0] with x[4], x[2] with x[6]…) are combined into 2-point DFTs: just a sum and a difference.

Last stage

The two 4-point DFTs (even and odd samples) are joined with the twiddle factors W₈ᵏ into the full 8-point result.
