What you are looking at

Top left: the test signal — a 2 Hz square wave with a 60 Hz hum and noise (grey) — and the filtered output (blue). Bottom left: their spectra in dB. Top right: the filter seen in time (its kernel or impulse response). Bottom right: the filter seen in frequency.

Every linear filter has two faces, linked by the convolution theorem:

$$y = h * x \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad Y(f) = H(f)\cdot X(f)$$

You can design it in either domain, but you always pay in the other. Sharp in frequency means long and wiggly in time; short in time means soft in frequency. This is the uncertainty principle at work.

Smoothing in time

Replace each sample by a weighted average of its neighbours: convolution with a kernel. The average cancels fast wiggles (noise, hum) and keeps slow changes.

  1. Rectangular kernel (moving average of M samples): \(|H| = |\text{sinc}(fM/f_s)|\). Zeros exactly at multiples of fs/M — but sidelobes in between let some high frequencies through.
  2. Gaussian kernel: \(|H|\) is a Gaussian too. No sidelobes, no ripples; the attenuation just keeps growing.
  3. Longer kernel → narrower H → more noise removed, but edges smeared over a longer time.

Worked example

fs = 512 Hz, moving average of 31 samples (61 ms): first zero at 512/31 = 16.5 Hz. The 60 Hz hum sits near the 4th sidelobe, about −20 dB. The square wave's edges ramp over 61 ms.

Common mistake

Thinking a moving average is a good low-pass. It is simple and has exact nulls, but its sidelobes are only −13 dB: a strong tone between nulls leaks through.

Try it

Lengthen the kernel and watch the hum vanish and the edges blur at the same time.

Moving average

The rectangle's spectrum is a sinc: deep nulls, but sidelobes of −13, −18, −21 dB… between them.

A long kernel

101 samples (0.2 s): almost all noise and hum are gone, but the square wave has become a slow, rounded wave.

A short kernel

5 samples: edges stay sharp, but H(60 Hz) is still near 1 — the hum passes almost untouched.

Masking the spectrum

Go to the frequency domain (FFT), multiply by a mask M(f), come back (inverse FFT). In time this is the same as convolving with \(m = \mathcal F^{-1}\{M\}\), shown top right.

  1. Brick wall: keep everything below fc, delete everything above. Perfect in frequency — but m is a sinc that rings on both sides, so every edge in the signal rings too (Gibbs).
  2. Butterworth \(M = 1/\sqrt{1 + (f/f_c)^{2n}}\): order n sets the steepness. Low n: gentle slope, little ringing. High n: approaches the brick wall, rings more.

Worked example

Brick wall at 10 Hz: the square wave keeps its harmonics at 2, 6 and 10 Hz and loses 14, 18, … The output is the three-harmonic partial sum, overshooting by about 18 % of the step height at each edge, with ripples at ~10 Hz. The ringing even starts before the edge: a zero-phase mask is not causal.

Common mistake

Assuming the ideal filter is the best filter. A sharp cut in frequency always means long ringing in time; for pulses and edges a gentler filter usually looks better.

Try it

Switch to Butterworth and raise the order from 1 to 12: the mask steepens and the impulse response grows side ripples.

Brick wall at 10 Hz

Every edge of the square wave rings, before and after. m[n] is a sinc of main lobe 1/fc = 100 ms.

Butterworth n = 2

A soft roll-off (−40 dB/decade): m[n] has only a small undershoot, the edges barely ring.

Butterworth n = 10

Nearly a brick wall: the ringing is back.

The Gaussian: the same shape in both domains

$$h(t) \propto e^{-t^2/2\sigma_t^2} \;\longleftrightarrow\; H(f) = e^{-f^2/2\sigma_f^2}, \qquad \sigma_f = \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma_t}$$

The Gaussian kernel is the best compromise: it is the most compact in time for a given width in frequency (it sits on the uncertainty bound), it has no sidelobes, and its step response never overshoots. That is why it is used for smoothing images (Gaussian blur), for GSM's GMSK pulses and for scale-space analysis.

Worked example

σt = 4 ms → σf = 40 Hz: H(60 Hz) = e^{−60²/(2·40²)} = 0.32 (−10 dB). σt = 15 ms → σf = 10.6 Hz: the hum is down by 140 dB.

Common mistake

Expecting a sharp cut-off from a Gaussian. It is smooth everywhere, including its transition band: good for edges, poor for separating close frequencies.

Try it

Widen σt: the time kernel spreads while |H| narrows, keeping their product fixed.

Narrow Gaussian

σt = 1 ms: σf = 160 Hz. Barely any smoothing; the hum passes.

Wide Gaussian

σt = 15 ms: σf = 10.6 Hz. Strong smoothing with no ringing at all.

FIR design: the windowed sinc

To build a real low-pass filter: take the ideal one's impulse response (a sinc), cut it to M taps, and taper it with a window (Hamming here) so the cut does not cause ripples.

$$h[n] = w[n]\cdot\frac{\sin\big(2\pi f_c (n-m)/f_s\big)}{\pi (n-m)}, \qquad \Delta f_\text{transition} \approx 3.3\,\frac{f_s}{M}$$
  1. High-pass: subtract the low-pass from a delta (all-pass).
  2. Band-pass: subtract two low-passes.
  3. More taps → sharper transition, more delay ((M−1)/2 samples), more computation.

Worked example

fs = 512 Hz, 101 taps: transition ≈ 17 Hz wide, stop band below −50 dB. With 15 taps: 113 Hz wide — barely a filter.

Common mistake

Choosing the cut-off but forgetting the transition width: the filter needs room between "pass" and "stop", and that room costs taps.

Try it

Set a band-pass around 60 Hz: only the hum survives.

High-pass at 40 Hz

The square wave's low harmonics are removed: what remains is the hum, the noise, and spikes at the edges (an edge has energy at all frequencies).

Band-pass 45–75 Hz

Isolates the 60 Hz hum, plus the square wave's harmonics that happen to fall in the band.

15 taps

A very soft edge: the transition spans about 100 Hz, so the hum is only partly removed.
