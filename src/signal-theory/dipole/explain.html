The current on a dipole

What you are looking at

Left: the wire (vertical), fed at the red dot, with the current at each point drawn as a horizontal bar. Right: the current along the wire — its envelope (dashed) and its value now (blue), oscillating in time.

A dipole is a transmission line opened out. The current must be zero at the wire's free ends, so it forms a standing wave:

$$I(z,t) = I_0\,\sin\!\big(k(L/2 - |z|)\big)\cos\omega t, \qquad k = 2\pi/\lambda$$
  1. Short dipole (L ≪ λ): only the start of the sine fits — a nearly triangular current, small everywhere, so the wire radiates weakly.
  2. Half-wave (L = λ/2): exactly one half-sine, maximum at the feed. Every part of the wire carries current in the same direction: all contributions add broadside.
  3. Full wave (L = λ): the feed is at a current null — the generator sees an enormous impedance.
  4. Longer: lobes of current alternate in sign; their radiation partly cancels, which shapes the pattern.

Common mistake

Thinking the current is uniform along the wire like in a DC circuit. At radio frequency the wire is comparable to a wavelength, and the current varies along it.

Try it

Slide L from 0.1 to 1.5 λ and watch the feed point's current fall to zero at L = λ.

Short dipole

0.1 λ: the current falls linearly from the feed to the ends. Radiation resistance only ≈ 2 Ω.

Full wave

The feed sits at a node of the standing wave: almost no current for a given voltage — a very high input impedance.

1.5 λ

Three half-waves of current; the middle one is opposite to the outer ones.

E and H fields

What you are looking at

A snapshot of the radiated field in the plane containing the wire (orange): E_θ on the left, H on the right (out of or into the page). Colour = sign and strength; the pattern moves outward over time.
$$E_\theta = \frac{\eta_0 I_0}{2\pi r}\,F(\theta)\,\sin(\omega t - kr), \qquad H_\phi = E_\theta/\eta_0, \qquad \eta_0 = 377\ \Omega$$

Far from the wire, the field is a spherical wave: rings that expand at the speed of light, one ring every half wavelength. Their strength depends on direction through F(θ): maximum broadside, zero along the wire's axis — a charge moving along a line cannot radiate along that line.

Worked example

100 W into a half-wave dipole: at 1 km broadside, |E| ≈ 7√(P·D)/r ≈ 0.09 V/m, |H| = E/377 ≈ 0.24 mA/m.

Common mistake

Thinking E and H are separate waves. In the far field they are locked together: in phase, at right angles, ratio 377 Ω.

L = 1.25 λ

The rings are no longer uniform in angle: the pattern now has a narrow broadside lobe and weak side lobes.

How radiation detaches

What you are looking at

The electric field lines of a tiny oscillating dipole at the centre, as time advances. The red circle is r = λ/2π.
$$E_\theta \propto \Big(\underbrace{\frac{1}{r}}_{\text{radiated}} + \underbrace{\frac{1}{jkr^2} - \frac{1}{k^2r^3}}_{\text{bound}}\Big)\sin\theta\,e^{-jkr}$$

Close to the dipole the 1/r² and 1/r³ terms dominate: the field follows the charges like the field of a static dipole, growing and collapsing every half cycle. But the field cannot change everywhere at once — the change travels at c. When the charges reverse, the outer part of each loop cannot get back in time: it pinches off and keeps going. Those closed loops are the radiated wave.

  1. Full: loops grow, pinch off near r = λ/2π and escape.
  2. Bound only: loops swell and collapse back — energy goes out and returns each cycle, nothing is lost.
  3. Radiated only: closed loops marching outward.

Worked example

At r = λ/2π (kr = 1) all three terms are equal. At r = λ they are 1 : 0.16 : 0.025 — already almost pure radiation.

Common mistake

Thinking the near field is "where radiation is strong". It is where energy is stored and sloshes back and forth; it does not travel.

Try it

Switch to "bound" and watch the loops refuse to leave.

Bound term only

Loops breathe in and out around the source but never detach: this is the reactive near field — stored, not radiated.

Radiated term only

Closed loops travelling outward at c, spaced λ/2: the part that carries power away for ever.

E-plane and H-plane patterns

What you are looking at

Left polar plot: the E-plane (the plane containing the wire, drawn vertical) in dB. Right: the H-plane (perpendicular to the wire). Bottom: the E-plane in dB against θ, and directivity against length.
$$F(\theta) = \frac{\cos\!\big(\tfrac{kL}{2}\cos\theta\big) - \cos\tfrac{kL}{2}}{\sin\theta}$$

The dipole is symmetric around its axis, so the H-plane pattern is always a circle: it radiates equally in every horizontal direction. All the shaping is in the E-plane.

Worked example

Half-wave: a figure-of-eight, half-power beamwidth 78°, D = 1.64 (2.15 dBi). Full wave: 47°, 2.41 (3.8 dBi). 1.5 λ: the main lobes tilt to about 45° off broadside.

Common mistake

Calling a dipole omnidirectional without saying in which plane. It is omnidirectional in azimuth only; straight up the wire it radiates nothing.

L = λ

A narrower broadside lobe: the two halves of the current add more coherently broadside.

L = 1.5 λ

The opposite-phase middle current cancels broadside: lobes appear at oblique angles.

The 3D pattern

What you are looking at

The radiation pattern as a surface: the distance from the centre in each direction is the radiated field there (in dB above the floor). The orange rod is the wire. Drag to rotate.

Revolving the E-plane pattern around the wire gives the full 3D pattern: a doughnut for a half-wave dipole, with a hole along the axis.

Common mistake

Reading the size of the surface as gain in linear units. Here it is in dB, so weak sidelobes look bigger than they are.

L = λ

A flatter doughnut: more of the power goes broadside.

L = 2 λ

The doughnut splits into cones: four current lobes produce lobes at several elevations.

The pattern as the length grows

What you are looking at

Each column of the image is one E-plane pattern (θ from 0 at the bottom to 180° at the top); left to right the dipole gets longer. Bright = strong. The green line is the current length, whose polar pattern is on the right.

Up to about 1.25 λ there is a single broadside lobe getting narrower. Beyond, the current develops opposite-phase lobes, the broadside direction weakens, and the beam splits into several lobes.

Try it

Follow the bright band at θ = 90° (the middle row): it narrows, then dies around 2 λ.

1.25 λ

The narrowest single lobe and the highest broadside directivity of any straight dipole (≈ 5.2 dBi); a little longer and it splits.

Input impedance

What you are looking at

Top: input resistance and reactance against length. Bottom left: how much of a 50 Ω cable's power is reflected (|Γ|). Bottom right: the radiation resistance referred to the current maximum.

The generator sees the ratio of voltage to current at the feed. The resistive part accounts for the power radiated; the reactive part for energy stored in the near field. At resonance the reactance vanishes.

$$R_\text{in} = \frac{R_m}{\sin^2(kL/2)}, \quad R_m = \frac{\eta_0}{2\pi}\int_0^\pi F^2\sin\theta\,d\theta, \qquad \Gamma = \frac{Z_\text{in} - 50}{Z_\text{in} + 50}$$

Worked example

L = 0.5 λ: Z = 73 + j42.5 Ω. Shortening to ≈ 0.48 λ removes the reactance (≈ 70 Ω): that is why real half-wave dipoles are cut about 5 % short. Short dipole 0.1 λ: R ≈ 2 Ω and X ≈ −1800 Ω — nearly impossible to feed efficiently.

Common mistake

Using the free-space λ for a thick wire: thicker wires resonate a little shorter and have a flatter reactance curve (wider bandwidth).

Try it

Increase the wire radius: the reactance swings become gentler.

Resonance

At ≈ 0.48 λ the reactance crosses zero: about 70 Ω, close to a 75 Ω coax.

A thick wire

a = 0.008 λ: the reactance varies more slowly with length — the antenna works over a wider band.

Directivity

What you are looking at

Directivity against length (linear and dBi), the half-power beamwidth, and the pattern at the chosen length.
$$D = \frac{4\pi\,U_{\max}}{P_\text{rad}} = \frac{2\,F_{\max}^2}{\int_0^\pi F^2(\theta)\sin\theta\,d\theta}$$

Directivity compares the peak intensity with what an isotropic antenna radiating the same total power would give. Narrower beam, higher directivity.

Worked example

Short dipole: 1.5 (1.76 dBi). Half wave: 1.64 (2.15 dBi). 1.25 λ: 3.3 (5.2 dBi). Gain = efficiency × directivity, so a lossless dipole's gain equals these.

Common mistake

dBi vs dBd: dBd is relative to a half-wave dipole, so 0 dBd = 2.15 dBi.

The best broadside dipole

1.25 λ: the highest directivity before the lobe splits.

Near field and far field

What you are looking at

Left: the field strength around a finite wire (orange) in dB, computed with all near-field terms. Green circle: r = λ/2π; blue circle: the far-field distance 2D²/λ. Right: field strength broadside against distance on log–log axes.
  1. Reactive near field (r ≲ λ/2π): 1/r³ dominates; energy stored, not radiated.
  2. Radiating near field (Fresnel): waves travel outward, but the pattern still changes with distance.
  3. Far field (Fraunhofer), r > 2D²/λ: the pattern is fixed and the field falls as 1/r.

Worked example

L = λ: far field from 2 λ. A 3 m dish at 10 GHz (λ = 3 cm): 2·9/0.03 = 600 m — antenna ranges must be long.

Common mistake

Measuring a pattern too close: in the Fresnel region the measured shape is wrong (lobes filled in).

Try it

On the right, read the slopes: steep near the wire, −20 dB/decade beyond the blue line.

L = 2 λ

The far field starts at 2·2² = 8 λ: bigger antennas need much more distance.
