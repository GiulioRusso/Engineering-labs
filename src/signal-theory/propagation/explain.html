A free-space link

What you are looking at

Top: received power against distance (log axis); the red line is what the receiver needs. Bottom: the link budget as bars — what each term adds or takes away.
$$P_r = P_t\,G_t\,G_r\left(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi d}\right)^2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad P_r[\text{dBm}] = P_t + G_t + G_r - 20\log_{10}\frac{4\pi d}{\lambda}$$
  1. The transmitter's power spreads over a sphere of area 4πd²: the density falls as 1/d².
  2. The receiving antenna collects through its effective area Gλ²/4π: that is where λ² enters.
  3. In dB: −20 dB per decade of distance, −6 dB per doubling of distance or of frequency.

Worked example

Wi-Fi: 20 dBm, 2 dBi antennas, 2.4 GHz, 1 km: FSPL = 100 dB → −76 dBm; with a −90 dBm receiver, 14 dB of margin.

Common mistake

Saying "high frequencies are absorbed more by free space". Space absorbs nothing: the λ² comes from the receiving antenna being smaller at high frequency. With fixed-size dishes, high frequencies actually win.

10 km

20 dB more loss: −96 dBm, below the sensitivity.

Dishes

+56 dB of antenna gain buys a 50 km link at the same power.

60 GHz

25 times the frequency: 20 log 25 = 28 dB more loss with the same small antennas.

The ground reflection

What you are looking at

Top: received power with only the direct wave (grey) and with a wave reflected by the ground as well (blue). Bottom: their difference.
$$P_r \approx P_t G_t G_r \frac{h_t^2 h_r^2}{d^4} \quad \text{beyond} \quad d_b = \frac{4 h_t h_r}{\lambda}$$

Over flat ground the receiver gets two waves: direct and reflected (which flips sign at grazing incidence). Close in, their path difference changes quickly: they alternately add and cancel, ±6 dB ripples. Far away the paths become almost equal and, with the sign flip, the two nearly cancel: power falls as 1/d⁴, 40 dB per decade.

Worked example

hₜ = 10 m, hᵣ = 1.5 m at 2.4 GHz: breakpoint 480 m. Beyond it, doubling the distance costs 12 dB, not 6.

Common mistake

Using free-space loss for ground links beyond the breakpoint: it overestimates the range dramatically. Raising the antennas helps (d_b grows as hₜhᵣ).

A taller mast

50 m: the breakpoint moves to 2.4 km.

The Yagi–Uda antenna

What you are looking at

Left: the elements — a reflector behind, the fed dipole (red), directors in front. Right: the pattern in the plane of the elements, forward up.

Only one element is connected. The others are plain rods: the driven dipole's field induces currents in them, and they re-radiate. A slightly longer rod behind (reflector) and slightly shorter rods in front (directors) carry currents with just the right phase lag that their waves add in front and cancel behind — a phased array with no phase shifters.

$$F(\varphi) = \Big|\sum_n a_n\,e^{\,j(k x_n\cos\varphi - n\phi)}\Big|$$

Worked example

Dipole + reflector: about 4–5 dB more than a dipole forward. With 10 directors: 12–14 dBi — the classic TV antenna.

Common mistake

Mounting it backwards: the beam points along the directors, away from the reflector.

Reflector only

A two-element beam: moderate front-to-back ratio, broad lobe.

10 directors

A long boom: narrower beam, higher gain — each extra director adds less.

Wrong phasing

With the currents lagging the wrong way, the waves add toward the back.

The patch antenna

What you are looking at

Patterns of a rectangular patch on a ground plane, broadside up: E-plane (along the patch length, left) and H-plane (along its width, right).

A metal rectangle about half a wavelength long (in the substrate) over a ground plane is a resonant cavity. Its two short ends act as slots with fields in phase: they add straight up and partly cancel sideways. The ground plane blocks almost everything behind.

$$E_E(\theta) \propto \cos\!\left(\tfrac{kL}{2}\sin\theta\right)\text{sinc}\!\left(\tfrac{kh}{2}\sin\theta\right), \qquad E_H(\theta) \propto \cos\theta\;\text{sinc}\!\left(\tfrac{kW}{2}\sin\theta\right)$$

Worked example

A typical patch: beams of about 80–100°, 6–8 dBi. Every phone, GPS receiver and Wi-Fi card has some.

Common mistake

Expecting wide bandwidth: a patch is a resonator, typically a few per cent wide; thicker substrates widen it.

A wide patch

W = 1.2 λ: a longer slot narrows the H-plane beam.
