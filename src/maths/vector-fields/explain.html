The dot product: how much two arrows agree

What you are looking at

Two vectors a and b (drag their tips), the angle between them, and the shadow of b on a.

The dot product multiplies the length of a by the part of b that points along a. Same direction: as big as possible. At right angles: zero — they share nothing. Pointing apart: negative. Work in physics (force · displacement) is exactly this.

$$\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b = a_xb_x + a_yb_y + a_zb_z = |\mathbf a||\mathbf b|\cos\theta$$
  1. Compute with components: multiply matching components and add.
  2. Then the angle: cos θ = a·b / (|a||b|).

Worked example

a = (1.6, 0.3), b = (0.6, 1.4): a·b = 0.96 + 0.42 = 1.38; |a| = 1.63, |b| = 1.52; cos θ = 0.557, θ = 56°.

Common mistake

Expecting a vector: the dot product is a number.

Perpendicular

\(\theta = 90°\), \(\cos\theta = 0\): \(\mathbf b\) casts no shadow on \(\mathbf a\), so \(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b = 0\). This is the test for perpendicular vectors.

Same direction

\(\theta = 0\), \(\cos\theta = 1\): the dot product is as large as it can be, \(|\mathbf a||\mathbf b|\).

Obtuse angle

Beyond 90° the shadow falls behind the origin: \(\cos\theta < 0\) and the dot product is negative.

Opposite

\(\theta = 180°\): the most negative value, \(-|\mathbf a||\mathbf b|\).

The cross product: an arrow out of the plane

What you are looking at

a, b, the parallelogram they span, and a × b standing perpendicular to both.

The cross product of two vectors is a third vector at right angles to both. Its length is the area of the parallelogram they span — biggest at 90°, zero when they are parallel. Its direction follows the right-hand rule. Torque (r × F) and magnetic force (q v × B) are cross products.

$$|\mathbf a\times\mathbf b| = |\mathbf a||\mathbf b|\sin\theta, \qquad \mathbf a\times\mathbf b = -\,\mathbf b\times\mathbf a, \qquad \hat x\times\hat y = \hat z$$

Worked example

a = (1.5, 0, 0), b = (0.6, 1.4, 0): a × b = (0, 0, 2.1), the area of the parallelogram.

Common mistake

Swapping the order: it flips the direction.

The axes

Unit vectors along \(x\) and \(y\): the square they span has area 1 and the cross product is the unit vector along \(z\).

At 90°

\(\sin\theta = 1\): the parallelogram is a rectangle, the area is largest.

Parallel

\(\sin\theta = 0\): the parallelogram is flat, the cross product is the zero vector.

Order matters

Swapping the vectors flips the result: \(\mathbf b\times\mathbf a = -\mathbf a\times\mathbf b\). Curl the fingers of the right hand from the first to the second: the thumb gives the direction.

A vector field: gravity of two masses

What you are looking at

At every point an arrow for the gravitational field of two masses; a probe point shows the field there and its parts from each mass.

A field gives a vector at every point of space. Gravity from several masses simply adds: each pulls toward itself with 1/r² strength. Where the pulls cancel, the field is zero.

$$\mathbf g(\mathbf r) = -\sum_i \frac{G m_i}{|\mathbf r - \mathbf r_i|^2}\,\hat{\mathbf r}_i$$

Worked example

Equal masses: the null point is exactly midway. With masses 10:1 it sits much closer to the small one (distances in ratio √10 ≈ 3.2:1) — like the Earth–Moon L1 point.

Common mistake

Adding the strengths instead of the vectors.

Equal masses

Midway between them the two pulls are equal and opposite: \(\mathbf g = 0\). A body placed there stays (but any nudge sends it to one side).

Unequal masses

The point where the pulls cancel moves towards the small mass: \(M_1/r_1^2 = M_2/r_2^2\).

Far away

Seen from far, the two masses act like one mass \(M_1 + M_2\) at their centre of mass.

Divergence: sources and sinks

What you are looking at

A flow field with tracers; a probe circle shows how much flows out of it.

Divergence measures how much more flows out of a tiny region than flows in. Positive: a source (a tap). Negative: a sink (a drain). Zero: whatever comes in goes out.

$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf F = \frac{\partial F_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial F_y}{\partial y}$$

Worked example

F = (x, y) (pointing outward, growing with distance): div F = 2 everywhere. The vortex (−y, x): div = 0 — it only circulates.

Common mistake

Thinking arrows that point outward always mean div > 0: they also have to grow along the way.

A sink

\(\mathbf F = -(x, y)\): everything flows inwards, \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf F = -2\) everywhere.

A vortex

\(\mathbf F = (-y, x)\) only goes round: as much flows in as out of any circle, so the divergence is 0.

Changing sign

\(\mathbf F = (x, y)e^{-r^2}\) spreads near the centre (div \(> 0\)) but slows down further out, so there the dots crowd (div \(< 0\)). The sign changes at \(r = 1\).

Curl: local spinning

What you are looking at

A flow with a small paddle wheel at the probe point: it spins if the curl is nonzero.

Curl measures how much a tiny paddle wheel placed in the flow would spin. A whirlpool spins it. Surprisingly, a straight shear flow (faster on one side) spins it too, while a vortex that slows as 1/r away from the centre does not.

$$(\nabla\times\mathbf F)_z = \frac{\partial F_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial F_x}{\partial y}$$

Worked example

F = (−y, x): curl = 1 + 1 = 2 everywhere. F = (y, 0) (shear): curl = −1, though every arrow is horizontal.

Common mistake

Judging curl by whether the arrows go round in circles.

A source

Straight out from the centre: nothing goes round, the curl is 0.

Shear

\(\mathbf F = (y, 0)\): all streamlines are straight, yet the top moves faster than the bottom, so a paddle wheel turns. Curl \(= -1\).

A spiral

\(\mathbf F = (x - y, x + y)\) both spreads and turns: divergence 2 and curl 2.
