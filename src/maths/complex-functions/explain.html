Euler's formula

What you are looking at

The terms of the series for e^{iθ} drawn as arrows, each starting where the previous one ended. The red dot is cos θ + i sin θ on the unit circle; the orange dot is the sum so far.

The exponential has the series eˣ = 1 + x + x²/2! + x³/3! + … Put in x = iθ. Multiplying by i is a quarter turn, so each term is turned 90° from the previous one and shrunk by θ/k. The arrows curl around and land exactly on the unit circle, at angle θ.

$$e^{i\theta} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(i\theta)^k}{k!} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta, \qquad e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0$$
  1. Real terms (even k) give the cosine series 1 − θ²/2 + θ⁴/24 − …
  2. Imaginary terms (odd k) give the sine series θ − θ³/6 + …

Worked example

θ = π: 1, then 3.14i, −4.93, −5.17i, 4.06, … The spiral overshoots but settles on −1 after about 14 terms.

Common mistake

Thinking e^{iθ} grows like eˣ: its size is always 1 — it only turns.

e^{iπ} = −1

Half a turn: the most famous equation in mathematics, linking e, i, π, 1 and 0.

e^{iπ/2} = i

A quarter turn lands on the imaginary unit.

Domain colouring

What you are looking at

Every point z of the plane is coloured by the value f(z): the hue shows its direction (red = positive real, cyan = negative real), the shading rings show its size (one ring per factor e).

A complex function takes a 2D input and gives a 2D output, so its graph would need four dimensions. Colouring the input plane by the output is a way to see it all at once. Zeros and poles jump out as points where every colour meets.

  1. Going once round a zero of order n, the colours cycle n times.
  2. Round a pole they cycle the other way, and the rings crowd in (|f| → ∞).

Worked example

z³ − 1: three points where all colours meet, at 1 and at −½ ± (√3/2)i — the cube roots of unity, 120° apart.

Common mistake

Reading brightness as height: the rings show log|f|, so equal spacing means multiplying by e.

z²

A double zero at the origin: the colours go round twice.

1/z

A pole: colours go round in the opposite order, and the size grows toward the centre.

eᶻ

No zeros at all; the colours repeat every 2π along the imaginary direction, and brightness grows to the right.

How a function bends the plane

What you are looking at

Left: a square grid of vertical (blue) and horizontal (orange) lines. Right: where f sends it.

A complex function moves every point of the plane. Watching a grid shows how: stretched, rotated, bent. Remarkably, wherever f′ ≠ 0, small squares stay square — they are only rotated and scaled. Angles are preserved, which is why complex functions are used to map flows around wings and fields around electrodes.

$$w = f(z), \qquad dw = f'(z)\,dz \;(\text{a rotation and a stretch})$$

Worked example

eᶻ sends the vertical line Re z = 0 to the unit circle, and the horizontal line Im z = 0 to the positive real axis.

Common mistake

Expecting angles kept where f′ = 0: z² doubles the angles at the origin.

eᶻ

Vertical lines wrap into circles, horizontal lines fan out into rays — polar coordinates appear.

1/z

Lines become circles through the origin; the outside of the unit circle goes inside.
