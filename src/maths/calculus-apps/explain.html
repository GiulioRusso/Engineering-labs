Limits

What you are looking at

Top: a function near a point a where it is not defined; two dots approach a from the left (orange) and from the right (blue). Bottom: the values at a − 10ᵏ and a + 10ᵏ as k grows: do they settle on one number?

Sometimes you cannot just plug in: (x² − 1)/(x − 1) at x = 1 is 0/0. The limit asks a gentler question: where are the values heading as x gets closer and closer to a? If both sides head to the same number, that number is the limit — whatever happens exactly at a.

$$\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L \iff \lim_{x\to a^-} f(x) = \lim_{x\to a^+} f(x) = L$$
  1. Try to simplify: (x² − 1)/(x − 1) = x + 1 for x ≠ 1, so the limit is 2.
  2. Check both sides separately when there is a jump or an absolute value.
  3. If the values grow without bound, the limit is ±∞; if they keep oscillating, there is none.

Worked example

sin x / x: at 0.1 → 0.99833, at 0.01 → 0.9999833. The limit is 1 — the reason sin θ ≈ θ for small angles.

Common mistakes

Saying "the limit is undefined" because f(a) is. Checking only one side.

Try it

Switch to the jump: the two dots stay on different heights however close they get.

A jump

|x|/x is −1 on the left and +1 on the right: the one-sided limits differ, so the limit does not exist.

An infinite limit

1/x² gets bigger and bigger from both sides: limit +∞, a vertical asymptote.

sin x / x

Both sides approach 1: a hole in the graph, filled by the limit.

sin(1/x)

Closer to 0 it oscillates faster and faster between −1 and 1: no value to settle on.

Curve sketching with derivatives

What you are looking at

Three stacked graphs: f (green where rising, red where falling), f′ and f″. Critical points (f′ = 0) and inflection points (f″ = 0) are marked and lined up across the three.

The derivative is a slope detector. Where f′ > 0 the graph climbs, where f′ < 0 it falls, and where it changes sign the graph turns: a maximum or a minimum. The second derivative says how the slope itself changes: positive means the curve bends up like a cup, negative like a cap.

$$f'(x_0) = 0, \; f''(x_0) > 0 \Rightarrow \text{minimum}, \qquad f''(x_0) < 0 \Rightarrow \text{maximum}$$
  1. Solve f′(x) = 0: the candidate points.
  2. Look at the sign of f′ on either side, or at f″ there.
  3. Solve f″ = 0 (with a sign change): the inflection points, where the bending flips.

Worked example

f = x³ − 3x: f′ = 3x² − 3 = 0 at x = ±1; f″ = 6x: at −1 it is −6 (maximum, f = 2), at +1 +6 (minimum, f = −2); inflection at 0.

Common mistake

"f′ = 0 means a maximum or minimum": x³ has f′(0) = 0 but keeps rising — a flat inflection.

x⁴ − 2x²

Two minima at ±1 and a maximum at 0: f′ = 4x³ − 4x has three zeros.

x³

f′ touches zero without changing sign: no turn, just a pause.

x e^(−x²)

A maximum at 1/√2 and a minimum at −1/√2, three inflection points.

Newton's method

What you are looking at

Top: the curve, and the zig-zag of the method — from each guess go up to the curve, then slide down the tangent to the axis. Bottom: the error after each step on a log scale.

Solving f(x) = 0 exactly is often impossible. Newton's idea: near a guess, the curve looks like its tangent line, and a line is easy to solve. Take that solution as the next guess and repeat. Close to the root the number of correct digits roughly doubles every step.

$$x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}$$

Worked example

√2 from x² − 2 = 0, start at 1: 1.5, 1.41667, 1.4142157, 1.41421356237… — 12 correct digits in 4 steps.

Common mistakes

Starting where f′ ≈ 0 (the tangent is flat and shoots far away). Assuming it always converges: x³ − 2x + 2 from 0 bounces between 0 and 1 forever.

A far start

From 4 the first steps are big; once close, the error collapses.

A cycle

From 0 the tangent sends it to 1, and from 1 straight back to 0.

cos x = x

No formula exists for this root, but Newton finds 0.7390851 in four steps.

The area between two curves

What you are looking at

Two curves and the region between them from a to b (shaded); below, the running integral of f − g.

Slice the region into thin vertical strips. Each strip has width dx and height top − bottom. Adding them all is an integral. Where the curves cross, top and bottom swap: split the integral there (or integrate |f − g|).

$$A = \int_a^b |f(x) - g(x)|\,dx$$

Worked example

Between y = x and y = x² on [0, 1]: ∫(x − x²) dx = 1/2 − 1/3 = 1/6.

Common mistake

Integrating f − g across a crossing: the parts cancel and the "area" comes out too small, even zero.

sin and cos on [0, π]

They cross at π/4: the signed integral is 2, the true area 2√2 ≈ 2.83.

√x and x²

Mirror images across y = x: the area between them is 1/3.

Solids of revolution

What you are looking at

A profile r(x) (orange) spun around the x axis into a solid; the disc marks one slice. Drag to rotate the view.

Spin a curve around an axis and you get a solid. Cut it across and every slice is a disc of radius r(x): area πr², thickness dx. Stack the discs — integrate — and you have the volume.

$$V = \pi\int_a^b r(x)^2\,dx$$

Worked example

Cone r = x on [0, h]: π∫x² dx = πh³/3 = (1/3)πr²h with r = h. Sphere r = √(1 − x²): π∫(1 − x²) dx from −1 to 1 = 4π/3.

Common mistake

Forgetting to square the radius.

Cylinder

Constant radius: V = πr²h = 3π for r = 1, h = 3.

Sphere

A half-circle spun round: 4π/3 for radius 1.

A vase

Any profile works: the disc method needs only r(x).
