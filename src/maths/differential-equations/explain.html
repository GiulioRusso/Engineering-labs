A differential equation gives the slope at every point: the short grey segments. A solution is a curve that follows them everywhere; drag the starting point to pick one. Euler's method (violet) walks in straight steps of length \(h\) along the slope at the start of each step.

$$y' = f(x, y), \qquad y_{n+1} = y_n + h\,f(x_n, y_n), \qquad \text{error} \propto h$$

y′ = ky: the rate depends on the amount

What you are looking at

The slope field: at every point a small dash shows the slope the equation demands there. The exact solution (blue) follows the dashes; Euler's method (orange dots) takes straight steps of size h along them.

A differential equation does not tell you y — it tells you how fast y changes. "The more you have, the faster it grows" (money, bacteria) or "the more there is, the faster it leaks" (a cooling cup, a discharging capacitor). Solving it means finding a curve that follows the dashes everywhere.

$$y' = ky \;\Rightarrow\; y(x) = y_0\,e^{k(x - x_0)}, \qquad \text{Euler: } y_{n+1} = y_n + h\,f(x_n, y_n)$$
  1. Start at the initial point; read the slope there; walk a step h along it.
  2. Read the slope at the new point; repeat. That is Euler's method — what a computer does.
  3. The error per step is about h²; over a fixed distance about h: halve the step, halve the error.

Worked example

k = 0.5, y₀ = 0.4, h = 0.5: Euler multiplies by 1.25 each step; the truth multiplies by e^{0.25} = 1.284. After 6 steps: Euler 1.53, true 1.79.

Common mistakes

Thinking a small step is always enough: for fast decay (k = −3) with h = 0.8, Euler's factor 1 + kh = −1.4 makes the numbers oscillate and grow — a numerical instability, not physics.

Try it

Drag the starting point: every solution is the same curve shifted. Then shrink h and watch the dots close onto the curve.

Decay

\(k < 0\): every solution falls towards 0, and halves every \(\ln 2/|k|\).

A smaller step

With \(h = 0.1\) Euler hugs the true curve. Its error is proportional to \(h\): five times smaller step, five times smaller error.

A big step

Each step uses the slope at its start, which is too small for a growing curve: Euler falls further and further below.

Numerical instability

For \(y' = -3y\) each Euler step multiplies by \(1 - 3h\). With \(h = 0.8\) that is \(-1.4\): the numbers flip sign and grow, although the true solution decays. Euler is stable only for \(h < 2/3\) here.

The logistic equation: growth with a limit

What you are looking at

The slope field of y′ = r y (1 − y/K), solutions from your starting point, and the line y = K.

Real populations cannot grow for ever: food runs out. The factor (1 − y/K) slows growth as y approaches the carrying capacity K: small populations grow exponentially, big ones level off. Above K the slope is negative and the population falls back.

$$y' = r\,y\left(1 - \frac{y}{K}\right), \qquad y(x) = \frac{K}{1 + \left(\frac{K}{y_0} - 1\right)e^{-r(x - x_0)}}$$
  1. Equilibria: y = 0 (unstable) and y = K (stable) — where the slope is zero.
  2. The fastest growth is at y = K/2, the inflection of the S-curve.

Worked example

K = 2, r = 0.5, y₀ = 0.1: growth is almost exponential until y ≈ 0.5, fastest at y = 1, and the curve levels near 2 after about 12 units.

Common mistake

Extrapolating early exponential data: the S-curve and the exponential look identical at the start.

Above the capacity

Too many: the slope is negative and the population falls back to \(K\). \(K\) is a stable equilibrium.

At equilibrium

Starting exactly at \(K\) the slope is 0 forever: the solution is a horizontal line.

Faster growth

A larger \(r\) makes the S steeper; the end value \(K\) does not change.

A driven equation: memory fades

What you are looking at

Solutions of a first-order equation pushed by a periodic input, from different starts.

A first-order system (an RC circuit, a thermometer) driven by an oscillating input: whatever the start, the difference between solutions decays exponentially, and every solution settles onto the same periodic steady state. The system forgets its initial condition.

$$y' = -y + \sin x \;\Rightarrow\; y(x) = \underbrace{C e^{-x}}_{\text{transient}} + \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{\sqrt 2}\sin\!\left(x - \tfrac{\pi}{4}\right)}_{\text{steady state}}$$

Worked example

Two starts 5 apart are 5e^{−3} = 0.25 apart after 3 time units and 0.03 after 5.

Common mistake

Thinking the output copies the input: it is smaller (1/√2) and late (45°).

From below

A different start, the same steady oscillation after a few units of \(x\): the transient \(Ce^{-x}\) dies out.

On the steady curve

Starting on it, \(C = 0\): there is no transient at all.

The phase plane: position against velocity

What you are looking at

An oscillator drawn as a point (position, velocity). Arrows show where each state moves next; the trajectory is the path the state follows.

A second-order equation (a mass on a spring) needs two numbers to know the future: where it is and how fast it moves. Plot those two as a point and the motion becomes a curve. No friction: a closed loop for ever. With damping: a spiral into the centre. Strong damping: it creeps in without circling.

$$x'' + 2\gamma x' + x = 0 \iff \begin{cases} x' = v \\ v' = -x - 2\gamma v \end{cases}$$
  1. γ = 0: ellipse (circle here) — energy conserved.
  2. 0 < γ < 1: spiral — oscillations die out.
  3. γ ≥ 1: no turning — overdamped.

Worked example

A pendulum released from rest at x = 2: starts at (2, 0), passes through (0, −2) a quarter period later, reaches (−2, 0) at half a period.

Common mistake

Plain Euler on an undamped oscillator spirals outward: it adds energy at every step. Physics did not change — the method is wrong for this job.

Damped

Friction drains energy: the circle becomes a spiral into the rest point at the origin.

Euler gains energy

Each Euler step on a circle moves along the tangent, slightly outwards: the energy grows by \(1 + h^2\) per step and the orbit spirals out. Better methods (like RK4, used for the blue curve) avoid this.

Overdamped

With \(\gamma > 1\) the state slides into the origin without ever turning around it.
