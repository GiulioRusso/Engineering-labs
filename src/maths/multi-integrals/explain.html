The double integral: volume under a surface

What you are looking at

A surface z = f(x, y) (transparent) over a square, and the boxes that approximate the volume under it: each box sits on a small square of the floor with the height of the surface at its centre. Drag to rotate.

A single integral adds up thin strips to get an area. In two variables, cut the floor into small squares, put on each a column as tall as the function there, and add their volumes. With smaller squares the stack of boxes matches the solid better and better.

$$\iint_R f(x, y)\,dA = \lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i,j} f(x_i, y_j)\,\Delta x\,\Delta y = \int_a^b\!\!\int_c^d f(x, y)\,dy\,dx$$
  1. Integrate in y with x held fixed: the area of one slice.
  2. Integrate those slice areas in x: the volume (Fubini).

Worked example

∬(x² + y²) over [−1.5, 1.5]²: each term gives (2·1.5³/3)·3 = 6.75, total 13.5. With 6 × 6 boxes the sum is 13.1; with 30 × 30, 13.48.

Common mistake

Forgetting dA = dx dy: the box volume is height × area of its base.

Fine boxes

900 boxes: the stack hugs the surface; the error is a fraction of a per cent.

A bump

2e^{−(x² + y²)}: the volume over the whole plane would be 2π; on this square it is a little less.

Lagrange multipliers: optimising with a constraint

What you are looking at

Left: level curves of f and the constraint circle (green). A point walks round the circle with the gradient of f (blue) and of the constraint g = x² + y² (orange). Right: f along the circle.

You may only move along the circle. Moving changes f whenever ∇f has a component along the circle. At the best point it has none: ∇f points straight across the circle — the same direction as ∇g. There the level curve of f just touches the circle.

$$\nabla f = \lambda\,\nabla g, \qquad g(x, y) = r^2$$
  1. Write ∇f = λ∇g component by component.
  2. Add the constraint equation; solve the three equations for x, y, λ.

Worked example

Maximise x + y on x² + y² = 1: (1, 1) = λ(2x, 2y) gives x = y, then x = y = 1/√2: maximum √2 at 45°.

Common mistake

Setting ∇f = 0: on a constraint the optimum is usually not a flat point of f.

Maximise xy

On a circle of radius r, xy is largest at 45° (and 225°): r²/2. The best rectangle in a circle is a square.

2x + y

The maximum sits where the radius points along (2, 1): at the angle atan(1/2) = 26.6°.

Line integrals: work along a path

What you are looking at

A force field (grey arrows), a path (green) and a particle moving along it with the force acting on it; right, the work accumulated.

Work is force times displacement — but only the part of the force along the motion counts. Along a curved path, add up F · dr over every small step. For some fields (gradients of a potential) the answer depends only on the start and end: going round a loop gives zero. Others — swirling fields — do net work around a loop.

$$W = \int_C \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r, \qquad \mathbf F = \nabla\varphi \;\Rightarrow\; W = \varphi(B) - \varphi(A), \qquad \oint \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r = 0$$

Worked example

F = (y, x) = ∇(xy) from A(−2, −1) to B(2, 2): W = 4 − 2 = 2 on any path. F = (−y, x) round a circle of radius 1.6: W = 2π·1.6² = 16.1.

Common mistake

Assuming every field is conservative: a non-zero curl (here the vortex) makes the work depend on the path.

Another path

The curved path takes a different route but ends with the same work, 2.

Around a vortex

The force always pushes along the circle: the work keeps growing, 16.1 per lap.

A loop in a conservative field

Positive and negative work cancel exactly: back where you started, total zero.
