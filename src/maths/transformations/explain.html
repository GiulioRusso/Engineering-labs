Shifting, stretching and reflecting a graph

What you are looking at

A base function f (dashed) and the transformed one y = a·f(b(x − h)) + k (blue). The knob is the point that started at the origin: drag it to shift the graph.

You do not need to learn a new graph for every formula. Most functions are a familiar one moved and reshaped. The rule to remember is: changes outside f act on y the way you expect; changes inside f act on x the opposite way.

$$y = a\,f\big(b(x - h)\big) + k$$
  1. k: add to every output — the graph moves up by k.
  2. a: multiply every output — stretches vertically; a negative a flips it upside down.
  3. h: the graph does at x what f did at x − h, i.e. h later — it moves right by h.
  4. b: f(2x) reaches each value at half the x — squeezed toward the y axis; b negative mirrors left–right.

Worked example

y = −2(x − 1)² + 3: start from x², move right 1, stretch ×2, flip, move up 3. Vertex (1, 3), opening down, roots where (x − 1)² = 1.5: x = 1 ± 1.22.

Common mistakes

Moving f(x + 2) to the right: it moves left. Doing the steps in a random order: stretch about the axis first, then shift.

Try it

Drag the knob around: the whole graph follows. Then change one slider at a time and say in words what happened.

Right by 2

f(x − 2) reaches at x = 2 what f had at 0: the graph moves right, not left.

Up by 2

Every output is 2 bigger: the whole graph lifts by 2.

Twice as tall

Each y doubles; points on the x axis stay put.

Upside down

−f(x) reflects in the x axis: the parabola now opens downward.

Squeezed

sin(2x) completes a cycle in π instead of 2π: twice the frequency.

Mirrored

√(−x) exists only for x ≤ 0: the graph is reflected in the y axis.

Composition: one function after another

What you are looking at

f (grey) and g (green, dashed), then f(g(x)) (blue) and g(f(x)) (orange). The probe follows one input x through g and then f.

Composition is a production line: x goes into machine g, its output goes into machine f. Write it f(g(x)) or (f∘g)(x) and read it from the inside out. Changing the order usually changes the result completely.

$$(f\circ g)(x) = f\big(g(x)\big), \qquad f\circ g \neq g\circ f \text{ in general}$$

Worked example

f = x², g = x + 1, x = 2: g gives 3, f gives 9. The other way: f gives 4, g gives 5. (x + 1)² ≠ x² + 1.

Common mistake

Applying f first because it is written first. The function nearest to x acts first.

Try it

Drag the probe and read the chain x → g → f in the HUD.

Order matters

(x + 1)² is the parabola moved left by 1; x² + 1 is it moved up by 1.

sin(x²) vs (sin x)²

sin(x²) oscillates faster and faster as x grows; (sin x)² keeps a steady rhythm and never goes negative.

√ after ²

√(x²) = |x|, not x: squaring forgets the sign and the square root cannot bring it back.

Inverse functions

What you are looking at

f (blue), its inverse (orange) when it exists, the mirror line y = x, and a probe point with its mirror image. The horizontal line through the probe tests whether an inverse exists.

The inverse runs the machine backwards: given the output, find the input. Swapping input and output means swapping x and y — reflecting the graph in the line y = x. It only works if every output comes from exactly one input: every horizontal line must hit the graph at most once.

$$y = f(x) \iff x = f^{-1}(y), \qquad f\big(f^{-1}(y)\big) = y$$
  1. Solve y = f(x) for x, then rename the variables.
  2. Check with the horizontal-line test; if it fails, restrict the domain.

Worked example

y = x³ → x = ∛y, so f⁻¹(x) = ∛x. y = x² fails (y = 4 comes from ±2); restricted to x ≥ 0 its inverse is √x.

Common mistake

Confusing f⁻¹(x) with 1/f(x). The inverse of x³ is ∛x, not 1/x³.

Exponential and logarithm

ln is the inverse of eˣ: their graphs are mirror images across y = x.

x² has no inverse

The horizontal line hits twice: from the output alone you cannot tell whether the input was positive or negative.

Restricting the domain

Keep only x ≥ 0: now the test passes, and the inverse is √x.

Polynomials from their roots

What you are looking at

The polynomial a(x − r₁)(x − r₂)…, its roots on the axis (drag the first one) and its y-intercept. The HUD shows the expanded form.

Writing a polynomial as a product of factors tells you its roots immediately: the product is zero exactly when one factor is. The leading coefficient and the degree decide what happens far away.

$$p(x) = a\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x - r_i), \qquad p(0) = a\prod(-r_i)$$
  1. Roots: where the graph meets the axis.
  2. A root repeated an even number of times: the graph touches and turns back. Odd: it crosses (a triple root crosses flat).
  3. End behaviour: like a·xⁿ — odd degree goes opposite ways, even degree the same way.

Worked example

0.5(x + 2)(x − 0.5)(x − 2): roots −2, 0.5, 2; y-intercept 0.5·2·(−0.5)·(−2) = 1; down on the left, up on the right.

Common mistake

Sign of the roots: the factor (x + 2) gives the root x = −2.

A double root

(x + 2)² does not change sign: the graph touches the axis and turns back.

Negative leading coefficient

The whole graph is flipped: now up on the left, down on the right.

Degree 4

Even degree, positive lead: both ends go up, with up to three turns in between.

A triple root

(x − 1)³ crosses the axis but is flat there, like x³ at the origin.

Rational functions

What you are looking at

A ratio of polynomials, with vertical asymptotes (dashed) where the denominator is zero, the horizontal or slanted line it approaches far away, its zeros, and holes.

Dividing by something that gets close to zero produces huge numbers: near each zero of the denominator the graph shoots off to ±∞ — a vertical asymptote. Far away the highest powers win, and their ratio says what the graph flattens toward.

$$f(x) = a\frac{(x - z_1)\cdots}{(x - p_1)\cdots}$$
  1. Bottom degree bigger: f → 0.
  2. Same degree: f → a (ratio of the leading coefficients).
  3. Top degree one more: f follows a slanted line (found by long division).
  4. A factor on top and bottom cancels, leaving a hole, not an asymptote.

Worked example

(x − 1)/(x − 2): vertical asymptote x = 2, horizontal y = 1, zero at x = 1. At x = 2.01 it is 101; at 1.99, −99.

Common mistake

Thinking a graph can never cross its horizontal asymptote: it can, in the middle; the asymptote is only about the far ends.

1/x

Two branches, asymptotes on both axes.

Two walls

1/(x² − 1): asymptotes at ±1; between them the graph is negative.

A hole

(x − 2)/((x + 1)(x − 2)) = 1/(x + 1) except at x = 2, where it is undefined: a single missing point.

A slanted asymptote

Quadratic over linear: far away the graph follows a straight, tilted line.
