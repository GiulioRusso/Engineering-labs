Chi-square goodness of fit

What you are looking at

Left: observed counts per category (blue bars) against the counts expected if H₀ is true (orange dashed). For the die, rolls accumulate as time runs. Top right: the χ² distribution with k − 1 degrees of freedom, the observed statistic (orange), and the p-value (red tail). Bottom right: each category's contribution to χ². Below: χ² as the rolls come in, against the 5 % critical value.

Is this die fair? Are these peas in Mendel's 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 ratio? A goodness-of-fit test compares counts with what a model predicts. For each category, square the gap between observed and expected and divide by expected, then add them up. The division makes a miss of 10 on an expected 20 count much more than a miss of 10 on an expected 1000. If the model is right, the total follows (approximately) a χ² distribution with k − 1 degrees of freedom. One degree is lost because the counts must add up to n.

$$\chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i}, \qquad E_i = n\,p_i, \qquad \text{df} = k - 1, \qquad E[\chi^2] = \text{df}$$
  1. Expected counts from the model: n times each probability.
  2. Add up (O − E)²/E.
  3. Compare with χ²(k − 1); the approximation needs every E of at least about 5.

Worked example

120 rolls of a fair die, expected 20 per face: observed 16, 21, 22, 18, 26, 17 give χ² = 0.8 + 0.05 + 0.2 + 0.2 + 1.8 + 0.45 = 3.5 on 5 df. Its expected value is 5 and the 5 % critical value is 11.07, so p = 0.62: nothing unusual.

Common mistake

“Not significant, so the die is fair.” A small sample cannot detect a modest bias; the test only says the data don't contradict fairness.

Try it

Load the die (six at 0.25) and watch the χ² line below: it wanders at first, then climbs steadily past the critical value as the rolls pile up.

Loaded, 60 rolls

The six has probability 1 in 4, yet in 60 rolls it came up only 10 times, exactly as expected for a fair die. χ² = 5.0, p = 0.42: the bias is real but invisible at this sample size.

Loaded, 600 rolls

150 sixes against an expected 100: that cell alone adds 25 to χ² = 31.4, and p = 0.000008. With enough data, small biases show up.

Mendel's peas

556 peas from a dihybrid cross: 315 round-yellow, 108 round-green, 101 wrinkled-yellow, 32 wrinkled-green, against expected 313, 104, 104, 35 for 9 : 3 : 3 : 1. χ² = 0.47 on 3 df, p = 0.93: an excellent fit. Fisher (1936) noticed that Mendel's results are, taken together, almost too good to be true.

Chi-square test of independence

What you are looking at

Left: a 2 × 3 table of patients (treatment or placebo, by outcome), each cell showing the observed count and, in brackets, the count expected if outcome and treatment were unrelated. Below the table, the share of each row in each outcome. Top right: χ² with 2 degrees of freedom. Bottom right: the Pearson residual of each cell. Below: the typical p-value for this effect size against the number of patients.

Does the outcome depend on the treatment? If the two were independent, each row would split across the outcomes in the same proportions as the whole table. So the expected count in a cell is its row total times its column total, divided by n. The same χ² sum then measures how far the table is from independence. It has (rows − 1)(columns − 1) degrees of freedom, because once the margins are fixed only that many cells are free. The test says whether there is an association, and Cramér's V says how strong it is.

$$E_{ij} = \frac{R_i\,C_j}{n}, \qquad \chi^2 = \sum_{i,j}\frac{(O_{ij}-E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}}, \qquad \text{df} = (r-1)(c-1), \qquad V = \sqrt{\frac{\chi^2}{n\,\min(r-1,\,c-1)}}$$
  1. Row and column totals; expected counts from them.
  2. Residuals (O − E)/√E show where the table departs: beyond about ±2 is notable.
  3. χ² is the sum of the squared residuals.

Worked example

200 patients: treatment 54 improved, 34 same, 18 worse; placebo 23, 30, 41. Expected for treatment–improved: 106 × 77 / 200 = 40.8. χ² = 21.1 on 2 df, p = 0.00003, V = 0.32: a modest-to-strong association, mostly from the improved and worse columns.

Common mistake

Reading a tiny p as a big effect. With thousands of subjects even a negligible difference is “significant”; always report an effect size such as V, or the difference in proportions.

Try it

Fix the strength at 0.15 and raise the number of patients: the expected p-value curve drops below 0.05 near 450 patients, even though nothing about the treatment changed.

No association

Both rows drawn from the same proportions: χ² = 1.36, p = 0.51, V = 0.08. The bars differ only by chance.

Strong association

70 of 111 treated patients improved against 10 of 89 on placebo: χ² = 76.6, V = 0.62.

Tiny effect, 2000 patients

χ² = 6.28, p = 0.043: significant. But V = 0.056, and the share improving differs by only 3.4 percentage points. Statistically detectable is not the same as clinically important.

About the model

Seeded rolls and multinomial tables. The χ² approximation is used throughout; with expected counts below about 5, an exact test (Fisher's, or a simulation) is better. Mendel's counts from his 1866 paper; R. A. Fisher, “Has Mendel's work been rediscovered?”, Annals of Science 1 (1936). Tests: χ² table values; a fair die rejects at about the nominal 5 % rate; expected counts reproduce the margins.
