The Galton board

What you are looking at

Balls falling through n rows of pegs; at each peg they go right with probability p. The histogram counts where they land; the curve is the binomial prediction (and the normal approximation).

Each ball makes n independent left/right choices. Its final position is the number of "rights". Many paths lead to the middle, very few to the edges: there is only one way to go right every time, but many ways to go right half the time. Counting those paths gives the binomial distribution.

$$P(k) = \binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}, \qquad \mu = np, \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{np(1-p)}$$
  1. One ball is unpredictable; thousands make a predictable shape.
  2. With many rows the binomial looks like a bell: the normal curve (central limit theorem).

Worked example

n = 10, p = 0.5: the middle bin gets C(10,5)/2¹⁰ = 252/1024 = 24.6 % of the balls; the edges 0.1 % each. σ = 1.58.

Common mistake

The gambler's fallacy: after five "rights" in a row, the next is still 50/50 — the pegs have no memory.

Try it

Drop only 50 balls: the histogram is ragged. 1000 balls: it hugs the curve.

Few rows

Four rows give five bins: \(P = \tfrac1{16}, \tfrac4{16}, \tfrac6{16}, \tfrac4{16}, \tfrac1{16}\). The middle is most likely because the most paths lead there (6 of 16).

Biased pegs

\(p = 0.7\): the pile moves right to \(np = 7\) and becomes a little lopsided.

Many rows

With 24 rows the histogram is almost the normal curve with the same \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) (de Moivre–Laplace). The sum of many small independent pushes is normal.

Few balls

50 balls give a ragged histogram: frequencies fluctuate around the probabilities by about \(\sqrt{NP}\) balls per bin. More trials, smaller relative noise (law of large numbers).

Sums of dice

What you are looking at

The histogram of the sum of k dice over many throws, and the normal curve with the same mean and spread.

One die is flat: every face equally likely. Two dice already make a triangle (7 has six combinations, 2 only one). Add more and the shape turns into a bell, whatever the starting distribution: this is the central limit theorem, the reason the normal distribution is everywhere.

$$\mu = 3.5\,k, \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{\tfrac{35}{12}k} \approx 1.71\sqrt k$$

Worked example

Ten dice: mean 35, σ = 5.4; about 68 % of throws land between 30 and 40.

Common mistake

Expecting the spread to grow like k: it grows like √k, so relative to the mean the sum gets more predictable.

One die

A uniform distribution: six equal bars, nothing bell-shaped.

Three dice

Already a smooth hump: 10 and 11 are the most likely sums (27 ways each out of 216).

Ten dice

The exact distribution and the normal curve differ by less than 0.002 everywhere.

About the model

A seeded random generator, so the same experiment replays every time. Tests: binomial mean, simulated mean within 3 standard errors, two-dice frequency of 7, ten dice vs normal (max difference < 0.002).
