The normal curve and z-scores

What you are looking at

Top: the scores of a class as a bell in its own units, shaded up to your score x; the dashed lines sit one and two standard deviations either side of the mean. Bottom: the standard normal bell with the classic bands (green within ±1σ, blue from 1σ to 2σ, grey from 2σ to 3σ) and your z-score as a vertical line. Below: Φ(z), the share of the bell to the left of z, which is your percentile.

Heights, exam scores, measurement errors: many quantities pile up in the same bell shape, the normal distribution. Its two numbers are the mean μ (where the peak sits) and the standard deviation σ (how wide it is). Every normal bell is the same curve, only shifted and stretched. Subtract μ and divide by σ and any bell becomes the standard normal, with mean 0 and standard deviation 1. The result z says how many standard deviations a value sits from the mean, and a single table, or curve, Φ(z), then gives the share below it for every normal at once.

$$z = \frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}, \qquad P(X \le x) = \Phi(z), \qquad P(|Z|\le 1) \approx 68\,\%,\; P(|Z|\le 2) \approx 95\,\%,\; P(|Z|\le 3) \approx 99.7\,\%$$
  1. Subtract the mean: the bell moves so its peak is at 0.
  2. Divide by the standard deviation: the bell is squeezed or stretched to width 1. The shaded area does not change.
  3. Read Φ(z), the area to the left: the share of values below x.

Worked example

Exam A has mean 70 and standard deviation 8; you score 82. Then z = (82 − 70)/8 = 1.5 and Φ(1.5) = 93.3 %: you beat 93 % of the class. The top 10 % starts at z = 1.28, which is 70 + 1.28 × 8 = 80.3 points.

Common mistake

Comparing raw scores from different tests. A 75 can be a better result than an 82 when the test was harder or the class more bunched: compare z-scores.

Try it

Keep x one σ above μ while you change σ: the top bell changes width but the shaded share stays at 84 %, and the bottom line stays at z = 1.

Two exams: who did better?

82 in exam A (mean 70, sd 8) is z = 1.5, the 93rd percentile. 75 in exam B (mean 60, sd 5) is z = 3, the 99.9th. The lower score is the rarer result: only 1 student in 740 does that well in B.

IQ 130

IQ tests are scaled to mean 100 and standard deviation 15, so 130 is z = 2: above 97.7 % of people, about 1 in 44.

One σ above the mean

z = 1 is always the 84th percentile: half the bell is below the mean, and another 34 % is between the mean and one σ above. Move σ and see the top bell change while the bottom one does not.

Two variables: joint, marginal, conditional

What you are looking at

Left: 1500 pairs (X, Y), for example a person's height and weight, as a cloud of dots. The dashed ellipses hold 39 % and 86 % of the pairs, and the red line is the mean of Y for each X. Above and to the right of the cloud are its two shadows: the distribution of X alone and of Y alone. The orange dots are a vertical slice of the cloud, the pairs with X close to the chosen value. Top right: Y in that slice against Y alone. Bottom right: the distribution of the sum X + Y.

Two random quantities are described together by their joint distribution, here the cloud. Looking at one variable and ignoring the other gives its marginal distribution, the cloud's shadow on one axis. Fixing one and looking at the other gives the conditional distribution, a slice of the cloud. When the two are correlated, the slice is narrower than the shadow and its centre moves with X: knowing X tells you something about Y. The covariance measures how they move together. It also decides how wide a sum is: variables that move together add their swings, and opposite ones cancel them.

$$\text{Cov}(X,Y) = \rho\,\sigma_X\sigma_Y, \qquad E[Y\mid X=x] = \rho\frac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X}x, \qquad \text{sd}(Y\mid X) = \sigma_Y\sqrt{1-\rho^2}, \qquad \text{Var}(X+Y) = \sigma_X^2+\sigma_Y^2+2\,\text{Cov}(X,Y)$$
  1. Project the cloud on each axis: the marginals ignore the other variable entirely.
  2. Slice the cloud at one X: what is left is the distribution of Y for that X.
  3. Add the two: the spread of the sum depends on the covariance, not only on the two spreads.

Worked example

σX = 1, σY = 1.5, ρ = 0.7. At X = 1 the slice has mean 0.7 × 1.5 × 1 = 1.05 and standard deviation 1.5 × √(1 − 0.49) = 1.07, against 1.5 for Y alone. The sum has variance 1 + 2.25 + 2 × 1.05 = 5.35, standard deviation 2.31, against 1.80 if X and Y were independent.

Common mistake

Adding standard deviations, or adding variances of correlated quantities without the covariance term. Two investments that rise and fall together are riskier than their separate risks suggest.

Try it

Slide ρ from 0 to 0.95 with the slice fixed: the orange histogram narrows and walks away from the dashed one. Then take ρ negative and watch the sum on the bottom right become narrower than either variable.

Independent

ρ = 0: the cloud is an upright ellipse, the red line is flat, and every slice looks like the whole Y shadow. Knowing X tells you nothing about Y.

Strong correlation

ρ = 0.95: the slice is only √(1 − 0.9) = 31 % as wide as Y alone. A good predictor narrows the uncertainty this much; ρ = 0.5 narrows it by only 13 %.

Negative correlation: hedging

ρ = −0.8 with equal spreads: Var(X + Y) = 1 + 1 − 1.6 = 0.4, a standard deviation of 0.63, smaller than either variable alone. This is why a portfolio of assets that move against each other is safer than either asset.
