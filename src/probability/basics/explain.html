Outcomes and events

What you are looking at

Left: the 36 outcomes of rolling two dice, one dot each. Event A is blue, event B orange, outcomes in both green. Top right: the probability of each sum. Bottom right: P(A), P(B), P(A and B), P(A or B).

An experiment has a set of possible outcomes (the sample space). An event is a set of outcomes — "the sum is 7" is six of the 36 dots. When every outcome is equally likely, a probability is just counting.

$$P(A)=\frac{\text{outcomes in }A}{\text{all outcomes}},\qquad P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)$$
  1. List (or picture) all outcomes, making sure they are equally likely.
  2. Count those in the event.
  3. Divide. For "A or B", add the two counts but subtract the green ones, counted twice.

Worked example

"At least one 6": 6 + 6 − 1 = 11 cells (the (6,6) cell is in both rows), so 11/36 ≈ 0.31 — not 2/6.

Common mistake

Treating the sums 2…12 as equally likely. They are not: 7 has six outcomes, 2 has one.

Sum = 7

The diagonal of six cells: 6/36 = 1/6, the most likely sum.

At least one 6

The last row and column: 11 cells.

Disjoint

A sum of 7 can never be a double: no green cells, so P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B).

Counting: choices and the birthday problem

What you are looking at

Left: Pascal's triangle, with C(n, k) outlined. Top right: all C(n, k) for your n. Bottom right: the probability that two people in a room share a birthday.

When outcomes are too many to draw, we count them. The number of ways to choose k objects out of n, ignoring order, is the binomial coefficient — row n, position k of Pascal's triangle.

$$\binom nk=\frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!},\qquad P(\text{shared birthday})=1-\frac{365\cdot364\cdots(365-g+1)}{365^g}$$

Worked example

Lottery 6 of 49: C(49, 6) ≈ 14 million tickets. Birthday: with 23 people there are 253 pairs, enough to make a match more likely than not.

Common mistake

Counting "at least one" directly. Count the complement ("none") and subtract from 1.

C(10, 5)

252 ways to pick 5 of 10 — the middle of row 10.

23 people

P ≈ 0.507: the famous surprise.

Conditional probability, as areas

What you are looking at

Left: a unit square of probability. The left column is A (width P(A)), the right "not A". Inside each column the lower part is B, with height P(B | that column). Right: the same as a tree diagram, and the key numbers.

P(B | A) is the probability of B once we know A happened: we zoom into the A column and look at the share that is B. Areas are joint probabilities: P(A and B) = P(A)·P(B | A).

$$P(B)=P(A)P(B\mid A)+P(\bar A)P(B\mid\bar A),\qquad P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A)P(B\mid A)}{P(B)}$$
  1. Multiply along a branch of the tree to get a joint probability.
  2. Add the branches that end in B to get P(B).
  3. Divide one branch by the total to reverse the condition (Bayes).

Worked example

P(A) = 0.3, P(B|A) = 0.8, P(B|¬A) = 0.1: P(B) = 0.24 + 0.07 = 0.31, P(A|B) = 0.24/0.31 ≈ 0.77.

Common mistake

Confusing P(B | A) with P(A | B) — "most smokers don't get cancer" vs "most lung-cancer patients smoked".

Independent

The B parts have the same height in both columns: knowing A changes nothing, and P(A and B) = P(A)·P(B).

Rare A

Even with a reliable B, most of the B area lies in "not A": P(A | B) stays low (see Bayes' theorem).

The law of large numbers

What you are looking at

Five runs of repeated trials (a coin with P(success) = p). Each line is the fraction of successes so far, against the number of trials on a log scale; the dashed band is p ± 2 standard errors.

Probability is what relative frequency settles to in the long run. Early frequencies are wild; as n grows they squeeze toward p, with a typical error that shrinks like 1/√n.

$$\frac{S_n}{n}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}p,\qquad \text{typical error}\approx\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}$$

Common mistake

The gambler's fallacy: after five tails, heads is not "due". The frequency converges because later trials swamp the early ones, not because the coin compensates.

p = 0.05

Rare successes: many trials pass before the first one, and the relative error stays large for longer.

Expected value and spread

What you are looking at

Left: the outcomes of a game and their probabilities; the triangle and orange line mark the mean (the balance point), the green bar ± one standard deviation. Right: the average winnings per play as you keep playing.
$$E[X]=\sum_x x\,P(x),\qquad \sigma^2=\sum_x(x-E[X])^2P(x)$$

The expected value is the long-run average per play. The standard deviation measures how far single outcomes typically land from it.

Worked example

Roulette, €1 on red: E = 1·18/37 − 1·19/37 = −1/37 ≈ −€0.027 per play.

Common mistake

Judging a game by its best prize or its most likely outcome. A lottery's top prize is huge, its mean still negative.

Roulette

The running average sinks toward −0.027.

Lottery

The average jumps up at each rare prize and drifts back down.
