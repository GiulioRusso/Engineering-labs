Sampling distributions

What you are looking at

Left: as time runs, 600 samples of size n are drawn and the chosen statistic of each is added to the histogram. The orange curve is a normal fitted to them, the green dashed curve (for the mean) is the theory, and the black dashed line is the true value. Top right: the population and the latest sample. Bottom right: the spread of the statistic (its standard error) against n, with the σ/√n line. Below: the standard error estimated from the first k samples.

Any number computed from random data is itself random: take another sample and you get another value. The distribution of those values is the sampling distribution, and its standard deviation is the standard error. It answers “how far off could my estimate be?”. For the mean it is σ/√n, whatever the population's shape. Quadrupling the data halves the error. Other statistics have sampling distributions too, with their own spread, and some of them are biased: on average they miss the true value.

$$\operatorname{SE}(\bar x) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}, \qquad \operatorname{SE}(\text{median}) \approx 1.25\,\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\ \text{(normal parent)}, \qquad E[s] < \sigma$$
  1. Draw a sample of n, compute the statistic, and repeat many times.
  2. The histogram of results is the sampling distribution; its width is the standard error.
  3. In practice we have one sample, so σ is replaced by s: SE ≈ s/√n.

Worked example

Exponential waiting times (mean 1, σ = 1), n = 10: the 600 sample means average 1.01 with a spread of 0.311, against the theory σ/√10 = 0.316. With n = 40 the spread is 0.155, half as much.

Common mistake

Confusing the standard deviation (the spread of the data, which does not shrink with n) with the standard error (the spread of the estimate, which does).

Try it

Switch between mean, median and sd at the same n and compare the widths of the left histogram.

n = 40

Four times the data: standard error 0.155 instead of 0.311, and the histogram is a clean bell even though the population is very skewed (the central limit theorem).

The median

From a normal population with n = 30, the median's standard error is 0.110, against 0.091 for the mean (0.5/√30). For normal data the median wastes information: it needs about 1.57 times as many values for the same precision. For heavy-tailed data it can win.

The sd is biased

With n = 4 from the exponential population, the sample standard deviation averages 0.85 against σ = 1: it underestimates, even with the n − 1 divisor (which makes the variance unbiased, not its square root).

Three uniform values

The mean of only three values from a flat distribution is already close to a bell: averaging smooths shapes quickly unless the population has a long tail.

Maximum likelihood

What you are looking at

Left: the data, and what the model would predict with the trial value you set (for the coin: observed against expected counts). Top right: the log-likelihood, the total log-probability of the data, against the parameter; the dot is your trial value and the dashed line the maximum. Bottom right: the likelihood divided by its peak, for these data (blue) and for four times as many (green). Below: its slope, the score.

A model with an unknown parameter θ gives every possible data set a probability. Turn it around: for the data we actually got, which θ makes them most probable? That θ is the maximum-likelihood estimate. For a coin it is the share of heads; for normal measurements, the sample mean; for exponential waits, one over the mean wait. The curvature of the log-likelihood at the peak gives the standard error. A sharp peak means the data pin θ down, and the peak sharpens as data grow.

$$\ell(\theta) = \sum_i \log f(x_i\mid\theta), \qquad \hat\theta:\ \frac{d\ell}{d\theta} = 0, \qquad \operatorname{SE}(\hat\theta) \approx \Big(-\frac{d^2\ell}{d\theta^2}\Big)^{-1/2}$$
  1. Write the probability of each observation under the model.
  2. Take logs and add (products of small numbers underflow; sums do not).
  3. Find the peak, by calculus or numerically.

Worked example

14 heads in 20 flips: ℓ(p) = 14 ln p + 6 ln(1 − p), and dℓ/dp = 14/p − 6/(1 − p) = 0 gives p̂ = 0.7, with SE = √(0.7 × 0.3 / 20) = 0.10. A fair coin (p = 0.5) is 1.65 lower in log-likelihood: the data are e^1.65 ≈ 5 times less probable under it.

Common mistake

Reading the likelihood as the probability that θ is the true value. It is the probability of the data given θ; turning it into a probability for θ needs a prior (see Bayesian updating).

Try it

Slide the trial value: the slope chart crosses zero exactly where the log-likelihood peaks.

At the peak

p = 0.7: the expected counts (14 and 6) match the observed ones, and the score is zero.

Four times the flips

56 heads in 80: the same estimate, half the standard error (0.051), and the fair coin is now 6.6 below the peak, e^6.6 ≈ 700 times less likely.

Measurements

Twenty values with σ = 2 known: the log-likelihood is an exact parabola, peaking at the sample mean 5.96 with SE 2/√20 = 0.45. Least squares is maximum likelihood for normal errors.

Waiting times

Twenty waits with a mean of 2.34 min: λ̂ = 1/2.34 = 0.427 per minute (true 0.4), SE λ̂/√n = 0.095. The curve is not symmetric; the likelihood falls off faster towards small λ.

The bootstrap

What you are looking at

Left: the histogram of the statistic over resamples as they accumulate, with the 95 % percentile interval (orange), the estimate from the original sample (black), and the true value (green dashed). Top right: the original sample (bottom row) and one resample, in which repeated values stack up. Bottom right: the bootstrap interval against the textbook t interval. Below: the interval's ends as resamples accumulate.

To get a standard error we would like to repeat the experiment many times, but we only have one sample. The bootstrap (Efron, 1979) treats that sample as a stand-in for the population. Draw n values from it with replacement, so some appear twice and some not at all, and recompute the statistic. Repeat a thousand times. The spread of the results estimates the standard error, and their 2.5 and 97.5 percentiles give a 95 % interval. It works for statistics with no simple formula, such as the median, a ratio or a correlation.

$$x^*_1,\dots,x^*_n \sim \text{uniform from } \{x_1,\dots,x_n\}, \qquad \operatorname{SE}^* = \operatorname{sd}(\hat\theta^*_1,\dots,\hat\theta^*_B), \qquad \text{CI} = [\hat\theta^*_{(2.5\%)},\ \hat\theta^*_{(97.5\%)}]$$
  1. Resample n values with replacement; compute the statistic.
  2. Repeat B times (1000 is plenty for an interval).
  3. Read the spread and the percentiles.

Worked example

Twenty waiting times (true mean 10 min) with sample mean 9.37: bootstrap SE 1.35, 95 % interval [6.75, 12.1]. The t formula gives [6.40, 12.3], close but symmetric; the bootstrap follows the skew of the data.

Common mistake

Expecting the bootstrap to create information. It can only reshuffle the sample: with very few values, or for extremes such as the maximum, its intervals are too narrow.

Try it

Switch to the median: the histogram turns lumpy, because a median of resampled values can only take a few distinct values.

The median

Sample median 6.79 (true 6.93): interval [3.9, 13.1], SE 2.84, wider than for the mean, and lopsided. There is no simple textbook formula for it; the bootstrap doesn't need one.

80 values

Mean 10.1, interval [8.31, 11.9]: about half as wide as with 20, as 1/√n predicts.

Six values

Interval [5.7, 18.1] around a mean of 12.0. With six values the resamples can only recombine those six. The interval contains the truth here, but its 95 % label is optimistic.

About the model

Seeded draws, so each visit shows the same experiment. The SE-against-n chart uses 300 samples per n. The standard error from the likelihood's curvature uses the observed Fisher information. Bootstrap: 1000 resamples, percentile intervals (B. Efron, “Bootstrap methods: another look at the jackknife”, Annals of Statistics 7, 1979). Tests: the mean's standard error matches σ/√n; the MLE sits where the score is zero; bootstrap intervals cover the truth at about the stated rate.
