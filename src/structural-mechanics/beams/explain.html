A beam carries loads sideways to its supports by bending. Statics tells us the reactions; cutting the beam anywhere tells us the internal shear force and bending moment; the moment curves the beam and sets the stress in it.

The simply supported beam

What you are looking at

Left: the beam on a pin and a roller, the load (red) and the reactions (green). The blue line is the deflected shape, exaggerated by the factor written below it. Top right: the shear force V(x). Bottom right: the bending moment M(x). Below: the real deflection in millimetres.

The two supports push up just enough to balance the load and its turning effect. Cut the beam at x: the shear V is the net vertical force on the left piece, and the moment M is its net turning effect. M is largest where V crosses zero.

$$R_1=\frac{P\,b}{L},\quad R_2=\frac{P\,a}{L},\qquad \frac{dM}{dx}=V,\quad \frac{dV}{dx}=-w,\qquad EI\,\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}=M$$
  1. Reactions from ΣF = 0 and ΣM = 0 about one support.
  2. V(x): start from R₁, drop by P at the load (or by w per metre).
  3. M(x): the area under V, zero at both pins.
  4. Deflection: integrate M/EI twice, with y = 0 at both supports.

Worked example

P = 10 kN at midspan, L = 4 m, EI = 200 GPa × 6.67·10⁻⁵ m⁴ = 13.3 MN·m². M_max = PL/4 = 10 kN·m; δ = PL³/(48EI) = 10⁴·64/(48·1.33·10⁷) = 1.0 mm. Stress M c/I = 10⁴·0.1/6.67·10⁻⁵ = 15 MPa.

Common mistakes

Forgetting that the moment is zero at a pin, and mixing up signs: here a sagging moment (tension at the bottom) is positive.

Try it

Slide the load towards a support: the reaction there grows, the peak moment shrinks, and the peak moves with the load.

Off-centre load

a = 1 m: R₁ = 7.5 kN, R₂ = 2.5 kN. The moment peaks under the load at R₁·a = 7.5 kN·m.

Uniform load

V falls linearly, M is a parabola with M_max = wL²/8 at midspan, and δ = 5wL⁴/(384EI).

Twice the span

Deflection grows as L³ for a point load: double the span, eight times the sag (and twice the moment).

The cantilever

What you are looking at

Left: a beam built into a wall at x = 0 and free at the other end, its load, and the wall's reaction. The blue line is the deflected shape, exaggerated. Right: shear and bending moment. Below: the deflection in millimetres.

A cantilever has only one support, so the wall must provide a force and a moment. The moment is largest at the wall and hogging (tension on top); the beam leaves the wall horizontally and droops towards the tip.

$$M(0)=-P\,a,\qquad \delta_{\text{tip}}=\frac{PL^3}{3EI}\ (\text{tip load}),\qquad \delta_{\text{tip}}=\frac{wL^4}{8EI}\ (\text{uniform})$$

Worked example

P = 10 kN at the tip of a 4 m cantilever, EI = 13.3 MN·m²: δ = 10⁴·64/(3·1.33·10⁷) = 16 mm, sixteen times the simply supported beam with the same load.

Common mistake

Putting the largest moment at the load. In a cantilever it is always at the wall, where the whole load has the longest lever arm.

Try it

Move the point load from the tip toward the wall: the tip deflection falls fast, yet the tip still moves, because the beam beyond the load stays straight but tilted.

Load halfway

The beam beyond the load carries no moment and stays straight, but it is tilted, so the tip still moves: 5PL³/(48EI).

Uniform load

The moment at the wall is wL²/2, and the tip drops by wL⁴/(8EI).

Cross sections and bending stress

What you are looking at

Left: the cross section with its neutral axis (dashed) and arrows for the stress across the depth: compression (blue) above, tension (orange) below. Top right: the stress against height, a straight line, with the yield stress dashed. Bottom right: b(y)·y², how much each layer contributes to the second moment I. Below: outer-fibre stress against the moment.

Under a moment, plane sections stay plane: the strain, and so the stress, grows linearly from zero at the neutral axis. A layer far from the axis carries more stress and has a longer lever arm, which is why the second moment weighs area by y².

$$\sigma(y)=-\frac{M\,y}{I},\qquad I=\int y^2\,dA,\qquad I_{\text{rect}}=\frac{bh^3}{12}$$

Worked example

An I-beam 100 × 200 mm with 12 mm flanges and an 8 mm web: A = 38.1 cm², I = 2490 cm⁴. A solid rectangle with the same area and depth has I = 1270 cm⁴, so the I-beam is almost twice as stiff for the same steel.

Common mistake

Making the flanges thicker instead of the beam deeper. I grows as h³: depth is the cheapest stiffness there is, until the web gets thin enough to buckle.

Try it

Double the depth h of the I-beam: the bending stress roughly halves and the stiffness grows more than 4×, for little more steel.

Solid rectangle

Same area, same depth, but much of the steel sits near the axis where it does little: smaller I, higher stress.

Twice as deep

h from 200 to 400 mm adds only web: 1.4 times the steel, about 5 times the second moment.
