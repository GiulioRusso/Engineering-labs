The common-emitter amplifier

What you are looking at

Left: a transistor with its base fed by a small signal on top of a DC bias, a collector resistor R_C to the supply, and optionally an emitter resistor R_E. Top right: the output in time — real (blue) and what a perfectly linear amplifier would give (dashed). Bottom right: the transfer curve, output against input; its slope is the gain.

A transistor converts an input voltage into a current (g_m). To get a voltage back, push that current through a resistor. When the input goes up, more current flows, R_C drops more voltage, and the collector goes down: the stage inverts.

$$A_v=\frac{v_\text{out}}{v_\text{in}}=-\frac{g_m (R_C\parallel r_o)}{1+g_m R_E}\;\approx\;-\frac{R_C}{R_E}\ \ (\text{when } g_m R_E\gg1)$$
  1. Bias: pick I_C (here 1 mA) so that V_C sits mid-way between the rails.
  2. Gain: g_m = I_C/V_T = 38.7 mS; with R_C = 4.7 kΩ, A_v ≈ −182 (−166 with the transistor's own r_o).
  3. Swing: the output can move up to V_CC and down to about 0.2 V. Too large an input hits a rail: clipping.

Worked example

5 mV in, gain −166: 0.83 V out, inverted. 20 mV in would ask for 3.3 V — but the exponential makes the downward half (more current) larger than the upward half: visibly lopsided.

Common mistakes

Forgetting the minus sign. Expecting gain to keep growing with R_C: a larger R_C also pushes the Q point towards saturation.

Try it

Raise the input until the blue trace flattens at the top or bottom, then add R_E: the gain drops but the shape becomes clean.

20 mV

The transfer curve is not a straight line over ±20 mV: one half-cycle is stretched, the other squashed, and soon a rail cuts it off.

Emitter degeneration

With R_E = 470 Ω the gain falls to about −9 ≈ −R_C/R_E, but now it is set by two resistors, not by the exponential: 300 mV in still comes out clean. Trading gain for linearity is the theme of feedback.

The emitter follower (common collector)

What you are looking at

Left: a source with internal resistance R_s drives the base; the output is taken at the emitter, across R_E and the load R_L. Top right: the source (grey), the load voltage with the follower (blue) and if the source drove the load directly (red). Bottom right: how much of the source reaches the load, for any load.

The emitter sits one V_BE below the base and follows it: the voltage gain is just under 1. Its job is different: it is an impedance transformer. The source sees the load multiplied by β + 1; the load sees the source divided by β + 1.

$$A_v=\frac{g_m R}{1+g_m R}\approx1,\qquad R_\text{in}=r_\pi+(\beta+1)R,\qquad R_\text{out}\approx\frac1{g_m}+\frac{R_s}{\beta+1}$$

Worked example

A 10 kΩ source into a 100 Ω speaker directly: 100/10100 = 1%. With a follower (β = 150): R_in ≈ 16 kΩ, so the source keeps 60%, and the follower drives 100 Ω from about 90 Ω: overall about 50% of the signal, 50× more.

Common mistake

"Gain 1 means useless." Power gain is huge; most op-amp output stages are followers.

100 Ω load

Direct, the source collapses; through the follower most of the signal survives.

100 kΩ source

Even a feeble source can drive a small load: its resistance appears divided by 151 at the output.

The differential pair

What you are looking at

Left: two matched transistors whose emitters share a tail current I_tail. Top right: the two collector currents against the input difference — a tanh. Bottom right: in time, the difference between the outputs (blue) stays clean while each single output (grey) is polluted by common-mode noise.

The two currents must always add up to I_tail. So if both inputs move together (common mode), nothing can change: the current has nowhere else to go. Only the difference v₁ − v₂ moves current from one side to the other. That is why every op-amp starts with a differential pair: it ignores noise that hits both wires equally.

$$I_1-I_2=I_\text{tail}\tanh\!\left(\frac{v_1-v_2}{2V_T}\right),\qquad A_{dm}=g_mR_C,\quad A_{cm}\approx\frac{R_C}{2R_\text{tail}},\quad \text{CMRR}=\frac{A_{dm}}{A_{cm}}$$

Worked example

I_tail = 1 mA: each side 0.5 mA, g_m = 19.3 mS, R_C = 5 kΩ → A_dm ≈ 97. A 50 kΩ tail: A_cm = 0.05 → CMRR ≈ 66 dB.

Common mistake

Using a plain resistor as the tail. A real current source (a mirror!) has a huge R_tail, and CMRR rises with it.

100 mV

tanh(100/51.7) ≈ 0.96: almost all the tail current is on one side. The output is a squared-off wave: the pair is a natural limiter.

Weak tail

2 kΩ tail: common-mode gain 1.25, CMRR only 38 dB. The noise leaks into each output.

The Miller effect

What you are looking at

Left: a gain stage with the small capacitance C_μ between base and collector. Top right: frequency response with the Miller effect (blue) and if C_μ were counted only once (dashed). Bottom right: bandwidth against gain.

C_μ is a few picofarads. But one end moves by v and the other by −|A|·v: the voltage across it is (1 + |A|) v, so it draws (1 + |A|) times the charge. Seen from the input it looks like a capacitor (1 + |A|) times larger. Together with the source resistance it makes a low-pass filter far lower than you would expect.

$$C_\text{in}=C_\pi+C_\mu(1+|A|),\qquad f_{-3\text{dB}}=\frac1{2\pi R_s C_\text{in}}$$

Worked example

|A| = 200, C_μ = 5 pF: 1005 pF from a 5 pF part. With R_s = 1 kΩ: f₋₃dB ≈ 155 kHz instead of 6.4 MHz.

Common mistake

Ignoring a "tiny" feedback capacitance. Cures: low source resistance, a follower in front, or a cascode stage that keeps the collector voltage still.

Gain 400

Twice the gain, half the bandwidth: the product barely moves.

Low source resistance

50 Ω charges the big C_in 20× faster: the pole moves up by 20×.

Negative feedback

What you are looking at

Left: an amplifier A with a fraction β of its output subtracted from the input. Top right: open-loop gain (grey) and closed-loop gain (blue) against frequency. Bottom right: how far the closed-loop gain is from the ideal 1/β, as a function of the raw gain A₀.

Feed back part of the output against the input: the amplifier ends up amplifying only the small error between them. If A is huge, the error becomes tiny and the output settles at whatever makes βv_out = v_in. The gain becomes 1/β — fixed by two resistors, no longer by the transistor.

$$A_{cl}=\frac{A}{1+A\beta}\;\xrightarrow{\;A\beta\gg1\;}\;\frac1\beta,\qquad \frac{\Delta A_{cl}}{A_{cl}}=\frac{1}{1+A\beta}\,\frac{\Delta A}{A},\qquad f_{cl}=f_p(1+A\beta)$$
  1. Loop gain Aβ: the number that decides everything.
  2. Error: 1/(1 + Aβ) of the ideal.
  3. Sensitivity: a change of A is divided by 1 + Aβ.
  4. Bandwidth: multiplied by 1 + Aβ. Gain × bandwidth stays constant.

Worked example

A₀ = 100 dB = 10⁵, β = 0.1: A_cl = 9.999, error 0.01%. Halve A₀: A_cl changes by 0.02%. Pole 10 Hz → closed-loop bandwidth 100 kHz.

Common mistake

Thinking feedback is free: you pay with gain. And with too much phase lag around the loop it turns positive and oscillates (op-amp stability).

A₀ = 40 dB

Loop gain only 10: the closed loop is 9% short of 10 and follows every drift of A.

β = 1%

Ten times more closed-loop gain, ten times less loop gain and bandwidth.

Cascading stages

What you are looking at

Left: the chain of blocks with each one's gain factor, including the "loading" factor at each junction. Top right: the running gain after each block; the dashed line is what you'd get multiplying the stage gains naively. Bottom right: total gain against the final load.

Gains multiply only if each stage does not load the previous one. At every junction the output resistance of one stage and the input resistance of the next form a voltage divider. A common-emitter stage has a large R_out (≈ R_C) and a small R_in (≈ r_π): two of them in a row waste most of the gain.

$$A_\text{tot}=A_1\cdot\frac{R_{in,2}}{R_{out,1}+R_{in,2}}\cdot A_2$$

Worked example

1 mA, 4.7 kΩ: R_out1 ≈ 4.3 kΩ, R_in2 = r_π ≈ 3.9 kΩ → only 47% passes. Insert a follower: it sees a big R_in and presents a small R_out, and most of the gain returns.

Common mistake

Multiplying datasheet gains without checking impedances. Rule: each stage's R_out should be much smaller than the next stage's R_in.

With a follower

Two small losses instead of one big one: the total approaches A₁·A₂.

1 kΩ load

The last stage's own R_C is now in parallel with 1 kΩ: its gain drops too.
