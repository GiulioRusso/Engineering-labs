Thresholds and ROC curves

What you are looking at

Left: the scores a classifier gives to negatives (blue) and positives (gold), drawn in proportion to how common each class is. Everything right of the threshold (dashed) is called positive: green are the positives caught, red the negatives wrongly flagged. Top right: the ROC curve, every threshold at once, with the current one marked (grey: from a finite sample). Bottom right: the four counts per 1000 cases. Below: precision and recall for every threshold.

Most classifiers output a score, not a yes or no. Where you put the threshold is a separate decision: lower it and you catch more positives but also flag more negatives; raise it and the reverse. The ROC curve shows that trade-off for every threshold, plotting the share of positives caught against the share of negatives flagged. A useless score gives the diagonal; a perfect one hugs the top-left corner. The area under it, AUC, has a neat meaning: the chance that a random positive gets a higher score than a random negative.

$$\text{TPR} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}, \quad \text{FPR} = \frac{FP}{FP + TN}, \quad \text{precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}, \qquad \text{AUC} = P(s_+ > s_-) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{d}{\sqrt2}\right)$$
  1. Pick a threshold: count TP, FN, FP, TN.
  2. TPR and FPR give one point of the ROC curve.
  3. Sweep the threshold: the whole curve; its area is the AUC.
  4. Precision also depends on how rare positives are; TPR and FPR do not.

Worked example

Separation d = 2, threshold 1, 30 % positives: recall 84 %, false-positive rate 16 %; per 1000 cases TP 252, FN 48, FP 111, TN 589; precision 69 %. AUC = Φ(2/√2) = 0.92.

Common mistake

Comparing classifiers at one threshold only, or by accuracy. The ROC curve separates how good the scores are (AUC) from where you choose to cut them.

Try it

Slide the threshold and watch the marker run along the ROC curve, and the red and green areas trade places.

A strict threshold

t = 2.5: almost no false alarms (0.6 % of negatives) and 95 % precision, but only 31 % of the positives are found. Right for a costly follow-up, wrong for screening.

Well separated

d = 4: the two humps barely overlap, AUC = 0.998, and a threshold in between catches 98 % of positives while flagging 2 % of negatives.

No signal

d = 0: the scores of both classes are identical, every threshold flags the same share of each, and the ROC curve is the diagonal: AUC = 0.5, a coin toss.

Rare positives

Same scores and threshold, but only 1 % positives: recall is still 84 % and the false-positive rate still 16 %, yet per 1000 cases that means 8 true positives against 157 false ones: precision 5 %. The ROC curve does not move; the precision curve below collapses. With rare events, look at precision.

About the model

Scores are normal: negatives N(0, 1), positives N(d, 1) (the binormal model). Exact rates from Φ, computed with the Abramowitz–Stegun erf approximation (error below 1.5·10⁻⁷); the grey curve is the empirical ROC of 200 + 200 sampled scores. Tests: Φ at known values; the numerical area under the ROC curve equals Φ(d/√2) (10⁻⁵); the empirical ROC's trapezoid area equals the Mann–Whitney pair count exactly and is close to the exact AUC; rare positives keep recall and lower precision below 20 %.
