A model is useful only on data it has not seen. Everything here uses polynomial fits to 20–30 noisy samples of a smooth curve (dashed), and a large separate test set (grey dots) that the fits never touch.

Capacity: training error always improves

What you are looking at

Top: training points (yellow), test points (grey), the true curve (dashed) and the fit (blue). Bottom: training and test error against the polynomial degree, on a log scale.

A degree-d polynomial contains every lower-degree one, so raising d can only lower the training error. Training error measures capacity, not quality. The test error falls while the model learns the curve, then rises when it starts fitting the noise.

Worked example

20 points: degree 3 gives test error ≈ 0.1 (close to the noise σ² = 0.09); degree 12 drives training error toward zero and test error up about fivefold.

Common mistake

Reporting the training score. More data (the "60 points" example) pushes the overfitting point to higher degrees.

Degree 1

A straight line cannot follow the curve: both errors high — underfitting.

Degree 12

The fit threads every training point and swings wildly between them.

60 points

Same degree, three times the data: the swings mostly disappear.

Where the error comes from: bias and variance

What you are looking at

Top: the same model trained on 40 different noisy datasets (grey), their average (blue) and the truth (dashed). Bottom: bias², variance and their sum against the degree.
$$\mathbb E\big[(y-\hat f(x))^2\big]=\underbrace{\big(\mathbb E[\hat f(x)]-f(x)\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2}+\underbrace{\mathbb E\big[(\hat f(x)-\mathbb E[\hat f(x)])^2\big]}_{\text{variance}}+\underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{noise}}$$

Bias is how wrong the model is on average; variance is how much it changes with the particular sample. Flexible models trade one for the other.

Degree 1

All 40 lines nearly identical (low variance) and all equally wrong (high bias).

Degree 10

The average is close to the truth (low bias), but each single fit is wild, especially near the edges (high variance).

Ridge: keep the capacity, penalise the coefficients

What you are looking at

Top: a degree-12 fit with penalty λ. Bottom: training and test error against log λ. Bottom plot: the size of each coefficient.
$$\hat{\mathbf w}=\arg\min_{\mathbf w}\ \|A\mathbf w-\mathbf y\|^2+\lambda\|\mathbf w\|^2=(A^\top A+\lambda I)^{-1}A^\top\mathbf y$$

Wiggles need large coefficients; making them expensive smooths the fit without lowering the degree. λ is a dial from overfitting (λ → 0) to underfitting (λ large).

λ ≈ 0

No penalty: the degree-12 wiggles of plain least squares.

λ = 10

Everything shrunk: the fit flattens toward zero.

Early stopping: a regularizer for free

What you are looking at

The same degree-12 model trained by gradient descent from all-zero weights. Top: the fit as training goes on. Bottom: training and test error against iteration; green = the best moment to stop.

Gradient descent learns the directions the data constrains best first — the broad shape — and the poorly constrained wiggles last. Stopping when the test (validation) error is lowest keeps the weights small, much like ridge.

Choosing without touching the test set

What you are looking at

Top: the training points coloured by fold. Bottom: k-fold cross-validation error against degree (green), compared with the real test error (blue).

Picking d or λ by looking at the test error is cheating: the test set becomes part of training. k-fold cross-validation splits the training data into k parts, trains k times leaving one part out, and averages the errors on the left-out parts.

Common mistake

Shuffling time series or groups (the same patient in train and validation): the folds must be independent.

k = 10

More folds: each model sees 90% of the data; the estimate is less biased but costs 10 fits.

When classes are rare, accuracy lies

What you are looking at

Top: the classifier's score for 920 healthy (blue) and 80 ill (orange) people, and the decision threshold. Bottom: true positives, false negatives, false positives, true negatives. Bottom plot: accuracy, precision and recall against the threshold.
$$\text{precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP},\qquad \text{recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\qquad \text{accuracy}=\frac{TP+TN}{N}$$

Worked example

With 8% positives, answering "healthy" to everyone is 92% accurate and finds no one: recall 0.

Always negative

Accuracy 92%, recall 0%: useless, and accuracy hides it.

Low threshold

Nearly every ill person found (high recall), at the cost of many false alarms (low precision).
