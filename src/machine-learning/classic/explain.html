k-nearest neighbours

What you are looking at

Left: the training points (blue class 0, yellow class 1) and the shading the method gives every spot of the plane. The orange point is a query: grey lines join it to its k nearest neighbours, the circle reaches the farthest of them. Top right: accuracy on the training points and on new points against k. Bottom right: the neighbours' distances, coloured by class.

The simplest classifier there is: to label a new point, look at the k closest labelled points and take a vote. There is no training — the data itself is the model.

$$\hat p(\text{class 1}\mid\mathbf x)=\frac{\#\{\text{class-1 points among the }k\text{ nearest}\}}{k}$$
  1. Measure the distance from the query to every training point.
  2. Keep the k smallest.
  3. The majority class wins.

Worked example

k = 5, neighbours: 3 yellow and 2 blue → class 1, with p̂ = 0.6.

Common mistakes

Judging k on the training points: k = 1 is always 100% right there, because every point is its own nearest neighbour. And forgetting to scale the inputs: a feature in metres and one in millimetres do not weigh the same in a distance.

k = 1

Islands around every noisy point: perfect on training data, worse on new data. Overfitting.

k = 41

The vote spans a wide area: the border is smooth and ignores single noisy points. Push k much higher and the tips of the moons get outvoted by the other class: underfitting.

On the border

Near the boundary the vote is close and changes as k changes: these are the uncertain points.

Decision trees

What you are looking at

Left: the boxes the tree cuts the plane into, shaded by their majority class, with the cut lines in grey. Top right: accuracy on training and new points against the maximum depth. Bottom right: the tree itself (top levels), with the question at each node and the class of each leaf.

A tree asks yes/no questions of the form "is x₁ below 0.3?", one after another. Each question is chosen so that the two groups it creates are as pure as possible.

$$\text{Gini}=2p(1-p),\qquad \text{split}=\arg\min\;\frac{n_L\,\text{Gini}_L+n_R\,\text{Gini}_R}{n}$$

Worked example

A node with 10 points, 5 of each class: Gini = 0.5. A split into (5 blue, 1 yellow) and (0 blue, 4 yellow) gives (6·0.28 + 4·0)/10 = 0.17: a large drop, a good question.

Common mistake

Growing the tree until every leaf is pure. It then has a box for every noisy point. Limit the depth, or average many trees (a random forest).

A stump

One question, two boxes: easy to read, but far too simple for the moons.

Depth 10

Training accuracy reaches 100%; accuracy on new data drops. The tiny boxes are memorized noise.

A diagonal border

Trees can only cut along the axes, so a slanted border becomes a staircase of boxes.

k-means clustering

What you are looking at

Left: the points coloured by the centre they belong to; the black dots are the centres, and their grey trails show how they move. Top right: the inertia (total squared distance of points to their centre) iteration by iteration. Bottom right: the final inertia for every k — the "elbow" plot.

No labels this time: the algorithm must find groups by itself. It alternates two simple steps until nothing changes.

  1. Assign every point to its nearest centre.
  2. Move every centre to the mean of its points.
$$J=\sum_i\big\|\mathbf x_i-\boldsymbol\mu_{c(i)}\big\|^2\quad\text{never increases}$$

Choosing k

J always falls when k grows (k = number of points gives J = 0). Look for the elbow: the k after which adding a cluster gains little.

Common mistake

Trusting one run. The result depends on where the centres start; run it several times and keep the lowest J.

Unlucky start

Two centres start in the same group: they split it, and two real groups are merged. A local minimum.

Uneven clusters

The border sits halfway between the centres, so the big cluster loses points to the small one.

Moons

k-means borders are straight lines: it cuts the moons in the wrong place.

Principal component analysis

What you are looking at

Left: a cloud of points. A line sweeps through all directions θ, and every point is projected onto it (thin grey lines, orange dots). Green and red arrows: the principal components, 2 standard deviations long. Top right: the variance along θ (blue) and what is left over across it (orange). Bottom right: the share of variance each component keeps. Bottom: the projected points as a histogram.

PCA finds the directions along which the data spreads most. Keeping only the first few compresses the data with the smallest possible loss.

$$\Sigma=\frac1n\sum_i(\mathbf x_i-\bar{\mathbf x})(\mathbf x_i-\bar{\mathbf x})^\top,\qquad \Sigma\mathbf v_k=\lambda_k\mathbf v_k$$
  1. Centre the data.
  2. Compute the covariance matrix.
  3. Its eigenvectors are the principal directions, its eigenvalues the variances along them.

Two views of the same thing

At every θ, variance along + left over = total. So the direction with the largest variance is also the one with the smallest error after squashing to 1D.

Common mistake

Running PCA on features with different units without standardizing: the feature with the largest numbers wins regardless of its meaning.

Almost a line

PC1 keeps over 99% of the variance: one number per point describes it.

A round cloud

The variance is the same in every direction: the blue curve is flat and PC1 is arbitrary.
