A computer solves a differential equation by marching: from the state now, estimate the state a small step h later, and repeat. The method decides how accurate each step is, whether errors pile up, and whether the solution stays bounded at all.

Methods compared on an oscillator

What you are looking at

Left: the phase portrait, velocity against position. The exact motion goes round the unit circle (dashed). Top right: x(t) against cos t. Bottom right: the energy, constant for the exact solution. Below: the error in x over time.

The spring x″ = −x is written as two first-order equations, x′ = v and v′ = −x. Euler moves along the current slope for one step. On a circle the slope is the tangent, which always points slightly outside: the numerical orbit spirals out.

$$\text{Euler: } y_{n+1}=y_n+h\,f(y_n)\qquad\text{RK4: } y_{n+1}=y_n+\tfrac h6(k_1+2k_2+2k_3+k_4)$$
  1. Euler: one slope per step, error per period ∝ h.
  2. Semi-implicit Euler: update v, then x with the new v. Same cost, energy no longer drifts (a symplectic method).
  3. Heun: Euler guess, then average the two slopes. Error ∝ h².
  4. RK4: four slopes per step. Error ∝ h⁴.

Worked example

Euler with h = 0.1: energy × (1 + 0.01) per step. After t = 20 (200 steps) the energy is 1.01²⁰⁰ ≈ 7.3 times too large.

Common mistake

Judging a method by one step. A small error per step that always pushes the same way (outward) accumulates; long simulations of orbits or molecules use symplectic methods for that reason.

Try it

Pick RK4 and push h up to 1: the orbit stays close to the circle for a long time, even with only six steps per period.

Semi-implicit Euler

The energy wobbles by about h/2 but never drifts: the orbit is a slightly squashed closed curve.

RK4 with a big step

Twelve steps per period, and the energy is still within a fraction of a percent after three periods.

Stability

What you are looking at

Left: y′ = λy with λ < 0, exact decay (dashed), explicit Euler (red) and implicit Euler (green), one dot per step. Top right: how much each method multiplies y per step, against hλ. Bottom right: the complex hλ plane with each method's stability region; the dot is your step.

On the decay y′ = λy each Euler step multiplies y by a fixed factor. If that factor is larger than 1 in size, the numerical solution grows even though the exact one decays: the method is unstable at that step.

$$\text{explicit: } y_{n+1}=(1+h\lambda)\,y_n\qquad\text{implicit: } y_{n+1}=\frac{y_n}{1-h\lambda}$$
  1. Explicit Euler is stable when |1 + hλ| < 1, i.e. h < 2/|λ|.
  2. Between h = 1/|λ| and 2/|λ| it is stable but flips sign every step.
  3. Implicit Euler solves for y_{n+1} and is stable for every h > 0 (A-stable).

Worked example

|λ| = 10: explicit Euler needs h < 0.2 s. With h = 0.3 s the factor is 1 − 3 = −2: after 10 steps y ≈ 1000, sign alternating.

Common mistake

Confusing accuracy with stability. A stable result can still be inaccurate (implicit Euler with a huge step decays too slowly), and you cannot fix an instability by waiting.

Try it

Raise the step h past 2/|λ|: explicit Euler starts to oscillate and blow up, while the implicit method still decays smoothly.

On the edge

hλ = −2: the factor is exactly −1, the solution flips sign forever without decaying.

Past the edge

hλ = −3: explicit Euler doubles every step; implicit Euler divides by 4 and decays.

Order of accuracy

What you are looking at

Left: the error after one period of the oscillator against the step h, both logarithmic. Each method is a straight line whose slope is its order. Top right: the same errors against the work, counted in slope evaluations. Bottom right: Euler and RK4 at the marked step. Below: the local slope of each line.

A method of order p has a global error ∝ h^p. On log–log axes that is a line of slope p: Euler and semi-implicit Euler 1, Heun 2, RK4 4.

$$E(h)\approx C\,h^p\quad\Rightarrow\quad\log E=\log C+p\log h$$

Worked example

From h = 0.1 to h = 0.05: Euler's error halves, RK4's falls 16 times. To reach 10⁻⁸ Euler would need about 10⁹ steps; RK4 needs about a thousand.

Common mistake

Making h as small as possible. Below about 10⁻³ RK4's error stops falling: round-off in millions of additions sets a floor.

Try it

Halve the step h and compare the errors: Euler's halves, Heun's falls 4×, RK4's 16×. That is the order of each method.

Equal work

RK4 costs four slope evaluations per step, but even per evaluation its line sits far below the others.
