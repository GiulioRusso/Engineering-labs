Gaussian elimination and LU

What you are looking at

Left: the augmented matrix [A | b], replayed one operation at a time; the row being changed is shaded orange, the pivot green. Top right: L, where every multiplier is stored as it is used. Bottom right: the residual of a system whose first pivot is a tiny ε, solved with (green) and without (orange) partial pivoting. Below: the largest entry in [A | b] after each step.

To solve Ax = b, subtract multiples of one equation from the others until the matrix is upper triangular; then solve from the last equation up (back substitution). The multiplier for row i is the entry to remove divided by the pivot, the diagonal entry above it. Recording every multiplier gives a lower-triangular L with A = L·U: the costly part, about ⅔n³ operations, is done once, and each new right-hand side then costs only n². A zero pivot stops the method; a tiny one is worse, because huge multipliers amplify round-off. Partial pivoting fixes both: before each column, swap in the row whose entry is largest in size.

$$m_{ik} = \frac{a_{ik}}{a_{kk}}, \quad R_i \leftarrow R_i - m_{ik}R_k, \qquad PA = LU, \qquad x_i = \frac{1}{u_{ii}}\Big(y_i - \sum_{j>i} u_{ij}x_j\Big)$$
  1. Pick the pivot (with pivoting: the largest |entry| in the column, swapped up).
  2. Clear the column below it with row operations; store each multiplier in L.
  3. Repeat on the smaller matrix that is left; then back-substitute.

Worked example

2x₁ + x₂ − x₃ = 8, −3x₁ − x₂ + 2x₃ = −11, −2x₁ + x₂ + 2x₃ = −3. With pivoting, the −3 is swapped to the top; three eliminations and three back substitutions give x = (2, 3, −1). With a first pivot ε = 10⁻¹⁵ and no pivoting the multipliers are 10¹⁵ and x₁ comes out 0.777 instead of 0.75.

Common mistakes

Computing A⁻¹ to solve Ax = b. It costs three times as much and is less accurate; factorise once and substitute.

Try it

Choose the tiny pivot, untick pivoting, and lower ε: the orange residual climbs as 10⁻¹⁶/ε. Tick pivoting and it drops back to round-off.

Zero pivot: swap rows

The first column starts with 0. Partial pivoting swaps the row with the largest entry to the top, and elimination proceeds; the swaps are recorded in P.

Zero pivot, no pivoting: stuck

Without a swap the first multiplier would be a division by zero: elimination cannot even start, although the system has a unique solution.

Tiny pivot without pivoting

ε = 10⁻¹⁵: the multipliers are about 10¹⁵, the second row's 1 is swamped and lost, and the answer is wrong in the second digit. The matrix is perfectly well conditioned: the method, not the problem, is at fault.

Jacobi and Gauss–Seidel

What you are looking at

Left: the two equations dx + cy = 1 and cx + dy = 2 as lines; Jacobi's iterates (orange) and Gauss–Seidel's (green, one coordinate at a time) walk from the start toward the crossing. Top right: the log of the error per iteration. Bottom right: the spectral radius of each method against c/d. Below: the x estimates.

Large sparse systems are solved by improving a guess again and again. Jacobi solves equation i for xᵢ using the old values of all the others. Gauss–Seidel does the same but uses each new value as soon as it has it. Each step multiplies the error by the iteration matrix; in the long run the error shrinks by its spectral radius ρ per step. Convergence needs ρ < 1, which is guaranteed when the matrix is diagonally dominant. For a 2 × 2 system ρ(GS) = ρ(Jacobi)², so Gauss–Seidel needs half as many steps.

$$x_i^{(k+1)} = \frac{1}{a_{ii}}\Big(b_i - \sum_{j\ne i} a_{ij}x_j^{(\cdot)}\Big), \qquad \|e_{k}\| \approx \rho^k\|e_0\|, \qquad \rho_J = \left|\tfrac{c}{d}\right|,\ \rho_{GS} = \tfrac{c^2}{d^2}$$
  1. Split A into its diagonal and the rest; the diagonal is what each step inverts.
  2. Each sweep multiplies the error by the iteration matrix.
  3. Digits gained per step = −log₁₀ ρ.

Worked example

d = 3, c = 1: ρ = 1/3 for Jacobi and 1/9 for Gauss–Seidel, about 14 and 8 steps to reach 10⁻⁶. With d = 1.2: ρ = 0.83 and 0.69, 75 and 39 steps.

Common mistakes

Expecting an iterative method to work on any matrix. Without diagonal dominance (or symmetry and positive definiteness, for Gauss–Seidel) it may diverge, as it does here once |c| > |d|.

Try it

Lower d toward c and watch both paths take more and more steps; push c past d and they spiral away.

Strong diagonal: fast

d = 4, c = 1: ρ = 0.25 and 0.0625. Gauss–Seidel gains more than a digit per step.

Weak diagonal: slow

d = 1.2: the lines are nearly parallel and ρ = 0.83: the iterates creep along the narrow wedge between the lines.

|c| > |d|: they run away

ρ = 1.3: every step multiplies the error by 1.3 (Jacobi) or 1.69 (Gauss–Seidel). The system has a solution; the method cannot reach it.

Conditioning

What you are looking at

Left: two lines meeting at an angle, each drawn with its band of uncertainty (dashed, the error in b), and 400 solutions with b perturbed inside that band (green). Top right: the condition number κ against the angle. Bottom right: A⁻¹ turning the unit circle of possible errors into an ellipse. Below: how much each trial amplified the relative error, against the worst case κ.

Some problems are sensitive by nature, whatever the method. The solution of Ax = b is the crossing of the lines; if the lines are nearly parallel, a small shift of either moves the crossing a long way. The condition number κ = σmax/σmin, the ratio of A's largest and smallest stretch, is the worst-case amplification of a relative error. It belongs to the problem: pivoting or a better algorithm cannot reduce it. As a rule of thumb, κ ≈ 10ᵏ costs about k significant digits.

$$\frac{\|\delta x\|}{\|x\|} \le \kappa(A)\,\frac{\|\delta b\|}{\|b\|}, \qquad \kappa(A) = \|A\|\,\|A^{-1}\| = \frac{\sigma_\text{max}}{\sigma_\text{min}}$$
  1. A maps the unit circle to an ellipse with axes σmax and σmin; A⁻¹ maps it to one with axes 1/σmin and 1/σmax.
  2. An error in b along A's weakest direction is blown up by 1/σmin.
  3. κ compares the worst and the best direction.

Worked example

Two unit-length rows at 90° give κ = 1. At 3° they give κ = cot(1.5°) = 38: a 1 % error in b can become a 38 % error in x.

Common mistakes

Judging a solution by its residual. For an ill-conditioned matrix |Ax − b| can be tiny while x is far from the truth.

Try it

Close the angle from 90° to 1° and watch the green cloud stretch along the lines and the ellipse on the right grow long and thin.

At 90°: κ = 1

Perpendicular lines: an error in b moves x by the same amount, in every direction. The cloud is a square, the ellipse a circle.

At 3°: κ ≈ 38

The same small errors now spread the solutions along a long thin streak: the crossing is badly pinned down along the lines.
