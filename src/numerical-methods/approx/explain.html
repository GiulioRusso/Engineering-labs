Numerical methods replace a function by something simpler that a computer can handle: a polynomial through some samples, a sum of areas, a difference of two values. Each replacement has an error, and knowing how that error behaves is the whole craft.

Polynomial interpolation

What you are looking at

Left: the function 1/(1 + 25x²) (blue), the nodes where it is sampled, and the polynomial through them (orange). Top right: the error along x. Bottom right: the largest error against the number of nodes, for equispaced (red) and Chebyshev (green) nodes. Below: the node polynomial that controls the error.

Through n points goes exactly one polynomial of degree n − 1. More points should mean a better fit — but with equally spaced points the polynomial starts to oscillate wildly near the ends. This is Runge's phenomenon.

$$f(x)-p(x)=\frac{f^{(n)}(\xi)}{n!}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x-x_i),\qquad x_i^{\text{Cheb}}=-\cos\frac{(2i-1)\pi}{2n}$$
  1. The error has two factors: a derivative of f, and the node product.
  2. With equispaced nodes the product is huge near the ends.
  3. Chebyshev nodes cluster at the ends and make the product as small as possible: the error then falls as n grows.

Common mistake

Fitting a high-degree polynomial through equally spaced measurements. Use Chebyshev nodes if you can choose them, otherwise splines (low-degree pieces).

Try it

Equispaced, raise n from 5 to 25 and watch the error at x ≈ ±0.9; then switch to Chebyshev.

Equispaced, n = 15

The polynomial passes through every node yet overshoots to about 7 between the last ones.

Chebyshev, n = 15

Same degree, nodes moved towards the ends: the error is under 0.1 everywhere.

Numerical integration

What you are looking at

Left: the integrand (blue), the panel boundaries, and the curve the rule actually integrates (orange): chords or parabolas. Top right: the error against the number of panels, log–log. Bottom right: the error made in each panel.

Split [a, b] into n panels of width h. The trapezoid rule replaces f by a straight line on each; Simpson's rule by a parabola through each pair of panels. Simpson is exact for cubics and its error falls much faster.

$$T_n=h\Big[\tfrac{f_0}2+f_1+\dots+\tfrac{f_n}2\Big],\qquad S_n=\tfrac h3\big[f_0+4f_1+2f_2+\dots+4f_{n-1}+f_n\big]$$
$$E_T\approx-\frac{(b-a)h^2}{12}f'',\qquad E_S\approx-\frac{(b-a)h^4}{180}f^{(4)}$$

Worked example

∫₀¹ eˣ dx = 1.718282. Trapezoid with 4 panels: 1.727222 (error 9·10⁻³). Simpson with 4 panels: 1.718319 (error 4·10⁻⁵).

Common mistake

Using Simpson with an odd number of panels — it needs pairs.

Try it

Double the panels from 4 to 8 to 16: the trapezoid error falls 4× each time, Simpson's 16×. Then pick the narrow bump and see how many panels it needs.

Simpson

The parabolas are almost on top of the curve: the error line has slope −4.

A narrow bump

With 8 panels the rule barely sees the peak; the panel errors concentrate around it. Doubling n helps, but adaptive rules would refine only the middle.

Finite differences

What you are looking at

Left: the error of the derivative of sin at x = 1 against the step h, log–log, with the two error sources dashed: truncation (blue) and round-off (yellow). Top right: a zoom on sin with the chord being used. Bottom right: the number of correct digits. Below: the difference quotient itself.

The derivative is the limit of a difference quotient as h → 0. On a computer h cannot go to zero: f(x + h) and f(x) become nearly equal and their difference keeps only a few significant digits. Two errors pull in opposite directions.

$$\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}=f'+\frac h2f''+\dots,\qquad\text{round-off}\approx\frac{\varepsilon\,|f|}{h},\quad\varepsilon\approx2.2\cdot10^{-16}$$
  1. Large h: truncation dominates, the error falls as h (forward) or h² (central).
  2. Small h: round-off dominates, the error grows as 1/h.
  3. The minimum: h ≈ √ε ≈ 10⁻⁸ (forward), h ≈ ∛ε ≈ 10⁻⁵ (central).

Common mistake

Choosing h = 10⁻¹⁵ "to be accurate": the answer has no correct digits at all.

Try it

Lower h from 10⁻¹ toward 10⁻¹⁵: the error first falls, then rises again once round-off dominates. The central scheme reaches its best at a larger h, and is more accurate there.

h = 10⁻¹³

Only three digits of the difference survive the subtraction: the derivative is wrong in the fourth digit.

Central difference

Symmetric around x, the h term cancels: error ∝ h², best near 10⁻⁵ with about 11 correct digits.
