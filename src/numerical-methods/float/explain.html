Round-off and cancellation

What you are looking at

Left: one quantity computed two ways over a wide range of inputs (log scale), with the formula as written (orange) and an algebraically equal rewrite (green). Top right: the relative error of the naive form, against machine epsilon (dashed). Bottom right: how many correct digits each form keeps at the chosen input. Below: the naive form's correct digits across the whole range.

A computer stores a real number with about 16 significant decimal digits (float64): every value is rounded to the nearest representable number, with a relative error up to machine epsilon ε = 2⁻⁵² ≈ 2.2·10⁻¹⁶. That is plenty, until you subtract two nearly equal numbers. Their leading digits cancel, and what is left is made of the digits that were already rounded: the relative error is multiplied by the size of the numbers over the size of their difference. This is catastrophic cancellation. The cure is not more precision but algebra: rewrite the formula so the subtraction never happens.

$$\text{fl}(x) = x(1+\delta),\ |\delta| \le \varepsilon, \qquad \frac{|\Delta(a-b)|}{|a-b|} \approx \varepsilon\,\frac{|a|}{|a-b|}, \qquad 1-\cos x = 2\sin^2\tfrac{x}{2}$$
  1. Spot a subtraction of two quantities that become equal in the limit you care about.
  2. Estimate the damage: digits lost ≈ log₁₀(|a| / |a − b|).
  3. Rewrite: a trigonometric identity, multiplying by the conjugate, or using the product of the roots.

Worked example

At x = 10⁻⁴, 1 − cos x = 5·10⁻⁹ is computed from two numbers near 1, so about 8 of the 16 digits cancel: the naive (1 − cos x)/x² gives 0.49999999696, the stable 2 sin²(x/2)/x² gives 0.49999999958, an error of 5·10⁻⁹. At x = 10⁻⁸, cos x rounds to exactly 1 and the naive answer is 0 instead of ½.

Common mistakes

Blaming the computer and switching to higher precision. Quadruple precision only delays the problem to a smaller x; the rewrite removes it for every x.

Try it

Slide the input toward the trouble end and watch the correct digits of the naive form fall about one per decade, while the stable form keeps all of them.

x = 10⁻⁸: the naive answer is 0

cos(10⁻⁸) = 1 − 5·10⁻¹⁷, closer to 1 than the spacing of float64 numbers there: it rounds to 1, and 1 − 1 = 0. Every digit is lost.

√(x² + 1) − x at x = 10⁸

x² + 1 rounds to x², so the difference is exactly 0. Multiplying and dividing by the conjugate gives 1/(√(x² + 1) + x) = 5·10⁻⁹, correct to every digit.

The quadratic formula fails

For x² + 10⁶x + 1 = 0 the small root is −b + √(b² − 4) over 2, two nearly equal numbers: the naive result is off in the sixth digit (relative error 7.6·10⁻⁶). Compute the large root first, then the small one as c/(a·x₁) = −2/(b + √(b² − 4)).

The order of a sum

What you are looking at

Left: the error of Σ 1/k² in float32 (7 digits) against the number of terms, added forward (largest first, orange), backward (smallest first, blue), and with Kahan's compensated summation (green). Top right: the forward running sum against the exact one; the red dashed line marks where forward stops changing. Bottom right: the correct digits of each method. Below: the partial sums.

Adding a small number to a large one rounds the result to the large one's spacing: the small number's low digits are lost. Add many small terms to a large running sum and they can be lost entirely: the sum stalls. The cure is again in how you compute, not in the precision. Add the small terms first, so they build up before they meet the big ones, or use Kahan summation, which carries the lost low part of each addition into the next one.

$$S \oplus t = S \quad\text{when}\quad |t| < \tfrac12\,\text{ulp}(S) \approx \tfrac12\,\varepsilon_{32}|S|, \qquad \varepsilon_{32} = 2^{-23} \approx 1.2\cdot 10^{-7}$$
  1. Forward: S grows to about 1.64; terms 1/k² fall below half its spacing near k ≈ 3200 and stop counting.
  2. Backward: tiny terms add to each other first, at their own scale, and arrive as one sizeable number.
  3. Kahan: after each addition compute what was rounded away, c = (t − S) − y, and subtract it from the next term.

Worked example

10⁶ terms in float32: forward gives 1.6447253, backward 1.6449330, Kahan 1.6449331; the exact partial sum is 1.6449331. Forward has kept only 3–4 correct digits, the other two about 7.

Common mistakes

Assuming addition is associative. In floating point (a + b) + c and a + (b + c) can differ, which is why parallel sums, done in a different order, may not match a serial one bit for bit.

Try it

Slide the number of terms past about 3000: the orange running sum flattens while the exact one keeps rising, and the forward error stops improving.

1000 terms: all agree

Before the stall point every term still counts, and the three methods agree to about 7 digits.

10⁶ terms: forward has stalled

Forward stopped changing near k ≈ 3200: the last million terms changed nothing. Backward and Kahan used them all.
