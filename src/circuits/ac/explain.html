Phasors: a sine wave is a turning arrow

What you are looking at

Left: an arrow turning steadily in the complex plane. Right: its horizontal shadow (the real part) plotted against time — a cosine. Add a second arrow and the sum is just the two arrows tip to tail.

Every sine at a given frequency is the shadow of an arrow of length A, starting at angle φ, turning at ω. In a circuit every voltage and current has the same ω, so all arrows turn together and their relative angles never change. We can freeze the picture: the frozen arrow (a complex number) is the phasor.

$$A\cos(\omega t+\varphi)=\mathrm{Re}\{\underbrace{Ae^{j\varphi}}_{\text{phasor}}\,e^{j\omega t}\}$$
  1. Write each sine as an arrow (A, φ).
  2. Add arrows as vectors (tip to tail).
  3. Read the result: length = amplitude, angle = phase.

Worked example

cos ωt + cos(ωt + 90°): arrows of length 1 at 0° and 90° → √2 at 45°. Amplitudes do not simply add (1 + 1 ≠ √2).

Common mistake

Adding amplitudes of sines with different phases. They only add arithmetically when in phase.

Two sines

The purple arrow (the sum) is the diagonal of the parallelogram; its shadow is the purple wave.

180° apart

Equal arrows pointing opposite ways: the sum is zero at every instant.

Phasors in 3D: helices in time

What you are looking at

A 3D view: time runs to the right; the voltage arrow (blue) and current arrow (orange) of a series RLC turn at the left end and trace helices. Their shadows on the floor are the cosines you see on a scope; on the back wall, the sines. Drag to rotate.

The helix is the complete picture of a sinusoid: a rotation in the complex plane carried along in time. Looking from the side (the floor shadow) you see the real waveform; looking down the time axis you see the phasor. The angle between the two helices is the phase between voltage and current, set by the circuit's impedance.

Try it

Move f/f₀ through 1: below resonance the current helix runs ahead (capacitive), at resonance the two line up, above it the current falls behind (inductive). Its radius is largest at resonance.

At resonance

Same angle: the circuit looks like a pure resistor.

Below resonance

The capacitor dominates: the current leads and is smaller.

Impedance: resistance with a direction

What you are looking at

Left: a series R, L, C driven by a sine; the charges swing with the current. Top right: the impedance as arrows — R to the right, X_L up, X_C down — and their sum Z (yellow). Bottom right: |Z| against frequency.

With phasors, d/dt becomes multiplication by jω, so each part becomes a complex number. In series they simply add, as arrows:

$$Z_R=R,\quad Z_L=j\omega L,\quad Z_C=\frac{1}{j\omega C}=-\frac{j}{\omega C},\qquad Z=R+j\left(\omega L-\frac1{\omega C}\right),\qquad I=\frac VZ$$

Worked example

R = 10 Ω, L = 10 mH, C = 1 µF at 1 kHz: X_L = 62.8 Ω, X_C = 159 Ω → Z = 10 − j96.3 Ω, |Z| = 96.8 Ω, angle −84°: capacitive.

Common mistake

Adding |Z| values: 10 + 62.8 + 159 is not the impedance. X_L and X_C point in opposite directions.

Low frequency

The down arrow (X_C) is long: the capacitor blocks.

High frequency

The up arrow (X_L) wins: the inductor blocks.

Series resonance

What you are looking at

Top right: the current against frequency (a peak). Bottom right: the voltages across R, L and C as arrows, taking the current as reference. Watch how large V_L and V_C get near resonance.

At f₀ the reactances are equal and opposite: they cancel, Z = R, and the current is largest. How sharp the peak is depends on Q:

$$f_0=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}},\qquad Q=\frac1R\sqrt{\frac LC},\qquad \text{bandwidth}=\frac{f_0}{Q},\qquad |V_L|=|V_C|=Q\,V\ \text{at}\ f_0$$

Worked example

10 mH, 1 µF, 10 Ω: f₀ = 1.59 kHz, Q = 10, bandwidth 159 Hz. With 1 V in, 10 V appears across L and across C (in opposite directions).

Common mistake

Assuming no part sees more than the supply: at resonance the capacitor must be rated for Q times the source voltage.

R = 2 Ω

Q = 50: a narrow, tall peak — radio tuning.

R = 60 Ω

Q below 2: a broad hump, not very selective.

The parallel tank

What you are looking at

Left: R, L and C side by side across the source. Top right: the impedance — now a peak. Bottom right: the branch currents as arrows.

In parallel the admittances (1/Z) add. At f₀ the inductor's and capacitor's currents are equal and opposite: they cancel at the source and circulate between L and C. The source only supplies the resistor's current, so the impedance peaks at R.

$$Y=\frac1R+j\left(\omega C-\frac1{\omega L}\right),\qquad Z(f_0)=R$$

Worked example

1 kΩ, 10 mH, 1 µF at f₀ = 1.59 kHz: I_L = I_C = 10 mA circulating, but only 1 mA from the source.

Off resonance

I_L and I_C no longer cancel: the source must supply the difference, |Z| falls.

AC power

What you are looking at

Top right: voltage, current and their product p = v·i in time; the dashed line is its average. Bottom right: real power P, reactive power Q and apparent power S.

When current and voltage are out of step, p(t) dips below zero for part of each cycle: energy stored in a reactance flows back to the source. Only the average does useful work.

$$P=\tfrac12VI\cos\varphi\ [\text{W}],\qquad Q=\tfrac12VI\sin\varphi\ [\text{var}],\qquad S=\tfrac12VI\ [\text{VA}],\qquad \text{pf}=\cos\varphi$$

Worked example

10 V, 2 A peak, φ = 40°: S = 10 VA, P = 7.66 W, Q = 6.43 var. Power companies charge factories with low power factor because the wires carry S.

Common mistake

Multiplying peak values without the ½ (or RMS values with it).

φ = 90°

p(t) is positive half the time and negative half the time: zero average. A pure inductor or capacitor dissipates nothing.

φ = 0

p(t) never goes negative: everything delivered is used.

RC filters

What you are looking at

Left: a resistor and capacitor as a voltage divider. Top right: gain in dB against log frequency (the Bode plot). Bottom right: phase.

A filter is just a divider with Z_C instead of a resistor. Since Z_C falls with frequency, the output across C (low-pass) loses the highs; across R (high-pass) it loses the lows.

$$H_\text{LP}=\frac{1}{1+jf/f_c},\qquad H_\text{HP}=\frac{jf/f_c}{1+jf/f_c},\qquad f_c=\frac1{2\pi RC}$$

Worked example

1 kΩ, 100 nF: f_c = 1.59 kHz. At f_c: −3 dB, −45°. Ten times higher (16 kHz): −20 dB, the output is a tenth.

Common mistake

Loading the output: a load resistor in parallel with C changes f_c — buffer it (an op-amp follower).

High-pass

Swap R and C: the curve mirrors around f_c.

Ten times f_c

−20 dB and almost −90°.
