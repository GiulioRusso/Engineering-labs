Source and resistor: Ohm's law

What you are looking at

Left: a voltage source and a resistor in a loop. The yellow dots are charges drifting round the loop — the faster they move, the larger the current. Top right: current against voltage, a straight line. Bottom right: the power turned into heat.

The source pushes (voltage, in volts); the resistor opposes (resistance, in ohms); the result is a flow of charge (current, in amperes). For a resistor the three are tied by the simplest rule there is, with no memory and no delay:

$$I=\frac{V}{R},\qquad P=VI=\frac{V^2}{R}=I^2R$$
  1. Read V and R.
  2. I = V/R. Check the units: volts over ohms gives amperes.
  3. Power P = V·I: all of it becomes heat in the resistor.

Worked example

5 V across 100 Ω: I = 50 mA, P = 0.25 W. A quarter-watt resistor would be at its limit. At 10 V: 100 mA and 1 W — four times the heat for twice the voltage.

Common mistakes

Thinking current is "used up" in the resistor: the same current comes out as goes in (the dots never pile up); what is used up is energy. Reversing V reverses the current, not the heat.

Try it

Make V negative and watch the dots run the other way.

Double V

Current doubles (straight line), power quadruples (parabola).

10 Ω

5 V / 10 Ω = 0.5 A and 2.5 W: this is why shorting a battery makes it hot.

The capacitor: current only while the voltage changes

What you are looking at

Left: a source switching between V and 0 (a square wave) charging a capacitor through a resistor. Top right: the source and the capacitor voltage in units of the time constant τ. Bottom right: the current — big at each switch, then fading. Watch the dots: they rush at each switch and slow down as the capacitor fills.

A capacitor is two plates with an insulator between. No charge crosses it; charge piles up on one plate and leaves the other. The current is the rate at which that charge changes, so it is proportional to how fast the voltage changes:

$$i=C\frac{dv}{dt},\qquad v_C(t)=V\left(1-e^{-t/\tau}\right),\qquad \tau=RC$$
  1. At the switch the capacitor is empty: all of V is across R, so the current starts at V/R.
  2. As v_C grows, less is left across R: the current shrinks, and so does the rate of charging.
  3. After one τ: 63%. After 5τ: over 99% — practically full, current ≈ 0.

Worked example

1 kΩ and 10 µF: τ = 10 ms. With 5 V the initial current is 5 mA; after 10 ms v_C = 3.16 V and i = 1.84 mA.

Common mistake

"A capacitor blocks current." It blocks steady current; changing voltages drive plenty through it. And its voltage cannot jump: that would need infinite current.

47 µF

τ almost five times longer: same curve shape, stretched in time.

220 Ω

Faster charging, and a larger initial current V/R.

The inductor: the current cannot jump

What you are looking at

The same square-wave source, now with an inductor (a coil). Top right: the voltage across the coil. Bottom right: the current, which rises and falls smoothly. The dots start slowly and pick up speed.

An inductor stores energy in a magnetic field. Changing that field induces a voltage that opposes the change, so the current can only change gradually. It is the capacitor's mirror image, with voltage and current swapped:

$$v=L\frac{di}{dt},\qquad i(t)=\frac{V}{R}\left(1-e^{-t/\tau}\right),\qquad \tau=\frac LR$$

Worked example

100 mH and 100 Ω: τ = 1 ms, final current 50 mA. At the switch-on the whole 5 V appears across the coil; it fades as the current builds.

Common mistake

Opening a switch in series with an inductor: the current wants to continue, di/dt becomes enormous and so does the voltage — the spark you see in relays. Here the source goes to 0 V instead, so the current decays smoothly.

470 mH

τ = L/R grows: the current takes longer to reach V/R.

The three parts under a sine

What you are looking at

Left: one part driven by a sine source; the dots swing back and forth. Top right: voltage and current (each scaled to its own peak) in time. Bottom right: the same two as rotating arrows (phasors) — the angle between them is the phase.

Feed each part the same sine and the differences between their rules become differences in timing. For the capacitor, current = C·(slope of v): a sine's slope is largest at the zero crossing, so the current peaks a quarter cycle early. The inductor is the opposite.

$$Z_R=R,\qquad |Z_C|=\frac1{\omega C}\ (i\ \text{leads}\ 90°),\qquad |Z_L|=\omega L\ (i\ \text{lags}\ 90°)$$

Worked example

10 µF at 50 Hz: 1/(2π·50·10⁻⁵) = 318 Ω. At 500 Hz: 32 Ω. 100 mH at 50 Hz: 31 Ω; at 500 Hz: 314 Ω.

Common mistake

Adding magnitudes of R and X directly. Because they are 90° apart they add like the sides of a right triangle — see the AC lab.

Try it

Move the frequency and watch the bottom plot: the capacitor's opposition falls, the inductor's rises.

LC: energy sloshing between two stores

What you are looking at

Left: a charged capacitor connected to an inductor — no source. The dots swing round and back. Top right: capacitor voltage and current. Bottom right: electric energy, magnetic energy and their sum.

The capacitor pushes current into the coil and empties. The coil's current keeps going (it cannot stop suddenly) and charges the capacitor the other way. Then everything repeats in reverse. Energy moves between the electric field and the magnetic field at the natural frequency:

$$f_0=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}},\qquad \tfrac12Cv^2+\tfrac12Li^2=\text{const}$$

Worked example

100 mH, 10 µF: f₀ = 159 Hz. Starting at 5 V: 125 µJ, all electric; a quarter period later it is all magnetic, with i = 5√(C/L) = 50 mA.

Common mistake

Expecting it to run forever: any resistance turns some energy into heat each cycle — an oscillator needs an amplifier to top it up.

2 Ω of loss

The total-energy bar shrinks a little every cycle; the swings decay exponentially.
