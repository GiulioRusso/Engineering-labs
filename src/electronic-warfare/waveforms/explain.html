Three quantities decide everything

Every waveform is a compromise between three things the radar wants, and it cannot choose them all freely:

$$\text{energy} \propto P\,\tau \qquad \Delta R = \frac{c}{2B} \qquad \Delta v = \frac{\lambda}{2T}$$

Detection wants energy, so a long pulse τ. Range resolution wants bandwidth B. Velocity resolution wants a long observation T. A waveform is a decision about which of them you are willing to spend.

How to read the four panels

Top left: the pulse itself in time. Top right: its spectrum (how much bandwidth it occupies). Bottom left: the echo as received, buried in noise. Bottom right: the same echo after the matched filter (pulse compression), in dB, centred on the target. The dashed curve is the noise-free compressed pulse.

What the matched filter does

The receiver slides a copy of the transmitted pulse along the received signal and multiplies-and-adds at every position (a correlation). Where the copy lines up with the echo, everything adds in phase and a peak appears; elsewhere the noise adds randomly. The peak is as tall as the whole pulse's energy and as narrow as \(1/B\).

The plain pulse

The simplest thing a transmitter can do: switch on at one frequency for τ seconds. Its spectrum is forced by the rectangle shape alone:

$$s(t) = \text{rect}(t/\tau) \;\longrightarrow\; S(f) = \tau\,\text{sinc}(f\tau), \qquad B \approx \frac{1}{\tau}$$

So duration and bandwidth are locked together. Finer range means a shorter pulse, which means less energy, which means less detection range. There is no third option — that deadlock is what the chirp and the phase code break.

Its matched-filter output is a triangle \(2\tau\) wide with no sidelobes at all: the one thing the plain pulse does better than anything else.

Worked example

τ = 10 µs: B ≈ 100 kHz, so \(\Delta R = c/2B = 1.5\) km. Two aircraft 500 m apart are one blob. To split them you would need τ < 3.3 µs — and a third of the energy.

Common mistake

Believing "longer pulse = better radar". Longer gives more energy (detection) but worse range resolution. "Better" depends on the question.

Try it

Drag τ and watch the two right-hand panels move together: long pulse → narrow spectrum and fat triangle; short pulse → wide spectrum and sharp triangle.

Short pulse

1 µs: the spectrum widens to about 1 MHz and the triangle shrinks to 150 m. Fine range, but ten times less energy than 10 µs.

Long pulse

20 µs: 50 kHz of bandwidth, a 3 km resolution cell. Plenty of energy for a distant small target, useless for separating close ones.

Compression gain

At −5 dB the echo is invisible in the raw signal (bottom left), yet the matched filter adds up the whole pulse coherently while the noise adds randomly: the peak stands out. For a plain pulse the gain is \(B\tau \approx 1\) per sample bandwidth — the real benefit comes from integrating over the pulse.

The LFM chirp: bandwidth without a short pulse

Sweep the frequency linearly across the pulse (top left: I and Q rotate faster and faster). Now duration and bandwidth are independent knobs:

$$s(t) = e^{\,j\pi\frac{B}{\tau}t^2}, \qquad f_\text{inst}(t) = \frac{B}{\tau}\,t, \qquad -\tfrac{\tau}{2} \le t \le \tfrac{\tau}{2}$$

The matched filter squeezes the whole long pulse into a spike about \(0.886/B\) wide: range resolution from \(B\), energy from \(\tau\). The squeeze factor — the compression ratio — is the time–bandwidth product \(B\tau\), and it is also the SNR gain.

Worked example

τ = 20 µs, B = 10 MHz: \(B\tau = 200\) → 23 dB of compression gain. Resolution \(c/2B = 15\) m, against 3 km for a plain 20 µs pulse.

The bill

Unweighted, the sidelobes are at −13.3 dB — the same number as a uniformly lit antenna, because it is the same Fourier transform of a rectangle. Weighting the filter (Hamming) brings them to about −42 dB, at the price of a main lobe about 1.5 times wider and a dB or so of SNR.

Common mistake

Thinking the chirp's long pulse ruins resolution. Resolution is set by the bandwidth, not the duration — after compression.

Try it

Double B and watch the spike halve. Switch Hamming on and compare the sidelobes in the bottom-right panel.

More bandwidth

20 MHz → 7.5 m resolution with the same 20 µs pulse; compression ratio 400 (26 dB).

Weighting

Hamming tapers the filter's reference copy: the −13 dB sidelobes drop to about −42 dB and the main lobe widens. This is the same trade as tapering an antenna array.

Buried in noise

At −10 dB nothing is visible before compression. The filter's gain \(B\tau\) (23 dB) brings the peak well above the noise: that is why long-range radars chirp.

Phase codes: compression with a switch

Cut the pulse into N equal chips and flip the phase by 180° according to a code. No frequency sweep is needed — the transmitter only switches a sign.

$$s(t) = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} c_n\,\text{rect}\!\left(\frac{t - n t_c}{t_c}\right), \qquad c_n = \pm1, \qquad B \approx \frac{1}{t_c}$$

The bandwidth is set by the chip, so a 13-chip code compresses 13:1. Barker codes are special: every autocorrelation sidelobe has height exactly \(1/N\). Barker-13, the longest one that exists, gives \(1/13 = -22.3\) dB.

Worked example

τ = 13 µs, 13 chips of 1 µs: B ≈ 1 MHz → 150 m resolution, like a 1 µs plain pulse but with 13 times its energy.

Common mistake

Trying to weight the sidelobes away as with a chirp. They come from the code itself, not from a rectangular envelope: no biphase code beats −22.3 dB at any length.

Try it

Count the sidelobes in the compressed output: they are all the same height. Then see Drone beside a tanker for when that floor matters.

Shorter chips

0.25 µs chips: four times the bandwidth, four times finer range. The sidelobe floor is still −22.3 dB.

Noisy echo

The code compresses by 13 (11 dB): the peak emerges from −5 dB of noise.

Where the plain pulse wins: two aircraft by speed

What you are looking at

Two aircraft 30 m apart — far less than a 1 µs pulse can resolve (150 m), so the range profile (bottom left) is one blob. Right: the Doppler spectrum from a burst of pulses.

They are separated by motion. Across a coherent burst of N pulses the echo phase advances by \(2\pi f_d\,\text{PRI}\) each pulse; the burst is a spectrum analyser of length \(T = N/\text{PRF}\):

$$f_d = \frac{2v}{\lambda}, \qquad \Delta v = \frac{\lambda}{2T}$$

Worked example

λ = 3 cm, T = 5 ms: \(\Delta v = 0.03/0.01 = 3\) m/s. Aircraft 5 m/s apart: two lines. 1 m/s apart: one line — you need T = 15 ms or more.

Common mistake

Expecting more bandwidth or power to separate them. Only a longer look does. And a long look in one direction is time not spent searching elsewhere.

One line

1 m/s apart with 3 m/s resolution: the two Doppler lines fuse.

Look longer

25 ms → 0.6 m/s resolution: the lines split. Nothing about the targets changed, only how long you looked.

Where the chirp wins: fighter and tanker

What you are looking at

Two range profiles from the same 20 µs pulse with the same energy. Bottom left: unmodulated — a 3 km cell, one target. Right: chirped over B — two peaks at the true positions (yellow).

At 80 km you need a long pulse to detect anything, and a short one to split 30 m. The chirp is the only waveform here that gives both.

The catch: range–Doppler coupling

Inside a chirp, time and frequency are the same variable. A Doppler shift therefore looks exactly like a small delay, and a moving target is reported at the wrong range:

$$\Delta R_\text{coupling} = -\frac{c\,f_d\,\tau}{2B}$$

Worked example

300 m/s closing at X-band: \(f_d = 20\) kHz. With τ = 20 µs, B = 10 MHz: \(\Delta R = -3\times10^8 \cdot 2\times10^4 \cdot 2\times10^{-5}/(2\cdot10^7) = -6\) m. It is a bias, not noise: it does not average away.

Try it

Reduce the bandwidth until the two peaks merge (around \(c/2B\) ≈ separation). Then add closing speed and watch both peaks slide off the yellow markers together.

Less bandwidth

3 MHz → 50 m resolution: 30 m is no longer enough, the peaks fuse.

Coupling error

Both peaks read 6 m closer than they are. Radars that care correct it with a separate Doppler measurement or alternate up- and down-sweeps, whose errors cancel.

Where the phase code fails: a drone beside a tanker

What you are looking at

The compressed output in dB: a big peak (tanker), its sidelobes, and the drone's true position and level (orange). The red dashed line is the waveform's sidelobe floor.

Both waveforms have the resolution. The question is whether the small echo survives the big one's sidelobes. A target weaker than the floor lands underneath them and vanishes — and more SNR does not help, because the sidelobes grow with the tanker, not with the noise.

Worked example

Barker-13 floor: −22.3 dB. Drone at −25 dB: hidden. At −18 dB: visible. Unweighted chirp: −13 dB, worse. Hamming chirp: −42 dB — the drone at −25 dB reappears.

Common mistake

Thinking the chirp is "better" than Barker. Unweighted it is worse; what it has is a knob (weighting) that the phase code does not.

Try it

Drag the drone's level across the red line and watch it disappear and return. Then switch the waveform with the same geometry.

Above the floor

−18 dB is above Barker's −22.3 dB sidelobes: a separate peak.

Unweighted chirp

−13.3 dB sidelobes: even more of the neighbourhood is masked.

Weighted chirp

Hamming drops the floor to about −42 dB: the drone is clearly visible, at the cost of a wider main peak.
