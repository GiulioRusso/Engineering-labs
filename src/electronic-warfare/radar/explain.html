The radar equation as a budget

What you are looking at

Left: a waterfall of decibels. Each bar is one factor of the radar equation, green when it adds, red when it takes away. Then the received echo S (blue), the receiver noise N (grey) and their difference, the SNR. Right: the SNR against range, with the 13 dB line a radar typically needs to declare a target.

Why decibels? The radar equation is a product of many factors, some huge (a 100 kW transmitter) and some tiny (\(1/R^4\) at 40 km is \(10^{-18}\)). In dB a product becomes a sum, and you can read the whole equation like a bank statement:

$$S = \frac{P_t\,G^2\,\lambda^2\,\sigma}{(4\pi)^3 R^4} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad S_\text{dB} = P_t + 2G + 10\log\lambda^2 + 10\log\sigma - 10\log(4\pi)^3 - 40\log R$$
  1. Out: the transmitter radiates \(P_t\), concentrated by the antenna gain \(G\) towards the target. At range \(R\) it spreads over a sphere of area \(4\pi R^2\).
  2. Reflect: the target intercepts a power proportional to its radar cross section \(\sigma\) (m²) and re-radiates it — again over a sphere, another \(4\pi R^2\).
  3. Back: the antenna collects the echo with an effective area \(A = G\lambda^2/4\pi\): that is where the second \(G\) and the \(\lambda^2\) come from.
  4. Noise: the receiver adds its own noise \(N = kT_0BF\): \(-204\) dBW per Hz at room temperature, plus \(10\log B\), plus the noise figure.

Worked example

100 kW = +50 dBW. Gain 35 dBi twice: +70. λ = 3 cm: λ² = −30.5. σ = 1 m²: 0. (4π)³: −33. R = 40 km: −184. Sum: S ≈ −128 dBW. Noise in 1 MHz with F = 3 dB: −204 + 60 + 3 = −141 dBW. SNR ≈ 13 dB: just detectable.

Common mistakes

Forgetting that gain counts twice (transmit and receive). Using 20 log for powers (it is 10 log; 20 log is for amplitudes — that is why \(R^4\) becomes 40 log R). Thinking more power is the cheap way to more range: 16× the power buys only 2× the range.

Try it

Double the range and watch only the R⁴ bar move, by 12 dB. Switch to "Link": the R⁴ and σ bars are replaced by R² — the one-way path a warning receiver listens on.

Twice as far

\(40\log 2 = 12\) dB: the SNR drops from 13 to 1 dB and the target disappears into the noise. Every other bar stays the same.

One-way

A receiver that only listens (an RWR) pays the spreading loss once: \(20\log R\). Doubling the range costs only 6 dB. This is why a warning receiver hears a radar long before the radar can see it.

Sixteen times the power

+12 dB of transmitter, which exactly cancels one doubling of range: \(R_{\max} \propto P_t^{1/4}\). The plot at the bottom shows this fourth-root law.

A bigger antenna

+3 dB of gain appears twice: +6 dB of SNR. Antenna gain is worth twice as much as transmitter power, which is why radar antennas are large.

Detection in noise

What you are looking at

Top: an A-scope — the receiver output along range, re-drawn for every pulse. Grass is noise; the spike is the target; the dashed red line is the detection threshold. Bottom: the probability of detecting the target against range, with the current range marked.

Noise never stops. Its amplitude is random (Rayleigh-distributed), so any threshold will sometimes be crossed by noise alone: a false alarm. We choose the threshold from how many false alarms we accept:

$$P_{fa} = e^{-T^2/2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T = \sqrt{-2\ln P_{fa}}$$

Then the target is detected only if signal plus noise crosses that line. With a strong signal it almost always does; near the noise it is a coin toss.

  1. Fix \(P_{fa}\) (e.g. 10⁻⁶: one false alarm per million range cells).
  2. That fixes the threshold \(T\) — here about 5.3 times the noise rms.
  3. The SNR at the target's range decides how often it clears \(T\): that is \(P_d\).

Worked example

For \(P_{fa} = 10^{-6}\) and \(P_d = 90\%\) you need about 13 dB. At 12 dB Pd is already far lower: the curve is steep, so a radar goes from "always sees it" to "never sees it" within a few kilometres.

Common mistake

Thinking detection is yes/no at a fixed range. It is a probability; the "detection range" quoted for a radar is where Pd reaches a chosen value (often 50% or 90%) for a stated RCS and Pfa.

Try it

Slide the range out until the spike sinks into the grass. Then integrate more pulses and watch it come back.

Twice as far

The echo has to go out and come back: power falls as \(1/R^4\), 12 dB per doubling. Pd collapses.

Integration

Adding 16 pulses coherently multiplies the signal by 16 in amplitude but the noise only by 4 (random phases add as \(\sqrt{n}\)): the SNR grows by 16, +12 dB — exactly what doubling the range cost.

A lower threshold

\(P_{fa} = 10^{-2}\): the threshold drops to 3 × the noise. More detections, but one range cell in a hundred lights up on noise alone — count the false alarms in the readout.

A small target

0.01 m² is 20 dB below 1 m². Since range goes as the fourth root, detection range shrinks by \(10^{20/40} \approx 3.2\) times. That is the whole point of stealth.

Moving-target indication

What you are looking at

Top: how much the canceller passes, against target speed. It is zero at 0 m/s (where the ground is) and again at every blind speed. The orange dot is our target. Bottom: the range profile before (grey: clutter everywhere, target lost in it) and after the canceller (blue: clutter gone, the target stands out — if its speed is not blind).

The ground does not move: its echo is the same from pulse to pulse. A moving target's echo shifts its phase by \(2\pi f_d T\) between pulses. So subtract consecutive pulses:

$$y_n = x_n - x_{n-1}, \qquad |H(f_d)| = \left|2\sin\frac{\pi f_d}{\text{PRF}}\right|$$
  1. Stationary clutter: \(x_n = x_{n-1}\), so \(y_n = 0\). Gone.
  2. Moving target: the phase has rotated, the difference is not zero. Kept.
  3. But if the phase rotated by exactly a full turn (\(f_d = \text{PRF}\)), the radar cannot tell it from stationary: the target is cancelled too.
$$v_\text{blind} = k\,\frac{\lambda\,\text{PRF}}{2}, \quad k = 1, 2, \dots$$

Worked example

λ = 3 cm, PRF = 1 kHz: first blind speed 15 m/s. A target at 15 m/s moves exactly half a wavelength (one full round-trip phase turn) between pulses — it looks frozen. At 7.5 m/s it is a half turn: maximum response.

Common mistake

Thinking a faster target is always easier. At 30, 45, 60 m/s it is blind again. Cascading cancellers (three-pulse, four-pulse) widens the clutter notch but does not remove blind speeds — only changing the PRF does.

Try it

Slide the target speed to a blind speed and watch it vanish from the blue profile. Then change the PRF: the notches move, the target reappears. Radars stagger their PRF for exactly this reason.

Two-pulse canceller

The simplest filter: one subtraction. Its notch at zero is narrow, so slowly-moving clutter (trees in the wind, sea) leaks through. Compare with three pulses: \((1 - z^{-1})^2\), deeper and wider notch.

A blind speed

15 m/s at 1 kHz: the target moves λ/2 between pulses, its echo comes back with the same phase every time, and the canceller removes it with the clutter.

Change the PRF

At 1.4 kHz the first blind speed moves to 21 m/s: the target at 15 m/s is in the passband again. Alternating PRFs from burst to burst fills the holes.

The PPI scope

What you are looking at

A plan-position indicator: the radar is at the centre, north is up, rings every 25 km. The bright line is the beam sweeping round; each echo is painted where it is and fades slowly (phosphor persistence). Near the centre: ground clutter. Each blip is as wide as the beam at that range.

The angle on the screen is where the antenna was pointing; the distance from the centre is the echo delay. A blip is not a point: it spreads across the beam width, which grows with range:

$$\theta_{3\,\text{dB}} \approx 0.886\,\frac{\lambda}{D}, \qquad \text{cross-range size} = R\,\theta$$

Worked example

A 2 m antenna at 3 cm: θ ≈ 0.76°. At 60 km that is 800 m across: two aircraft 1 km apart at that range appear as two blips. With a 0.5 m antenna the beam is 3°, 3.2 km across: they merge into one.

Common mistake

Assuming angular resolution is as good as range resolution. Range resolution is set by bandwidth and is the same everywhere (metres); cross-range resolution gets worse linearly with range.

Try it

Shrink the antenna and watch the pair of aircraft at the top right merge. Slow the rotation: the blips fade before they are refreshed.

A fast jet

Between two sweeps (5 s) a jet at 300 m/s moves 1.5 km: the tracker must predict where to look for it next.

Rain

Raindrops reflect too: a large patch of weak echoes that can hide targets. Doppler processing and circular polarization help remove it.

A small antenna

0.5 m: four times wider beam, four times wider blips. The two aircraft near 60 km become one blob — angular resolution is bought with aperture.

Slow scan

At 4 rpm each target is seen every 15 s: more pulses per look (longer dwell, better detection), but slow updates.

Jamming and burn-through

What you are looking at

The power arriving at the radar from the target's echo (blue) and from the jammer (red), against range on a log axis. Where blue climbs far enough above red the radar sees through the jamming: the dashed line, the burn-through range.

A jammer has a huge advantage: its signal travels one way, the echo travels two ways.

$$S \propto \frac{P_t G^2 \sigma}{R^4}, \qquad J \propto \frac{P_j G_j G}{R^2}\cdot\frac{B_r}{B_j}$$
  1. Far away, \(1/R^4\) is far smaller than \(1/R^2\): the jammer wins easily, even with a tiny power.
  2. As the target approaches, S grows 40 dB per decade, J only 20: the lines must cross.
  3. Below that crossing (plus the required S/J margin) the radar burns through.

Worked example

A 1 W jammer against a 100 kW radar looks hopeless — 50 dB weaker. But at 100 km the echo has lost 40 log R ≈ 200 dB and the jammer only 100: the jammer is ahead by a wide margin. The radar wins back only inside a few kilometres.

Common mistake

Forgetting bandwidth. Noise spread over 10 MHz into a 1 MHz receiver loses 10 dB. Barrage jamming covers many radars but dilutes itself; spot jamming is efficient but easy to dodge by changing frequency.

Try it

Raise the jammer power: the red line rises and burn-through moves in by √10 per decade. Switch to stand-off: the red line goes flat.

A stronger jammer

Ten times the jammer power (10 W) pulls the burn-through range in by \(\sqrt{10} \approx 3.2\) times, because S/J grows as \(1/R^2\).

Spot jamming

Concentrating the noise in the radar's own 1 MHz instead of 10 MHz puts ten times more of it where it matters. Radars answer by hopping frequency.

Stand-off jamming

A jammer far away, outside the radar's beam, must come in through the sidelobes (here −30 dB). Its power at the radar is constant while the target's echo grows as it approaches: low sidelobes are the radar's defence.
