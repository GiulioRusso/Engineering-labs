From an RF pulse to numbers

How to use this view

The row of boxes is the receiver. Pick a stage with the slider (the box is outlined): below you see the signal at that point in time (left) and its spectrum (right). Walk through the stages one by one, from the antenna to the ADC.

The echo arrives at hundreds of MHz or GHz, as a burst of carrier. Nothing digital can work at that frequency, and only the amplitude and phase of the echo matter (the carrier carries no information). The receiver's job is to bring the echo down to frequencies near zero without losing either — and to throw away everything else the antenna picked up.

Why I and Q?

A single real signal at baseband cannot tell \(+f\) from \(-f\): a target closing and a target opening at the same speed would look identical. Two channels in quadrature (cos and −sin) keep the sign. Together they form one complex number \(z = I + jQ = A e^{j\varphi}\) per sample.

$$I = \text{LPF}\{s\cdot 2\cos 2\pi f_{LO} t\}, \quad Q = \text{LPF}\{-s\cdot 2\sin 2\pi f_{LO} t\}, \quad s = A\cos(2\pi f_c t + \varphi) \Rightarrow z = A e^{j\varphi}$$

The superheterodyne receiver

The classic architecture: filter, amplify, shift to a fixed intermediate frequency (IF), filter sharply there, then demodulate into I and Q.

  1. RF band-pass: keep only the radar band (the 650 MHz interferer disappears).
  2. LNA: amplify with as little added noise as possible — the first amplifier sets the noise of the whole chain.
  3. Mixer: multiplying by the LO creates \(f_{RF} - f_{LO}\) and \(f_{RF} + f_{LO}\). We keep the difference: 400 − 330 = 70 MHz.
  4. IF band-pass: easy to make narrow and steep at a fixed, low frequency. It sets the receiver bandwidth.
  5. IQ demodulator: mix the IF down to 0 Hz in quadrature: I and Q.
  6. ADC: sample and quantise I and Q.

Worked example: the image

The mixer cannot tell above from below: a signal at \(f_{LO} - f_{IF} = 260\) MHz also produces a 70 MHz difference. That is the image frequency, and once it lands on the IF no later filter can separate it. Only the RF filter, before the mixer, can remove it — which is why its bandwidth matters.

Common mistake

Thinking the mixer "moves" the signal. It multiplies, so it creates two copies (sum and difference) and folds everything symmetric about the LO onto the same IF.

Try it

At the mixer stage, change the LO and watch the IF line move. Then widen the RF filter and look at the IF stage: the image signal now sits on top of the pulse.

Sum and difference

At the mixer output there are two copies of the pulse: at 70 MHz and at 730 MHz, plus mixed versions of everything else that got through.

The image problem

With a 200 MHz wide RF filter the 260 MHz image passes, mixes to 70 MHz and adds to our pulse at the IF: a ghost signal that cannot be filtered any more.

The IQ output

Flat I and Q during the pulse: a constant complex number whose angle is the echo phase. That phase, compared pulse to pulse, is what gives the Doppler.

Quantisation

With 4 bits the I and Q steps are visible and the spectrum's floor rises: each bit is worth about 6 dB of dynamic range.

The digital IF receiver

Do the first mix in analogue, then sample the real IF with one fast ADC and do the IQ demodulation in software. The IQ channels are then perfectly matched (no analogue imbalance).

  1. ADC at IF: sample the 70 MHz IF at 100 MS/s. Undersampling is fine for a narrow band: it aliases to 30 MHz (bandpass sampling).
  2. Digital down-conversion (DDC): multiply by \(e^{-j2\pi f n/f_s}\): the spectrum shifts to 0 Hz and becomes one-sided.
  3. Digital low-pass and decimation: keep only the baseband, then drop samples you no longer need.

Worked example

70 MHz sampled at 100 MS/s: \(|70 - 100| = 30\) MHz. As long as the IF band (here 20 MHz wide) does not straddle a multiple of \(f_s/2\), sampling it below its frequency loses nothing.

Common mistake

Thinking you must sample above twice the highest frequency. For a band-limited signal you must sample above twice its bandwidth and place it within one Nyquist zone.

Bandpass sampling

The IF appears at 30 MHz in the sampled spectrum. Change the sampling rate and watch it move — and fold over itself near \(f_s/2\).

Digital down-conversion

After multiplying by the complex exponential the spectrum is no longer mirror-symmetric: the pulse sits at 0 Hz, its mirror at −60 MHz (removed next).

Decimation

Low-pass, then keep one sample in four: 25 MS/s complex is plenty for an 8 MHz baseband.

The homodyne (direct-conversion) receiver

Mix straight from RF to 0 Hz with an LO at the carrier. No IF, no IF filters, no image: small and cheap, which is why phones use it.

The price: everything that happens at 0 Hz lands on the signal. LO leakage mixing with itself gives a DC offset; an LO slightly off frequency makes the baseband rotate; any gain or phase mismatch between the I and Q mixers creates a mirror image.

Try it

Add a little LO error at the mixer stage: I and Q oscillate at the error frequency during the pulse. Then add leakage: the whole trace shifts up by a constant.

Straight to baseband

The pulse appears as a flat I and Q directly after the mixer, plus the \(2f_{RF}\) terms that the low-pass removes.

LO error

200 kHz off: during the 1 µs pulse I and Q turn by 72°. In a radar this looks exactly like a Doppler shift.

DC offset

A constant added to I: in the spectrum, a spike at 0 Hz exactly where the zero-Doppler clutter and slow targets live.

The IQ demodulator, close up

The four panels

RF pulse at the antenna · its spectrum with the LO marked · I and Q after the low-pass · the envelope \(|I + jQ|^2\).

Multiply \(A\cos(2\pi f_c t + \varphi)\) by \(2\cos 2\pi f_{LO} t\): you get a difference term at \(f_c - f_{LO}\) and a sum term at \(f_c + f_{LO}\). The low-pass keeps the difference. With the LO exactly on frequency the difference is at 0 Hz: I and Q are constant, \(|z| = A\) and \(\angle z = \varphi\).

Worked example

Echo phase 40°: during the pulse \(I = \cos 40° = 0.77\), \(Q = \sin 40° = 0.64\). Detune the LO by 2 MHz and they rotate at exactly 2 MHz.

The filter trade-off

The low-pass must remove the sum term and the noise, but every filter takes time to respond: rise time ≈ \(0.48/B\) here. A 16 MHz filter rises in 30 ns; a 2 MHz filter in 240 ns. When the rise time approaches the pulse length there is no flat top left to measure, and the amplitude reading collapses.

Common mistake

Narrowing the filter "to reduce noise" without checking the pulse: noise goes down as \(\sqrt B\), but so does the pulse's own peak once the filter is too slow.

LO detuned

I and Q are sines at 2 MHz: the complex value spins. |z| is still right; the phase is not constant any more.

Narrow filter

4 MHz: 120 ns edges on a 500 ns pulse. The flat top is shorter but still there.

Too narrow

1.5 MHz: the filter is slower than the pulse. The output never reaches the true amplitude.

Another phase

150°: I negative, Q positive. The magnitude is unchanged; only the angle tells them apart.
