Measuring range with a stopwatch

What you are looking at

Top: the radar (left) and an aircraft (right), seen from above. The orange bar is the pulse flying out, the blue bar the echo flying back. Bottom: what the receiver records against time — the grey block is its own transmission, the blue block is the echo. Press play and watch both pictures move together.

A radar cannot see a target. It can only time how long its own pulse takes to come back. Light travels 300 m every microsecond, and the pulse makes the trip twice — out and back — so the range is half the distance the pulse covered.

$$R = \frac{c\,\Delta t}{2}, \qquad c \approx 3\times10^8\ \text{m/s} = 0.3\ \text{km/µs}$$

Step by step

  1. The transmitter switches on for τ (the pulse width). In space the pulse is a "slab" of radio wave \(c\tau\) long: 1 µs → 300 m.
  2. It travels to the target, a part of it bounces back, and travels home: total path \(2R\).
  3. The receiver sees the echo start at \(\Delta t = 2R/c\) and last τ.
  4. Then the next pulse leaves, one PRI later. The gap between pulses is the listening window.

Worked example (the default scene)

Target at 20 km: \(\Delta t = 2 \times 20\,000 / 3\times10^8 = 133\ \text{µs}\). Reading it backwards: an echo at 133 µs means \(R = 3\times10^8 \times 133\times10^{-6} / 2 = 20\ \text{km}\). Memory aid: every microsecond of delay is 150 m of range.

Common mistakes

Forgetting the factor 2 (the pulse goes out and back) — you would put the target twice as far. And mixing up the pulse width τ (how long it is on) with the PRI (how often it fires): they are different knobs with different jobs.

Try it

Double the range and check the echo moves to exactly twice the delay. Then make the pulse much longer and bring the target close: when the echo comes back before the pulse has finished, the receiver is still switched off — the radar is blind close in.

Twice as far

40 km → 267 µs: delay is proportional to range. The echo is also weaker: power falls as \(1/R^4\), a factor 16 here (the drawn amplitude is only indicative).

A longer pulse

10 µs on means a 3 km slab of wave and a 10 µs echo. More energy reaches the target (good for detection), but the echo is a long block: its start is less sharp, and two targets close together will overlap (see Range resolution).

The blind zone

While transmitting, the receiver must be protected from its own kilowatts, so it is off. An echo returning in less than τ is lost: the minimum range is \(c\tau/2\) — 1.5 km for a 10 µs pulse. That is why long-range radars, which use long pulses, cannot see close in.

Duty cycle

Duty cycle = τ/PRI = 20 %: the transmitter is on a fifth of the time. Average power is peak power × duty cycle. A high duty cycle gives more energy per second but shortens the listening window, and therefore the maximum range \(c\,\text{PRI}/2\).

Telling two targets apart

What you are looking at

Two aircraft one behind the other. Each sends back its own echo (blue and yellow lanes). On the receiver timeline the two echoes are blocks τ long, separated by \(2\Delta R/c\).

If the second echo starts before the first has ended, the blocks merge and the receiver sees one long blob: one target. The two echoes are separate only if their time gap is longer than the pulse:

$$\frac{2\Delta R}{c} > \tau \iff \Delta R > \frac{c\tau}{2}$$

Worked example

τ = 2 µs → \(c\tau/2 = 300\) m. Targets 500 m apart: echoes 3.3 µs apart, longer than 2 µs → two targets. At 200 m apart: 1.3 µs < 2 µs → one blob.

Common mistake

Thinking a stronger radar resolves better. Resolution depends only on the pulse width (more precisely on the bandwidth, see Radar waveforms), not on power.

Try it

Set the separation exactly at \(c\tau/2\) and watch the blocks just touch. Then shorten the pulse: the same two aircraft split.

One blob

200 m apart with a 2 µs pulse (300 m resolution): the echoes overlap for 0.67 µs and form a single 3.3 µs return. The operator sees one, slightly long, target.

The limit

Exactly 300 m: the second echo starts the instant the first ends. Any closer and they merge.

A shorter pulse

0.5 µs → 75 m resolution: 200 m is now plenty. The price: four times less energy per pulse. Chirps get around this (next topic group).

Pulse trains and the range–time picture

What you are looking at

The bottom picture is a range–time intensity (RTI) map: each row is the receive window of one pulse, bright where the echo landed. Rows stack upwards as pulses go out. Top: the aircraft at the current pulse.

A radar fires a pulse every PRI. If the target moves, each echo lands a little further (or closer) than the last. Stacked, the echoes draw a track whose slope is the velocity:

$$R_n = R_0 + v_r\, n\,\text{PRI}, \qquad v_r = \frac{\Delta R \text{ per pulse}}{\text{PRI}}$$

Worked example

v = 300 m/s, PRI = 5 ms: the target moves 1.5 m per pulse, 150 m over 100 pulses — 10 range bins of 15 m. Slope 1.5 m / 5 ms = 300 m/s.

Common mistake

Expecting to see real aircraft move between two pulses: at real PRIs (≈1 ms) a jet moves 30 cm per pulse, far less than a range bin. Velocity is normally measured from the echo phase (Doppler), not from range change — this map is the slow, intuitive version.

Try it

Set v = 0: the track is vertical. Reverse the sign: it tilts the other way. Lengthen the pulse: the track gets fatter and the slope harder to read.

At rest

Same range every pulse: a straight vertical line. Ground clutter looks exactly like this, which is why moving-target processing removes vertical tracks.

Closing

Negative radial velocity: the range shrinks each pulse and the track leans left.

A fat track

1 µs → 150 m bins: the track becomes a wide smear, and the range change over the whole burst is barely one bin.

Pulses in flight: range ambiguity

What you are looking at

Left: plan view with the radar in the centre; each green ring is a pulse still travelling outwards (rings every 20 km). Right, top: the transmit ticks (grey) and the echoes of T1 (orange) and T2 (yellow). Right, bottom: the ranges the receiver reports.

The receiver assumes every echo belongs to the latest pulse. That is true only if the echo returns before the next pulse leaves, i.e. if the target is closer than

$$R_u = \frac{c\,\text{PRI}}{2} = \frac{c}{2\,\text{PRF}}, \qquad R_\text{reported} = R \bmod R_u$$

Step by step

  1. Count how many PRIs fit into the true delay \(2R/c\): that many pulses left while the echo was in the air.
  2. The receiver measures the time since the latest of them: what is left over.
  3. So the reported range is the true range minus a whole number of \(R_u\).

Worked example

PRF 8 kHz → \(R_u = 3\times10^8 / 16\,000 = 18.7\) km. T1 at 58 km: \(58 / 18.7 = 3.1\): three whole \(R_u\) (56.2 km) disappear and T1 is reported at 1.8 km. Nothing about the target changed, only the PRF.

Common mistake

Thinking a folded target looks "wrong". It looks perfectly normal — a clean echo at a plausible range. That is what makes ambiguity dangerous. Radars resolve it by switching between several PRFs and checking which true range is consistent with all of them.

Try it

Slide the PRF up slowly and watch more rings appear in flight and T1's reported range jump each time it crosses a multiple of \(R_u\).

Low PRF

1.5 kHz → \(R_u = 100\) km: at most one wavefront is in flight within the display, and both targets are reported honestly.

High PRF

8 kHz: three or four pulses are in the air at once, and T1 is reported at 1.8 km — in the clutter right next to the radar.

Both folded

T2 at 30 km also exceeds \(R_u\) (18.7 km) and appears at 11.3 km. Two targets, two wrong ranges, no warning.
