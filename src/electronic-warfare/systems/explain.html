How to read the diagram

Every symbol is a physical box you could hold: a triangle is an amplifier, ⊗ a mixer, a circle with a wave an oscillator, a box with waves a filter, the pointed box a converter (ADC/DAC), the circle with an arrow a ferrite circulator. The small tiles on the wires show the signal at that point (carriers drawn slowed down so the shape is readable). The signal is followed block by block: the lit block is the one described above. Untick "Follow the signal" to pick a block yourself. Amplitudes in the tiles are normalised; the plot below gives the real levels.

The pulsed radar

The simplest radar, still the template for all others. A synchronizer fires once per pulse repetition interval. The modulator dumps a high-voltage pulse into a magnetron, a vacuum-tube oscillator that rings at microwave frequency for as long as the voltage lasts: ~1 MW for ~1 µs.

The same antenna transmits and receives, so a duplexer (circulator + TR tube) switches it: transmitter → antenna, antenna → receiver. The echo, around 10⁻¹² W, goes through a limiter, an LNA, a mixer to IF, the IF amplifier/filter and an envelope detector; the display sweep starts with the trigger, so the blip's position is the range.

  1. Transmit chain: power. Every dB of transmitter is expensive.
  2. Receive chain: sensitivity. The first amplifier sets the noise.
  3. The synchronizer ties them together: range = delay × c/2.
$$P_r = \frac{P_t G^2 \lambda^2 \sigma}{(4\pi)^3 R^4}, \qquad P_n = kT_0 B F$$

Worked example: the levels

1 MW = +90 dBm leaves the transmitter. A 1 m² target at 60 km returns about −95 dBm. With 25 dB of duplexer isolation, +65 dBm leaks into the receiver during transmission: 160 dB more than the echo. That is why the limiter exists: without it the LNA would burn on the first pulse.

Common mistake

Thinking the magnetron radar can measure Doppler. Its start phase is random every pulse and the envelope detector throws the phase away: it measures range and bearing only. Doppler needs the coherent chain of the next topic.

Try it

Move the target from 20 to 150 km and watch the echo tile sink into the noise (−12 dB per doubling). Lower the duplexer isolation and look at the leak in the plot.

The leak

The tile after the duplexer is clipped at the transmit time: the leak is far off the scale of the echo. After the limiter it is cut down to the limiter level, and the echo, much later in the window, passes untouched.

A far target

At 140 km the echo is a few dB above the noise: the tile looks almost like noise. The plot shows signal and noise moving together through the receiver: amplifiers raise both, only the IF filter improves the ratio (by rejecting noise outside the pulse band).

The detector

After the diode only the envelope is left: a positive bump. Two echoes with different phases give the same bump — the phase, and with it the Doppler, is gone.

The coherent pulse-Doppler radar

To measure velocity the radar must compare the phase of the echo from pulse to pulse. That only works if every oscillator is locked to one reference: the COHO (IF reference) and the STALO (RF local oscillator) are synthesised from the same crystal, and the transmitter is an amplifier (TWT, klystron or solid state) that keeps the phase it is given.

The waveform is generated digitally (DDS + DAC) — usually a chirp — up-converted with the STALO and amplified. On receive the same STALO brings the echo back to IF, the COHO demodulates it into I and Q, and two ADCs hand numbers to the signal processor: pulse compression (range), a Doppler FFT across the pulses of a dwell (velocity), and a CFAR threshold (detection). The data processor turns detections into tracks.

$$z_k = A\,e^{j(\varphi_0 + 2\pi f_d k T)}, \qquad f_d = \frac{2v}{\lambda}$$

Worked example

At 10 GHz (λ = 3 cm) a target at 120 m/s gives f_d = 8 kHz. With a PRF of 20 kHz the phase advances by 0.4 of a turn per pulse; 16 pulses through an FFT put it in bin 16 × 0.4 ≈ 6.

Common mistake

Calling the STALO and COHO "two more oscillators". Their whole point is that they are not independent: if they drifted relative to each other the pulse-to-pulse phase would wander and Doppler processing would smear.

Try it

Change the radial velocity and watch the I and Q of the target cell (plot) rotate faster, and the peak in the Doppler tile move. Set the chirp sweep to 0 and look at the pulse compression tile: no sweep, no compression.

I and Q

Blue and orange tiles: the echo as a complex number. During the chirp they oscillate (the chirp is still there, at baseband); compression and the Doppler FFT are linear operations on these two channels.

Pulse compression

The long chirp collapses to one narrow peak at the target's delay: the energy of a long pulse with the resolution of a short one. Increase the sweep: the peak becomes narrower.

Doppler across pulses

The plot shows the target cell over 16 pulses: I and Q trace a rotating phasor. The FFT turns that rotation into one peak; the sign of the velocity decides which side of zero it lands on.

The active electronically scanned array

An AESA has no single transmitter: every radiating element has its own transmit/receive module with a phase shifter, an attenuator, a power amplifier, a circulator, a limiter and an LNA. A beamformer splits the drive among the modules and sums their outputs; a beam-steering computer loads each phase shifter.

For an echo from angle θ, element m receives the wave late by a phase m·π·sin θ (spacing λ/2). If the phase shifters cancel exactly that progression, all module outputs are in phase and add up M times; from any other angle they partly cancel. The beam moves in microseconds, by changing numbers.

$$\varphi_m = -m\pi\sin\theta_s, \qquad AF(\theta) = \frac{1}{M}\left|\sum_{m} e^{jm\pi(\sin\theta - \sin\theta_s)}\right|$$

Common mistake

Thinking the phase shifters "bend" the waves. Each element still radiates in all directions; the beam exists only because the contributions add in one direction and cancel in the others.

Try it

With the receive steps lit, move the target angle away from the beam: the tiles after the modules fall out of step and the sum shrinks. Then steer the beam to the target.

In phase

Left tiles (at the elements): staggered sines, each later than the one above. Right tiles (after the phase shifters): all aligned. The sum tile is full scale.

Off the beam

The target at −15° while the beam points at 20°: after the modules the sines are spread over a full turn and the sum almost vanishes — the target sits in a sidelobe.

The ESM receiver

Electronic support measures listen instead of transmitting. A wide-band antenna hears every radar around, so their pulses arrive interleaved. For each pulse the receiver measures the carrier (an IFM: instantaneous frequency measurement), the angle of arrival (amplitude comparison of four squinted antennas), the time of arrival, the width and the amplitude, and writes them in a pulse descriptor word (PDW).

The deinterleaver sorts the PDWs into pulse trains (same frequency and angle, regular time differences = the PRI); the trains are compared with a threat library and shown on the threat display, or handed to the jammer.

Why ESM sees first

The radar's echo falls as 1/R⁴, the radar's pulse at the ESM only as 1/R²: a receiver with modest sensitivity hears the radar well beyond the range at which the radar sees it.

Try it

Untick "two radars": the plot shows a single regular train. With two, the dots split into two frequency rows, which the deinterleaver separates.

Pulse descriptor words

From here on the receiver no longer keeps waveforms: one number set per pulse. The plot shows them as dots (time of arrival, frequency).

Deinterleaving

The two coloured trains in the tile are the same pulses as before, separated: one every 23 % of the window (the search radar), one every 9 % (the fire-control radar).

The DRFM jammer

A digital radio-frequency memory jammer receives the radar's pulse, down-converts and samples it, keeps it in memory, and plays it back delayed and with an added Doppler phase ramp, through a DAC, an up-converter (same LO) and a power amplifier. Because the copy is the radar's own waveform, it passes the radar's matched filter like a real echo.

  • Delay → a false range. Increasing the delay slowly walks the radar's range gate away: range-gate pull-off.
  • Phase ramp → a false velocity (velocity-gate pull-off).
  • J/S > 0 dB: the copy is stronger than the skin echo, so the tracker prefers it.

Try it

Increase the delay and watch the second pulse move away from the skin echo in the bottom tile and in the plot. Lower J/S below 0 dB: the copy becomes weaker than the real echo.

Range-gate pull-off

The copy arrives well after the skin echo: the radar's range gate, having locked on the stronger copy, now reports a target that is not there; the real one is outside the gate.

Quantisation

With one bit the stored pulse is a square wave: harmonics and spurs, a copy a modern radar can tell from an echo. Three or four bits already look like the original.
